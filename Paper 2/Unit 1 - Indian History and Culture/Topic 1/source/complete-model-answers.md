## Q1. Evaluate the importance and limitations of archaeological evidence in reconstructing Harappan society.

The Harappan civilization is known primarily through archaeology because its script has not been securely deciphered. Material evidence therefore supplies the foundation for reconstructing urban life, production and exchange, while also placing limits on what historians can claim.

**Contributions.** Excavated street layouts, drains, wells and reservoirs show substantial investment in urban infrastructure. Sites such as Mohenjo-daro and Dholavira demonstrate both shared planning principles and regional adaptations. Seals, standardized weights, bead workshops and traded materials suggest craft specialization and interconnected exchange networks. Botanical remains and animal bones reveal aspects of farming, diet and environment. Burials and skeletal evidence may illuminate health, social practices and population history. Scientific dating and stratigraphy help establish sequences of change.

**Limitations.** An undeciphered script prevents confident identification of rulers, administrative offices and written beliefs. Monumental structures cannot automatically be assigned a specific ritual function. Differences in house size or grave goods may indicate inequality but do not translate directly into modern social categories. Preservation is uneven, and individual sites cannot represent the entire civilization.

**Contemporary relevance.** The 2025–26 Rakhigarhi burial investigations illustrate how new scientific evidence can improve understanding, although conclusions must await research results.

**Conclusion.** Archaeology reveals a complex, regionally diverse civilization, but reliable history requires comparing evidence types and distinguishing observed facts from plausible interpretations.

## Q2. Compare the emergence and teachings of Jainism and Buddhism in the sixth-century BCE setting.

Jainism and Buddhism gained influence amid changing urban, political and intellectual conditions in the first millennium BCE. Both participated in wider debates about suffering, moral conduct, rebirth and liberation, but their doctrines should not be treated as interchangeable.

**Common setting.** Expanding towns, commerce and new political formations helped create environments in which renunciant teachers and monastic communities attracted lay support. Both traditions stressed disciplined ethical life and offered paths to liberation that did not depend exclusively on elaborate sacrificial ritual.

**Jain teaching.** Jainism affirms an enduring living soul (*jiva*) that becomes bound by karma. Liberation requires rigorous ethical restraint and purification. Its five vows include non-violence, truthfulness, non-stealing, chastity and non-possession. *Anekantavada* emphasizes the many-sided character of reality.

**Buddhist teaching.** Buddhism diagnoses *dukkha* through the Four Noble Truths and prescribes the Noble Eightfold Path. The Middle Way rejects both indulgence and extreme mortification. Dependent origination and *anatta* reject the idea of an independent, permanent self.

**Impact and distinction.** Both developed monastic and lay institutions and inspired literary, artistic and pilgrimage traditions. Yet Jainism's doctrine of *jiva* contrasts fundamentally with Buddhist *anatta*, while their approaches to ascetic discipline also differ.

**Conclusion.** The two movements shared a historical setting and concern with liberation, but their philosophical differences are essential to understanding India's intellectual diversity.

# Complete Model Answers — original mains practice (not PYQs)

## Q1. Was British territorial expansion primarily a result of military superiority? (15 marks)

The East India Company’s rise from a commercial corporation to a territorial ruler cannot be explained by military superiority alone. It was a **fiscal-military and political transformation** in which local alliances, revenue access, diplomacy and battlefield outcomes reinforced one another.

The Carnatic Wars demonstrated the value of European military organization, but they were fought amid disputes involving Indian claimants and regional courts. At Plassey in 1757, Siraj ud-Daulah’s defeat owed much to political conspiracy and defections, showing that a battle could settle an already fractured political contest. Buxar in 1764 was a stronger test of arms because the Company defeated a coalition involving Mir Qasim, Shuja ud-Daulah and Shah Alam II. The diwani grant of 1765 then supplied Bengal’s revenue, enabling a larger army and further territorial expansion.

Mysore, the Marathas and the Sikh kingdom reveal the limits of a technological-determinist explanation. Haidar Ali and Tipu Sultan fought four wars with substantial military adaptation; the Maratha confederacy combined formidable forces with decentralized politics; Ranjit Singh developed an effective Punjab army. British success relied on changing coalitions, the Treaty of Bassein, subsidiary alliances, pressure on court politics and ultimately annexation. The Doctrine of Lapse and the annexation of Awadh illustrate that legal and administrative instruments complemented war.

Thus, military capacity was necessary but insufficient. The Company’s decisive advantage emerged when **coercive power, Indian collaborators and access to Indian fiscal resources** formed a self-reinforcing system. An adequate historical explanation must recover Indian agency rather than depicting conquest as inevitable.

## Q2. Assess the economic consequences of colonial land revenue settlements and commercialization. (15 marks)

Colonial revenue settlements reshaped agrarian property claims and obligations, while commercialization tied cultivation more closely to cash markets. Their consequences depended on local ecology, bargaining power and the burden of revenue collection.

The Permanent Settlement of 1793 fixed the government demand on zamindars in Bengal but did not guarantee security for tenants. Revenue arrears and auctions could destabilize estates, and landlords often had incentives to extract rents. Ryotwari arrangements assessed cultivators directly in parts of Madras and Bombay, yet periodic revision and high demands could still intensify indebtedness. Mahalwari settlements placed responsibility on village estates or proprietary bodies and likewise exposed cultivators to collective fiscal pressure. None was an all-India model.

Cash crops such as indigo, cotton, opium and jute linked farmers to expanding trade networks. Some producers benefited from demand and transport access, but many faced price volatility, costly credit, coercive contracts and vulnerability when food prices rose. Indigo resistance and the Deccan disturbances demonstrate that market participation could coexist with unequal power. Land transfers and debt contributed to landlessness in some regions, though substantial cultivators and entrepreneurial farmers also emerged.

Revenue extraction and commercialization must therefore be assessed together: cash obligations pushed cultivators toward markets, while volatile markets could undermine their capacity to pay. The outcome was **uneven integration without reliable welfare gains**. For Telangana, the institutions of princely Hyderabad must be analyzed separately from British provincial settlements.

## Q3. Were colonial railways instruments of exploitation or modernization? (20 marks)

Colonial railways were both transformative infrastructure and instruments shaped by imperial priorities. Their economic and political effects cannot be reduced to either a purely exploitative or a purely modernizing narrative.

Railway construction connected inland producing regions with ports and enabled faster movement of raw materials, manufactured imports and troops. Strategic routes strengthened the colonial state. Early financial arrangements, especially guaranteed returns to investors, could shift risk onto Indian revenues while profits and procurement benefits flowed outward. Railways therefore belonged to the wider fiscal and commercial architecture of empire.

At the same time, cheaper and faster transport expanded market integration, passenger movement and access to some employment and trading opportunities. Indian entrepreneurs used the network for commerce. Railways could move grain toward famine-affected areas, but transport availability did not ensure affordability: poverty, inflation and unequal purchasing power could prevent people from accessing food. The technology also helped spread newspapers, ideas and activists, indirectly strengthening nationalist networks.

A sound assessment separates **technical capacity, ownership and finance, distribution of gains, and political control**. The railways undeniably changed India’s economic geography, yet their planning and financing were not designed primarily around equitable Indian development. Their long-run public utility and their colonial political economy are both essential to the answer.

# Detailed Notes — Freedom Struggle, Mass Movements and Partition, 1885–1947

## 1. Reading the national movement historically

### A freedom struggle with multiple political languages

Between 1885 and 1947, the demand for Indian self-rule changed from demands for greater participation in imperial institutions to an insistence on sovereign independence. This transformation was neither automatic nor universally shared. Constitutionalists, revolutionaries, peasants, industrial workers, women activists, anti-caste organizers, socialists and communal organizations framed freedom in different ways. A sound analytical account asks who participated, what they wanted, how they organized and whose interests were incompletely represented. The Congress was pivotal but never exhausted the political life of colonial India.

### The three phases: a teaching device rather than rigid boundaries

The conventional periodization is early nationalist or moderate politics, approximately 1885–1905; assertive or extremist-nationalist and Swadeshi activity, approximately 1905–1919; and mass Gandhian nationalism, approximately 1919–1947. Its strength is that it maps changing methods, from petitions and economic critique to boycott and coordinated civil resistance. Its weakness is the temptation to portray each phase as replacing the last. Constitutional negotiation, revolutionary violence, social reform and labor organizing persisted across these boundaries. The final phase itself included strategic changes, divisions and temporary suspensions.

### Why war and world politics mattered

The First World War enlarged fiscal extraction, generated price pressures and created expectations of constitutional concession. The Russian Revolution inspired new debates about capitalism, labor and social justice. The Great Depression affected commodity prices and agrarian indebtedness. The Second World War intensified economic distress, brought Indian politics into a global anti-fascist and anti-imperial setting, and undermined British capacity to sustain rule. These pressures interacted with indigenous organization; neither world events alone nor one leader alone explain independence.

## 2. Phase I: Early nationalism, 1885–1905

### Founding and social base of the Indian National Congress

The Indian National Congress first met in Bombay in December 1885, with W. C. Bonnerjee presiding. Its early membership was disproportionately English-educated and urban, including lawyers, professionals and journalists. Its sessions helped leaders from different presidencies and regions identify common grievances and coordinate political language. The social base was narrow, but newspapers, local associations and campaigns gave some demands a larger audience. It is inaccurate to dismiss this period as politically useless merely because its repertoire appears cautious to later generations.

### Moderate techniques and achievements

Early nationalists sought representative institutions, Indianization of higher services, budget transparency, administrative accountability, civil liberties and reduction of military expenditure. They used resolutions, delegations, public meetings and economic studies. The Indian Councils Act of 1892 offered limited enlargement of councils but not responsible government. Political concessions were restricted; nevertheless, the campaign normalized scrutiny of colonial finance and helped connect provincial issues to a larger Indian public sphere.

### The economic critique of empire

Dadabhai Naoroji developed the drain-of-wealth argument: taxes, remittances, pensions, profits and payments abroad could transfer Indian-generated resources without equivalent domestic reinvestment. R. C. Dutt and others linked revenue systems and agrarian distress to colonial economic policy. The critique was not a single econometric formula, and historians debate the magnitude of different transfers. Its political consequence was profound: poverty became a question of institutions and power rather than simply individual backwardness.

### Limits and internal critiques

Early Congress politics often assumed that argument and imperial liberalism could deliver reform. Elite dominance and limited participation by women, laborers and oppressed-caste communities narrowed its claims to represent the whole country. Still, petitions and councils should be evaluated as political choices shaped by surveillance, repression and resources, not as evidence that early leaders lacked commitment. Later mass movements inherited some of their organizational infrastructure and economic vocabulary.

## 3. Phase II: Swadeshi, assertive nationalism and new strategies, 1905–1919

### The partition of Bengal and Swadeshi

Lord Curzon announced the partition of Bengal in 1905, officially on administrative grounds; critics argued that it also had divisive political consequences. Protests promoted swadeshi goods, boycotts of imported products, national education and public mobilization. Women, students, traders and sections of rural society participated, although their motivations differed. Swadeshi blended economic self-reliance with political protest. The 1911 reversal of Bengal partition was symbolically important, but it cannot be attributed to protest alone without considering changing imperial calculations.

### Assertive politics and the Surat split

Bal Gangadhar Tilak, Bipin Chandra Pal and Lala Lajpat Rai emphasized assertive forms of agitation, self-reliance and swaraj. The Congress split at Surat in 1907 amid disagreements over leadership and methods; the contrast between Moderates and Extremists is useful but oversimplifies overlap in goals and mutual influence. Repression, prosecutions and factional divisions constrained the movement. The relationship between religion-inflected symbols and inclusive nationalism also requires scrutiny, because symbols that mobilize one constituency may alienate another.

### Revolutionary nationalism

Groups and networks in Bengal, Maharashtra and abroad adopted clandestine organization, arms acquisition and targeted attacks. Their activities expressed frustration with the pace of constitutional politics and state repression. They drew on sacrifice and anti-colonial internationalism, but small clandestine cells faced severe intelligence and logistical challenges. Revolutionary nationalism should not be collapsed into the Congress program, nor treated as the dominant participation mode of the period.

### Home Rule and the Lucknow Pact

The Home Rule Leagues associated with Tilak and Annie Besant in 1916 pressed for self-government and broadened public campaigning. The Congress and Muslim League reached the Lucknow Pact in 1916, indicating a moment of constitutional cooperation while accepting separate electorates and communal representation. This duality matters: political agreements could foster joint negotiation while institutionalizing group distinctions. Montagu’s 1917 declaration promised progressive realization of responsible government, but without immediate sovereignty.

### War, coercion and the threshold of mass politics

War taxation, recruitment and price increases strained colonial society. The Rowlatt legislation of 1919 enabled extraordinary coercive powers despite Indian opposition. The massacre at Jallianwala Bagh on 13 April 1919 profoundly damaged imperial legitimacy. These events helped redirect anger toward mass politics, although local variations in mobilization remained substantial.

## 4. Phase III: Gandhian mass movements, 1919–1947

### Gandhi’s methods: satyagraha as political organization

Gandhi’s campaigns at Champaran (1917), Ahmedabad (1918) and Kheda (1918) connected local grievances to a method emphasizing public discipline, nonviolent resistance and negotiation. Satyagraha was not mere passivity: it sought to make unjust authority difficult to sustain while keeping participants accountable for their conduct. Its success depended on organizing, resources, communication and the responses of authorities. Gandhi’s national leadership also faced critics who rejected his social philosophy or his tactical decisions.

### Khilafat and Non-Cooperation, 1920–22

The Non-Cooperation Movement linked anger over Punjab repression and colonial constitutional failures with the Khilafat issue. Congress adopted the campaign in 1920, encouraging boycott of titles, schools, courts and imported goods, together with spinning and national institutions. Its meaning varied: plantation workers sought mobility, peasants resisted landlords and forest communities demanded local rights. Gandhi suspended the movement after the violent Chauri Chaura incident in February 1922. The withdrawal sparked debate between nonviolent discipline and the risk of demobilizing an energized society.

### Interlude, institutions and constitutional disagreement

After 1922, political activity diversified. The Swaraj Party, formed in 1923 by C. R. Das and Motilal Nehru, pursued council entry to obstruct colonial governance from within, while others stressed constructive programs outside councils. The Simon Commission, appointed in 1927 without Indian members, met widespread protest. The Nehru Report of 1928 proposed a constitutional framework but exposed disagreements over minority safeguards. At Lahore in December 1929, Congress declared Purna Swaraj as its goal and observed Independence Day on 26 January 1930.

### Salt march and Civil Disobedience

Gandhi’s Dandi March in March–April 1930 challenged the salt monopoly through a symbol intelligible across class and region. Civil disobedience embraced salt-making, refusal of some taxes, boycotts and demonstrations. Participation varied: business groups feared prolonged disorder; rich peasants and poorer cultivators could disagree over tax and rent demands; Dalit leaders developed independent political claims. The Gandhi–Irwin Pact in 1931 and Round Table Conferences showed interaction between protest and negotiation, not a simple straight line to concession. Renewed repression and shifting organization reduced momentum later.

### Constitutional restructuring and provincial politics

The Government of India Act 1935 envisaged a federation that did not take effect and established provincial autonomy under continuing colonial constraints. Congress ministries took office in several provinces after the 1937 elections, giving nationalists governing experience but also exposing tensions over representation, reform priorities and relations with the Muslim League. Provincial office was not equivalent to independent national government.

### Quit India and wartime rupture

The British decision to involve India in the Second World War without Indian consent intensified the dispute over sovereignty. After the failure of the Cripps Mission, Congress adopted Quit India on 8 August 1942. Top leaders were rapidly arrested; local activists, students, workers and peasants sustained decentralized resistance, including some sabotage and parallel administrative experiments. Colonial repression was severe. The movement did not achieve immediate withdrawal, but the upheaval exposed the political cost of continued imperial rule.

### INA, military dissent and the final political crisis

Subhas Chandra Bose led the Indian National Army, seeking independence through an alliance with Axis powers in wartime. The INA’s battlefield campaign failed, and the political reception of its trials later energized anti-colonial sentiment. The Royal Indian Navy ratings’ revolt of 1946 demonstrated unrest within imperial military structures, though its causes and leadership differed from Congress mass movements. Neither INA trials nor naval revolt single-handedly caused Britain’s withdrawal; they belonged to a larger crisis of legitimacy, finance, war exhaustion and competing mass political claims.

## 5. Peasants, workers and tribal political agency

### All India Kisan Sabha and agrarian demands

The All India Kisan Sabha was established in 1936, with Swami Sahajanand Saraswati as its first president. Local peasant organizations demanded rent reduction, debt relief, security of tenure, limits on landlord power and rights over cultivated land. Their politics intersected with Congress and left organizations but retained distinct agrarian priorities. The Bihar peasant movement, Awadh mobilizations and later Telangana peasant struggle should be compared without assuming identical land-tenure systems or chronologies.

### The Telangana peasant struggle: careful chronology

In the Hyderabad State, agrarian inequality, vetti (forms of forced labor), landlord domination and princely authoritarianism provided conditions for communist-led peasant mobilization. The armed Telangana struggle developed primarily in the later 1940s and continued beyond Indian independence into the early 1950s. It cannot be attributed only to British provincial policy because Hyderabad State was a princely polity. For this particular syllabus topic, it illustrates how the wider anti-colonial period also involved struggles over land, caste and social power that independence alone did not resolve.

### Industrial workers and trade unions

The All India Trade Union Congress was founded in 1920. Labor movements addressed wages, working hours, safety, recognition and colonial labor law. Workers joined nationalist protests in some settings while also acting through industrial disputes independently of Congress strategy. Differences among moderate unionists, communists and other socialists sometimes divided trade union organization. A strike was not automatically anti-colonial, and nationalists did not universally agree about labor militancy.

### Tribal movements, forests and freedom

Adivasi resistance cannot be reduced to participation in Congress organizations. Forest laws, customary access, landlord and moneylender relations and missionary change shaped region-specific politics. Alluri Sitarama Raju’s Rampa rebellion of 1922–24 in the Andhra hills drew on grievances about forest restrictions and forced labor and adopted guerrilla methods. Other tribal communities interpreted swaraj through their own land and livelihood concerns. The history of the freedom struggle must record those aims rather than treating tribal activists as supporting characters in an urban nationalist story.

## 6. Gender and women in the freedom struggle

### Forms of participation and leadership

Women participated in picketing, salt protest, underground communication, demonstrations, relief networks, education, journalism and political organizations. Sarojini Naidu, Kamaladevi Chattopadhyay, Aruna Asaf Ali, Usha Mehta and Sucheta Kriplani exemplify different modes of public leadership, but attention to famous figures should not erase anonymous participants. Household and caste obligations constrained mobility, even as mass campaigns created new space for political engagement.

### Women’s organizations and substantive demands

The All India Women’s Conference, established in 1927, advocated women’s education and legal-social reform, later engaging broader questions of rights and political status. Women’s activists debated franchise, marriage, inheritance, labor conditions and representation. The category women was never socially uniform: caste, religion, class and location shaped experiences of nationalist politics. Participation in a movement for national freedom did not guarantee equal power within the movement.

### Gender and the limits of nationalist rhetoric

Nationalist imagery sometimes treated women as symbols of moral purity or the nation-as-mother while political organizations remained male-dominated. Women’s activism challenged this separation between symbolic importance and decision-making authority. In writing answers, distinguish women as icons in political culture, as organizers in campaigns and as thinkers demanding enforceable rights; these are overlapping but different historical roles.

## 7. Socialists, communists and ideological contest

### Socialism within the nationalist movement

From the 1920s onward, socialist arguments gained ground in response to global revolution, depression, unequal landownership and labor organization. Jawaharlal Nehru and Subhas Chandra Bose emphasized economic planning and industrial development, though their political paths differed. The Congress Socialist Party was established in 1934 by leaders including Jayaprakash Narayan and Acharya Narendra Deva. Socialists often collaborated with Congress while criticizing its limits on agrarian and class issues.

### Communist organization and political strategy

The Communist Party of India’s origins and founding chronology are debated in historiography; communist organizing developed through networks and meetings in the 1920s, with the 1925 Kanpur conference often used as a domestic organizational landmark. The party faced colonial surveillance and prosecutions. Communists organized workers, peasants and cultural fronts, but wartime political positions changed sharply after Germany attacked the Soviet Union in 1941. An analytical answer should explain these strategic shifts rather than presenting a continuous unchanging line.

### Revolutionary and socialist strands

Bhagat Singh and the Hindustan Socialist Republican Association increasingly framed political liberation alongside social transformation. Bhagat Singh’s writings criticized communalism and exploitation, not only colonial rule. His execution in 1931 generated broad public response. The revolutionary tradition included different political visions; treating all armed resistance as a single ideology erases debates over social equality, violence, secularism and mass politics.

## 8. Communalism and constitutional conflict

### Communalism as a political process

Communalism is not synonymous with personal religious faith. It emerges when religion is organized as a political identity presumed to have uniform, competing interests. Colonial censuses, separate electorates, competing elites, mass mobilization, socioeconomic anxieties and organizations contributed in changing proportions. Explaining communalism requires historical causation, not assigning collective blame to entire religious communities.

### Separate electorates and representational dilemmas

The Indian Councils Act 1909 institutionalized separate electorates for Muslims; constitutional negotiations later expanded forms of communal representation. Some minority leaders viewed safeguards as protection against majoritarian exclusion, while critics feared permanent political separation. The Lucknow Pact of 1916 expressed cooperation and also accepted communal electorates. Other claims, including those of depressed classes, raised distinct questions about representation and social hierarchy and should not be merged into Hindu–Muslim competition.

### The Pakistan demand and evolving negotiations

The Muslim League’s Lahore Resolution of March 1940 called for independent states in Muslim-majority northwestern and eastern zones; later politics consolidated around Pakistan. Congress, the League, provincial parties, Sikhs and other constituencies differed over the powers of the center, federal structure and protections. The Cabinet Mission Plan of 1946 sought to preserve a loose Indian union but failed amid disagreements and distrust. Communal violence, wartime political changes and breakdowns in negotiation narrowed options for a unified settlement.

## 9. Independence and Partition, 1947

### Transfer of power under compressed deadlines

The British announcement of February 1947 accelerated the timetable for transfer of power. The 3 June Plan envisaged partition, and the Indian Independence Act 1947 created the independent dominions of India and Pakistan in August. The Radcliffe boundary awards were announced after independence ceremonies, contributing to confusion. The timing and administrative limitations of partition mattered greatly for security and displacement.

### Violence, displacement and the human cost

Partition generated widespread communal violence, abductions, forced migration and loss of homes and livelihoods, particularly in Punjab and Bengal. Estimates of deaths vary substantially across studies, so exact totals require cited methodological choices. Women experienced gender-specific violence, including kidnapping, coercion and disputed recovery arrangements. Oral histories, refugee records and regional scholarship reveal experiences that formal high-politics narratives often neglect.

### Why independence and partition must be studied together but separately

Independence ended formal colonial sovereignty; partition divided territories and populations through new state boundaries. They were simultaneous, causally related events but not one triumphal story. A balanced assessment considers imperial withdrawal, Congress and League strategies, constitutional deadlock, communal violence and regional power struggles while avoiding deterministic arguments that partition was inevitable from 1909 onward. It also examines how national freedom could coexist with incomplete social and economic justice.

# Complete Model Answers — Original exam-style responses

## Q1. Examine the usefulness and limits of dividing the Indian freedom struggle into three phases. (15 marks)

The division of the Indian freedom struggle into early nationalism, assertive nationalism and Gandhian mass politics is a useful way to explain changes in strategy and social reach between 1885 and 1947. Yet it must be understood as a teaching framework rather than a sequence of sealed compartments.

The first phase, conventionally dated 1885–1905, centered on the early Indian National Congress. Leaders used public meetings, petitions, constitutional demands and analysis of colonial finances. Dadabhai Naoroji’s drain theory helped transform the discussion of poverty into a critique of unequal imperial power. Despite a narrow professional and urban social base, the early Congress created networks and an all-India vocabulary of rights.

The second phase, approximately 1905–1919, saw boycotts, swadeshi enterprise, national education and more assertive political action after the Bengal partition. Tilak and others emphasized self-reliance and agitation, while revolutionary groups adopted distinct clandestine methods. The Home Rule leagues and the Lucknow Pact in 1916 showed that public mobilization and constitutional bargaining could coexist. War, the Rowlatt legislation and Jallianwala Bagh intensified opposition to imperial rule.

The third phase was marked by far greater mass participation. Gandhi’s Non-Cooperation, Civil Disobedience and Quit India campaigns expanded the repertoire of resistance through boycott, satyagraha, deliberate lawbreaking and decentralized protest. At the same time, labor unions, peasant organizations, women activists and socialists articulated demands not exhausted by a transfer of governmental power. The 1935 Act, provincial elections, wartime negotiations and 1947 Partition further complicate any story of uninterrupted advance.

The framework's limitation is that moderation, revolution, social movements and political negotiation overlapped. Nor did participants agree on the meaning of swaraj: rights over land, caste equality, industrial labor protection and minority security could carry as much urgency as national sovereignty. Thus the three phases illuminate changing dominant strategies, but the freedom struggle is best understood as overlapping and sometimes conflicting currents united only incompletely around the goal of ending colonial rule.

## Q2. How did peasants, workers and Adivasi communities reshape the objectives of anti-colonial politics? (20 marks)

Anti-colonial nationalism did not grow solely through resolutions of educated elites. Peasants, industrial workers and Adivasi communities brought questions of land, wages, forests and everyday coercion into the political sphere, reshaping what freedom was expected to deliver.

Agrarian mobilization was not a single uniform movement. Tenant cultivators demanded rent reduction and security, indebted peasants sought debt relief, and agricultural laborers faced distinct claims over wages and social dignity. The All India Kisan Sabha, formed in 1936, organized peasant demands and interacted with Congress and socialist groups. In many regions agrarian protests drew energy from national campaigns but also challenged powerful local social groups whose interests could overlap with the Congress leadership.

Industrial labor developed trade union organizations, notably the All India Trade Union Congress founded in 1920. Workers linked colonial economic conditions with workplace concerns such as hours, pay, safety and recognition. Strikes at times reinforced nationalist campaigns but also followed their own industrial rhythms. Socialists and communists tried to connect political liberty with class-based transformations, producing both collaboration and conflict with other nationalist leaders.

Adivasi politics foregrounded customary use of forests, land rights and resistance to coercive labor or outside intermediaries. Alluri Sitarama Raju's Rampa rebellion of 1922–24 illustrates how local livelihood grievances could lead to armed resistance under conditions different from Gandhi's nonviolent programme. Their visions of swaraj were not simply copies of Congress resolutions.

The Telangana peasant struggle developed principally in the late 1940s under the Nizam's Hyderabad State. Its mobilization against vetti, landlord power and princely authoritarianism shows that political independence and agrarian transformation were connected but not identical objectives. This case requires care because Hyderabad was not a directly administered British province and the struggle continued after 1947.

These groups expanded the content and reach of nationalism while also exposing its contradictions: landlords, employers, caste elites and laboring populations did not always define freedom alike. Their contribution was therefore both to challenge colonial authority and to insist that sovereignty without social and economic justice would be incomplete.

## Q3. Analyze the political and social factors leading to Partition and its human consequences. (20 marks)

The Partition of British India in 1947 occurred alongside independence, but it resulted from a contingent breakdown of constitutional compromise amid sharpened political competition, communal violence and a rapidly shrinking imperial timetable. It should not be treated as inevitable from the beginning of Indian nationalism.

Colonial institutions helped structure political competition around group representation. Separate electorates for Muslims introduced in 1909 were defended by some leaders as safeguards and criticized by others for hardening political divisions. These arrangements interacted with differences in provincial demographic balance, elite interests and fears of domination. The 1916 Lucknow Pact illustrated that Hindu–Muslim political cooperation remained possible, even while accepting communal representation.

From the 1930s, disagreements over federation, the distribution of provincial and central powers and representation deepened. The Muslim League's 1940 Lahore Resolution and subsequent Pakistan campaign gained force in a political environment marked by war, elections and competing constitutional visions. Congress and League representatives disagreed about the foundations of a postcolonial state. The Cabinet Mission Plan of 1946 attempted to preserve a union with substantial provincial grouping, but mistrust and disputes over interpretation undermined agreement.

Meanwhile, communal mobilization and violence hardened fears among communities. British wartime exhaustion and the decision to transfer power rapidly left little time for creating stable political and security arrangements. The June 1947 plan accepted partition; the Indian Independence Act formalized the creation of two dominions. Boundary lines were finalized under severe time pressure, and announcements after the independence ceremonies compounded uncertainty in affected regions.

The human consequences were enormous. Communities were uprooted across Punjab and Bengal; widespread killings, displacement, looting and destruction fractured social and economic life. Women experienced sexual violence, abduction and coercive family or state interventions. Death estimates vary among scholars, reminding us that the human tragedy cannot be reduced to a confidently stated single statistic. Refugee rehabilitation, property disputes and memories of separation shaped both new states for decades.

Partition emerged from an interaction of institutional design, competing political projects, distrust, communal violence and hurried imperial withdrawal. Recognizing this complexity avoids communal blame and restores the suffering of ordinary people to the center of the history of independence.

## Explained MCQs

**MCQ 1. Which statement best describes the early Congress phase?**
A. It rejected all council reform.  B. It used economic critique and constitutional petitions.  C. It launched Quit India.  D. It was dominated by military rebellion.

**Answer: B.** Drain theory, institutional reform and representation were central. A falsely erases council demands; C occurred in 1942; D confuses a nineteenth-century uprising with later organized politics.

**MCQ 2. Which organization was founded in 1936?**
A. All India Kisan Sabha  B. All India Trade Union Congress  C. Congress Socialist Party  D. All India Women's Conference.

**Answer: A.** AIKS dates to 1936; AITUC to 1920; CSP to 1934; AIWC to 1927. Verify specific founding terminology when using different historical reference works.

**MCQ 3. Which best differentiates Non-Cooperation from Civil Disobedience?**
A. Both demanded military revolt.  B. Non-Cooperation emphasized withdrawing participation; Civil Disobedience deliberately defied particular laws.  C. Civil Disobedience began in 1885.  D. Non-Cooperation excluded villagers.

**Answer: B.** A misstates Gandhi's stated strategy; C misdates the movement; D is contradicted by peasant, plantation and tribal mobilizations.

**MCQ 4. Which conclusion about Quit India is historically defensible?**
A. It achieved immediate British withdrawal.  B. It included decentralized activism after national leaders were arrested.  C. It was a League-led campaign.  D. It occurred before World War I.

**Answer: B.** Arrests disrupted central coordination, but local resistance continued. A misstates outcomes; C misidentifies leadership; D is chronologically impossible.

**MCQ 5. Why is the Lahore Resolution of 1940 often treated carefully in historical answers?**
A. It abolished all separate electorates.  B. Its formulation referred to independent states, while later political interpretation consolidated around Pakistan.  C. It immediately created Pakistan.  D. It ended negotiations in 1940.

**Answer: B.** The wording and later evolution differ. A, C and D are historically untenable.

## Substantive new depth repair: Gandhi–Irwin Pact and constitutional bargaining


# Substantive depth supplement — Paper II, Unit I, Topic 5

**Focus: Gandhi–Irwin Pact, the Round Table Conferences, constitutional bargaining, mass mobilisation and Partition.** This supplement adds explanation to an existing published draft; it does **not** certify the older chapter as academically approved. The original PDF is preserved.

## 1. Gandhi–Irwin Pact, 5 March 1931: why negotiations became possible

The Gandhi–Irwin Pact was a negotiated truce between Mahatma Gandhi, representing the Indian National Congress, and Viceroy Lord Irwin. Its immediate background was the Civil Disobedience Movement that followed the 1930 Salt March. Salt law violations, boycotts, demonstrations and arrests had shown that imperial authority could be challenged by disciplined mass action, but prolonged confrontation also imposed costs on participants and the colonial government. The First Round Table Conference in London (November 1930–January 1931) had proceeded without Congress participation, limiting its representative authority. Irwin needed a way to bring the largest nationalist organisation into constitutional discussion; Gandhi needed concessions sufficient to justify temporarily suspending civil disobedience.

Negotiation was neither a surrender nor a declaration of independence. It reflected an unstable balance: the government retained coercive institutions and final constitutional authority, while Congress possessed broad mobilising capacity but could not immediately compel full sovereignty. The agreement is therefore best understood as a political exchange rather than as an ideological reconciliation. A serious answer should identify the negotiators, the setting, the exchange of commitments and the unresolved conflicts.

## 2. What the pact provided—and what it did not

Congress agreed to suspend the Civil Disobedience Movement and participate in the next Round Table Conference. The government agreed, subject to qualifications, to release prisoners detained for nonviolent civil-disobedience activities, withdraw specified ordinances and prosecutions, return confiscated property not already sold where applicable, and permit peaceful picketing of liquor and foreign-cloth shops. Coastal communities were allowed limited salt manufacture for their own use. These concessions were significant because they recognised civil disobedience as a political force worthy of negotiation.

But the agreement did **not** grant dominion status, independence, a binding constitution or an unrestricted right to manufacture and sell salt. It did not provide general amnesty for those convicted of violent offences. It also did not stop the execution of Bhagat Singh, Rajguru and Sukhdev later in March 1931, a failure that generated severe criticism of Gandhi and the negotiating strategy. Claims that the pact “secured freedom” or “saved every political prisoner” are historically wrong. The agreement concerned immediate de-escalation and entry into talks, not the final settlement of colonial rule.

## 3. Why the agreement was controversial

Supporters saw a strategic recognition of Congress and a temporary opening for political prisoners and ordinary volunteers. They argued that disciplined movements require periods of negotiation and that participation in constitutional conferences could broaden the nationalist position. Critics regarded the concessions as inadequate after a mass campaign. Revolutionary nationalists objected to the exclusion of those convicted in violent cases and to the failure to obtain commutation of death sentences. Some peasant activists expected stronger commitments on rent, land revenue and police repression. The pact's language did not guarantee relief for every local grievance that participants had associated with civil disobedience.

These criticisms reveal a central tension in mass nationalism: a national negotiating organisation must frame demands that can be put into an agreement, but participants enter movements with heterogeneous aims. When a national leadership suspends agitation, it may appear to some supporters that their local sacrifices have been traded for limited institutional concessions. An excellent mains answer therefore weighs bargaining gains against representational limits instead of offering a simple “success/failure” verdict.

## 4. Second Round Table Conference and the breakdown of the truce

Gandhi attended the Second Round Table Conference in London in 1931 as the Congress representative. The talks exposed deep disagreements over the structure of an Indian federation, safeguards for minorities and the nature of political representation. The British government emphasised constitutional complexity and communal questions, while Congress claimed to speak for a national demand for responsible self-government. Other representatives challenged Congress's claim to represent all communities. The conference failed to deliver a settlement acceptable to the principal parties.

After Gandhi's return, repression and confrontation resumed; Civil Disobedience was renewed in early 1932. The failure does not prove that participation was irrational. It shows the limits of bargaining when parties disagree over the unit of representation and when the colonial state controls the constitutional timetable. In analytical writing, distinguish the pact (March 1931), the Second Round Table Conference (September–December 1931), and the later 1932 constitutional controversies. They were linked but separate events.

## 5. The Poona Pact and the question of representation

The 1932 Communal Award proposed separate electorates for Depressed Classes. Dr B. R. Ambedkar defended distinct political safeguards against entrenched caste exclusion; Gandhi opposed separate electorates for Depressed Classes and began a fast in prison. The Poona Pact of September 1932 replaced the proposed separate electorates with reserved seats in joint electorates and additional arrangements. Its immediate compromise cannot be treated as proof that the underlying debate over independent Dalit political representation was resolved.

For TGPSC, the analytical point is that nationalism and social justice were not always expressed through identical institutional designs. Gandhi prioritised a conception of Hindu social unity, while Ambedkar insisted that representation must protect communities vulnerable to caste-majority domination. A strong answer describes the competing political principles without caricaturing either figure. The dispute also demonstrates why the Round Table period cannot be understood only as a negotiation between Congress and Britain.

## 6. From constitutional experiments to the 1942 rupture

The Government of India Act, 1935, introduced provincial autonomy but left decisive imperial controls in place and envisaged a federation that did not come into operation. Congress ministries formed in several provinces after the 1937 elections, creating experience of administration and sharpening disputes with other political parties. The Second World War changed the context dramatically: Britain involved India in war without obtaining consent from Indian elected representatives. Congress ministries resigned in 1939, and constitutional bargaining increasingly intersected with wartime security and economic hardship.

The Cripps Mission of 1942 offered a postwar constitutional framework but failed to secure agreement, partly because of unresolved issues concerning immediate responsible government, defence and future provincial options. The Quit India resolution of August 1942 demanded British withdrawal. Leaders were arrested and the movement developed varied regional forms, including strikes, underground communications and, in some places, sabotage. It is inaccurate to call the campaign uniformly nonviolent or uniformly centrally directed after arrests. The historical lesson is that repression can decentralise a movement and make its tactics less predictable.

## 7. Partition: constitutional decisions and human consequences

The 1946 Cabinet Mission Plan attempted to preserve an Indian Union through a complex federal arrangement, but disputes over grouping, sovereignty and political trust undermined implementation. The Direct Action Day violence and subsequent communal conflicts deepened insecurity. The Mountbatten Plan of June 1947 led toward Partition, and the Indian Independence Act of July 1947 created the legal framework for the two dominions in August. Dates should be tied to the correct instrument: the plan, the Act and the transfer of power are not the same event.

Partition caused displacement, killings, abductions and long-term disruption, though exact aggregate figures vary by method and source. The political history must include the experience of refugees, women, minorities and communities whose lives were divided by borders. Explaining Partition solely as the decision of one leader is inadequate; the causes included constitutional deadlock, communal mobilisation, colonial policy and escalating violence. Yet a structural explanation must not erase the responsibility of organisations and individuals for particular acts.

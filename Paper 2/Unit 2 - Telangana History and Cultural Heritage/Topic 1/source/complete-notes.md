# Ancient Telangana: historical evidence, state formation and cultural heritage

## Understanding ancient Telangana through evidence

“Telangana” is a useful present-day geographic lens, not an unchanging ancient kingdom. Ancient polities crossed the Krishna and Godavari basins and reached territory now in Andhra Pradesh, Maharashtra and Karnataka. Historical reconstruction combines excavated settlements and burials, inscriptions, coins, religious structures and literature. These categories answer different questions. Coins reveal issuing authorities, distribution and commercial networks but a findspot alone cannot prove sovereignty. Royal inscriptions assert titles, grant privileges or praise rulers and must be read as interested statements; inscriptions recording donations show participation by monks, nuns, women and merchants but cannot describe an entire society. Excavation strata clarify the relative order of occupation, and absolute dating requires independent justification. A cautious answer states where a claim is certain and where chronology or territorial extent is disputed.

## Iron Age and early historical transition

Megalithic cemeteries, iron implements, black-and-red ware and changing settlements indicate societies already developing before large inscriptional kingdoms are visible. Burial assemblages offer clues about craft, mortuary ritual and differentiated access to objects. They do not prove the religious identity of a buried individual, and a rich grave is not a full social census. The Godavari and Krishna river systems helped link agricultural zones, minerals, towns and seasonal routes. Over time, exchange facilitated specialization, production of transportable goods and patronage of places of worship. There was no single date when all communities transitioned from a “prehistoric” to a “historic” era; rural communities and urbanizing corridors changed at different tempos.

## Satavahana chronology and political geography

The Satavahanas emerged as a major Deccan polity across the late centuries BCE and early centuries CE, although genealogical sequence and precise start dates remain debated. Political power varied by ruler and period, extending across sections of both western and eastern Deccan. Their history is not reducible to one fixed capital in present-day Telangana. Royal coinage, Prakrit inscriptions and inscriptions by other contemporaries help reconstruct their authority. Gautamiputra Satakarni appears in an inscription associated with Gautami Balashri at Nasik; its honorific claims need contextual interpretation rather than literal acceptance of every territorial boast. The Satavahana-Shaka struggles also show dynamic frontiers. Different geographical parts of the dynasty's sphere had distinct networks of local elites, craft producers and religious donors.

## Satavahana administration and social relations

Terms from grants and records suggest a network of rulers, officials, local notables and religious beneficiaries rather than uniform administration extending identically into every village. Agrarian production remained fundamental, but crafts, caravan trade, ports linked indirectly to Deccan hinterlands and monetary exchange created additional economic possibilities. Roman coins and imports recovered in peninsular India demonstrate wider connections, not proof that every settlement participated directly in Mediterranean trade. Guild-like corporate groups and merchants could finance Buddhist establishments through endowments. Women recorded as donors complicate a narrative that attributes every monument to kings. References to caste, lineage and ritual status must be studied in their own source context; social categories were experienced differently across settlements and centuries.

## Satavahana language, literature and visual culture

Prakrit inscriptions written in Brahmi-derived scripts represent a major channel of elite and donative communication. They cannot be used to claim that no other languages were spoken in the region. The Gathasaptashati is traditionally associated with Hala, but its compilation history makes it unreliable as a direct transcript of one monarch’s court. Buddhism and commercial patronage created settings for sculptural, architectural and inscriptional production. The Amaravati tradition in the lower Krishna area offers an important artistic comparison, yet Amaravati is outside modern Telangana. Site-specific Telangana evidence is needed for local cultural claims: borrowing a famous monument from a neighboring state without qualification weakens examination accuracy.

## Ikshvakus and eastern Deccan networks

The Ikshvakus became prominent after Satavahana political authority fragmented, especially in the eastern Krishna basin. Vijayapuri, associated with Nagarjunakonda in present-day Andhra Pradesh, is a major center of inscriptional and architectural evidence. Its relevance to Telangana is regional rather than an argument that Nagarjunakonda is in Telangana. Records name royal women and patrons associated with Buddhist establishments; other traditions of religious giving and Brahmanical ritual coexisted. The widespread participation of women as recorded donors matters both for social history and for explaining how monastic institutions assembled resources. Political support for Buddhist monasteries did not mean that all royal officials or ordinary residents shared one religious identity.

## Phanigiri: a Telangana-based case study

Phanigiri in Suryapet district is a particularly valuable way to ground broad dynastic history in Telangana. The Telangana Department of Heritage identifies an early historic Buddhist monastic complex on a hilltop and describes its initial occupation as Satavahana-period, with important development associated with the Ikshvaku period. Excavated chaityas, viharas, a congregation hall, coins and sculpture reveal sustained institutional investment and complex worship spaces. Distinguish an excavated object from the historical explanation proposed for it: material recovery is direct evidence; its dynastic stylistic attribution is an interpretation requiring comparison. The department reports a 2024 hoard of lead coins. A coin hoard can illuminate circulation, production or deposit behavior, but one hoard cannot independently establish an entire imperial boundary.

## Religious institutions and Buddhism

Buddhism spread through movements of teachers, pilgrims, merchants, patrons and monastic residents rather than solely by royal decree. A stupa is a commemorative devotional monument; a chaitya is a worship or assembly space, sometimes a hall; a vihara is a monastic residence and institutional complex. These terms should not be treated as interchangeable architectural labels. Donative inscriptions can preserve the names and occupations of supporters. Monastic institutions were both religious and economic actors: they received gifts, required supplies and were situated in wider settlement and trade landscapes. Artistic practices also evolved, so an image or relief from one phase must not be projected backward onto all earlier periods. Understanding Phanigiri requires respecting its archaeological phasing rather than merging centuries into a single “Buddhist golden age.”

## Jainism across Telangana’s early and medieval landscape

Jainism's presence and expansion should be traced using securely dated inscriptions, icons, institutional remains and donor networks, not by attaching every later Jain shrine to ancient Satavahana origins. Its doctrines of ascetic discipline, non-violence and release have distinct philosophical and monastic histories; similarities in the use of renunciation or merchant patronage do not make Jainism and Buddhism one religion. Across the wider Deccan, religious communities competed for, and sometimes shared, spaces of public patronage and commercial contact. The historical problem is often evidentiary: a substantial proportion of visible Jain monument patronage in Telangana belongs to later periods. The answer should note continuity where documented but avoid unsupported dynastic attribution.

## Vishnukundins and early medieval restructuring

The Vishnukundins became a significant political force in parts of the eastern Deccan, especially around the fifth and sixth centuries CE, although chronology and geographic boundaries remain matters for epigraphic study. Copperplate land grants, claims of royal lineage and religious endowments are central sources. Copperplates directly support the record of a grant and its stated conditions; they cannot prove that every village followed the same property arrangements. The growth of Sanskritized royal representation and Brahmanical ritual patronage formed part of wider transformations in political authority, landed relations and religious institutions. These changes did not everywhere displace pre-existing Buddhist or Jain traditions simultaneously. The transition from early historic to early medieval forms was an uneven process, not a clean break on one ruler's accession.

## Social structure, gender and patronage

The social history of ancient Telangana becomes clearer when the unit of analysis moves beyond kings. Inscriptions recording donations from royal women, household members and professionals show the importance of patronage relationships. Yet the prominence of wealthy donors in surviving records creates selection bias: poorer agricultural workers, laborers and many village communities appear less often. The difference between literary prescriptions about social hierarchy and actual local practice needs explicit recognition. Donations to a monastery could be intended for religious merit, prestige, sustaining learned communities or marking a family's standing; the motives were not necessarily mutually exclusive. Consequently, political and religious change must be analyzed in terms of who provided resources, who received institutional benefits and whose actions the sources leave undocumented.

## Arts, architecture and linguistic change

Chronological attribution is essential in architectural study. Monastic hilltop complexes, votive stupas, assembly halls, inscriptions and sculpture illustrate the long evolution of Buddhist sites. The presence of Brahmi-derived scripts does not imply one fixed language across the region; early epigraphic Prakrit and later expanding Sanskrit usage are evidence of changing documentary traditions, not sudden replacement of local speech. Temple building and Brahmanical institutions became important under later political formations, but an architectural style should not be identified from an undated isolated fragment without archaeological context. The correct comparative frame is regional connectivity: Phanigiri in Telangana, Nagarjunakonda in Andhra Pradesh, and Amaravati in Andhra Pradesh were distinct sites connected through a wider cultural landscape.

## Exam synthesis and historiographical cautions

The best mains answer integrates chronology with evidence. Begin with geographic nuance; then explain Satavahana state and trade, Ikshvaku regional political networks and Buddhist patronage, and Vishnukundin land-grant politics and religious change. Bring the analysis back to specific Telangana material, especially Phanigiri, rather than relying entirely on adjoining Andhra sites. Explain Jain and Buddhist histories independently and situate language, literature and monuments within economic and political life. Conclude by acknowledging debate over royal chronologies and by separating a recorded find from a conjecture. This evidence-led method produces a stronger answer than lists of rulers, dates and monuments without causal explanation.

**Q1. Which location is correctly described?**

A. Phanigiri is a Buddhist site in present-day Telangana; Nagarjunakonda is in Andhra Pradesh

B. Both lie in modern Telangana

C. Both lie in modern Maharashtra

D. Neither has any Buddhist remains

**Correct answer: A.** The sites are historically connected but geographically distinct.

**Q2. Which is the safest inference from finding an ancient coin in an excavation?**

A. The king controlled every nearby district

B. The coin circulated or was deposited at the site

C. Every resident used coins

D. The site had no barter exchange

**Correct answer: B.** Findspot alone does not demonstrate sovereignty or universal usage.

**Q3. Which source is particularly important for Vishnukundin land grants?**

A. Copperplate inscription

B. Colonial railway reports

C. Modern voter roll

D. Persian court diary of Akbar

**Correct answer: A.** Copperplates and related inscriptional records document particular grants.

**Q4. A vihara refers primarily to**

A. A military fort

B. A royal tax

C. A monastic residence or complex

D. A mint

**Correct answer: C.** Differentiate religious architectural functions.

**Q5. What is a sound conclusion from Phanigiri’s long archaeological sequence?**

A. All buildings were constructed in one year

B. The site evolved across phases

C. It was exclusively a royal palace

D. Its inhabitants had one uniform profession

**Correct answer: B.** Excavation layers and diverse features imply complex occupation phases.

## Q1. Analyze the contribution of Satavahanas, Ikshvakus and Vishnukundins to the cultural history of ancient Telangana.

Ancient Telangana formed part of a wider Deccan world in which political authority and cultural networks rarely matched present-day state borders. The Satavahanas supported an early historic environment of agricultural production, craft specialization and routes of exchange. Coinage and Prakrit inscriptions illuminate royal representation and commercial connection, while Buddhist donative records reveal the participation of merchants and women as well as rulers. The Ikshvakus became important in the eastern Deccan after the fragmentation of Satavahana power. Monuments and inscriptions around Nagarjunakonda, now in Andhra Pradesh, illustrate diverse Buddhist patronage and the visibility of royal women as donors; regional connections extended into Telangana. Phanigiri, in present-day Telangana, provides a more direct local archaeological case, with Buddhist structures and artifacts attributed to multiple occupation phases. In a later setting, the Vishnukundins used land grants and genealogical claims to consolidate political authority; Brahmanical ritual and Sanskritizing inscriptional practices became more prominent in surviving evidence. Yet these changes did not extinguish all other religious communities at once. Taken together, the three dynastic spheres demonstrate a transition from early historic networks to layered early medieval institutions. Their heritage must be interpreted through local archaeology, documentary limitations and regional comparison rather than a fixed sequence of uniform kingdoms.

## Q2. Explain the significance of archaeological and epigraphic evidence for reconstructing the religious history of ancient Telangana.

Religious history in ancient Telangana cannot be reconstructed from dynastic labels alone. Archaeology reveals how people organized sacred spaces: stupas supported commemorative devotion, chaityas accommodated worship, and viharas served monastic residence and instruction. Phanigiri’s Buddhist remains in Suryapet show a complex institution developing over successive phases. Coins, sculptural pieces and structures provide clues about chronology, donations and the wider networks sustaining a monastery. Inscriptions add the names of donors, religious beneficiaries and sometimes ruling families. These records help recover the role of women and merchants, but they also favor literate and prosperous patrons and do not represent every ordinary resident. Jainism requires its own chronological source evaluation: later Jain temples cannot be assumed to document continuous Satavahana-period practices without intervening evidence. Comparative sites such as Nagarjunakonda and Amaravati, both outside present-day Telangana, illuminate wider Deccan artistic circulation while not replacing local field evidence. Furthermore, an inscribed political claim and an excavated object are different kinds of evidence. Secure historical interpretation cross-checks location, stratigraphy, dates, text and material. Such source criticism allows us to recognize deep religious diversity without exaggerating royal control or claiming that one creed characterized an entire region.

# Paper II · Unit II · Topic 1 — Substantive Source-Checked Depth Repair: Early Historic Telangana

**Original supplement, 25 October 2026.** This supplement strengthens the older shallow published draft. It does not replace or academically approve the original PDF. Sources are official district archaeology and heritage descriptions; dynastic chronology and contested capitals require independent epigraphic review.

## 1. Reconstructing early Telangana from material evidence

Ancient Telangana cannot be understood simply by memorising a list of dynasties. The region's early history is reconstructed from coins, inscriptions, habitation mounds, brick structures, burial sites, pottery and religious monuments. Each category answers different questions. Coins can indicate circulation, authority or trade, but a coin found in a village does not prove the issuer personally governed that village. An inscription may identify a donor, title or regnal year but usually reflects an elite perspective. Pottery and house remains reveal everyday production and consumption without naming kings. Historians therefore compare evidence rather than forcing every object into one dynastic story.

The Department of Heritage Telangana records excavations at Peddabankur, Phanigiri and other sites, while Karimnagar's district museum exhibits material from Dhulikatta, Peddabankur and Kotilingala. The geographical distribution matters: a network of settlements and monastic sites along routes and rivers indicates exchange and religious patronage, not merely a court-centred civilisation. A mains answer should distinguish an archaeological find, an official heritage interpretation and a hypothesis requiring scholarly verification. Modern district boundaries are administrative conveniences; they were not the borders of ancient kingdoms.

## 2. Satavahanas: political reach and the problem of a single capital

The Satavahanas were a major early historic Deccan dynasty whose rule and influence extended across areas that today belong to several states. Telangana's Kotilingala, Dhulikatta and Peddabankur are important for studying the early historic landscape. The Karimnagar district history identifies Kotilingala as an early Satavahana capital, but that official heritage formulation should not be mistaken for a settled scholarly consensus on a single capital throughout the dynasty's long history. Capitals, political centres and zones of coin circulation can change across reigns; inscriptions and numismatic sequences require careful comparison.

Satavahana history is inseparable from agriculture, routes, craft production and religious donations. Coins imply monetised exchange in some contexts, while brick-built settlements and Buddhist structures suggest concentrations of resources and labour. Dynastic patronage and merchant donations could coexist; one should not assume every monastery was built directly by a king. The dynasty is often discussed with Prakrit inscriptions, regional language histories and evolving artistic forms, but precise chronology differs across scholarly reconstructions. In an examination, present secure relationships and indicate when an exact date or attribution remains debated.

## 3. Dhulikatta: stupa architecture and what it reveals

The Peddapalli district's official account describes a Buddhist stupa at Dhulikatta with a circular drum, dome, circumambulatory path and *ayaka* platforms projecting toward the cardinal directions. Limestone decorative slabs and a Brahmi-inscribed label are part of the archaeological record. The district dates a label palaeographically to around the second century BCE. This does not automatically date every structural phase of the monument to the same year: buildings can be enlarged, repaired and decorated over generations.

The site illustrates how Buddhism was embedded in a settled economy. Construction required craftsmen, stone transport, donors and maintenance; a monastery also depended on food and support from nearby communities. Symbolic representations associated with the Buddha reveal religious visual conventions that differed across periods. The official page uses the term “Hinayana”, but contemporary academic discussion often prefers more precise descriptions of early Buddhist practice because the older label can be polemical or imprecise. Students should identify the architectural evidence first and avoid equating one artistic convention with the beliefs of every resident.

## 4. Ikshvakus and the Buddhist network of the Krishna valley

The Ikshvakus are associated with the lower Krishna valley and the Nagarjunakonda region, today administratively in Andhra Pradesh, while related Buddhist art and inscriptions illuminate neighbouring Telangana. Modern state boundaries must not obscure the interconnected cultural landscape of ancient trade routes. The Telangana Department of Heritage identifies Phanigiri as a major early historic Buddhist complex and reports a stucco Bodhisattva image in Ikshvaku-period style. A stylistic attribution is evidence of artistic connection, not proof that every patron or monk at the site was an Ikshvaku official.

The network included monasteries, stupas, congregation spaces and donors. Women and non-royal patrons may appear in inscriptional evidence, challenging an exclusively king-centred narrative. Buddhist establishments could receive support from merchants and local households as well as elites. The religious map was plural: Buddhism, Brahmanical practices and Jain traditions existed in different local contexts. For mains, explain the importance of trade, patronage and art together, then add a source-critical note about the limits of assigning a site to a dynasty on the basis of style alone.

## 5. Phanigiri: excavations, chronology and original objects

Phanigiri in present-day Suryapet district is one of Telangana's strongest archaeological case studies. The state heritage department reports seven excavation seasons between 2001 and 2019 and identifies a congregation hall, chaitya structures, viharas, votive stupas and a substantial group of antiquities. It also records a discovery of thousands of lead coins in March 2024. The excavation dates are dates of modern fieldwork, not the construction dates of the ancient buildings. Similarly, a 2024 discovery of an ancient coin hoard does not mean coins were minted in 2024.

A site can contain multiple occupation phases. Architectural styles, coins, inscriptions and stratigraphic contexts need to be compared before assigning dates. A coin hoard may have been deposited long after the first coins in it were issued. Conservation adds another layer: restored monuments should be distinguished from excavated original fabric. The site's location along historical routes helps explain how Buddhist learning, goods and artistic forms circulated. An effective answer uses Phanigiri to show the relationship between archaeological method and regional cultural history, rather than merely calling it a tourist attraction.

## 6. Vishnukundins: regional power and religious landscapes

The Vishnukundins belong to the political history of the Deccan after the Satavahana and Ikshvaku phases. Their expansion and cultural influence are discussed through inscriptions, royal titles, settlement patterns and religious foundations. It is tempting to attach every early temple in Telangana to one Vishnukundin ruler, but such claims require dated inscriptions or a defensible architectural sequence. Suryapet district's official heritage account links early construction at Undrugonda with Vishnukundin-era activity; this is a useful lead, not a substitute for a published excavation report.

Religious patronage during this period included evolving Brahmanical institutions while older Buddhist sites continued to shape the landscape. Dynastic succession was not a clean replacement of one population or faith by another. Different centres could rise or decline at different times, and local chiefs or donors could continue practices across political transitions. To explain Vishnukundin history, organise the answer around political geography, inscriptions, religious foundations and cultural continuities. Avoid importing legendary temple narratives as verified dynastic chronology. When a site's attribution rests on tradition, explicitly label it as tradition.

## 7. Jainism, Buddhism and cultural pluralism

Jain and Buddhist histories in Telangana are often reduced to the names of a few monasteries or statues. A fuller account asks why renunciant traditions attracted patronage, how teachers and merchants moved through the Deccan, and what forms of social organisation sustained religious sites. Buddhist stupas and viharas required donors and communities; Jain images and later centres indicate different strands of devotional and monastic practice. The existence of a monument demonstrates the presence of a tradition, not the religious affiliation of an entire district.

Comparing material culture can reveal both overlap and difference. Sculptural conventions, inscriptions, donor lists and trade routes are more reliable than assuming a continuous line from a modern festival to a specific ancient dynasty. Telangana's State Museum and district collections hold Buddhist and Jain objects, providing opportunities to verify iconography and provenance. A balanced mains answer acknowledges plural patronage, the role of lay supporters and changing artistic practices. It should also avoid a teleological narrative in which one tradition inevitably displaced another everywhere at the same moment.

## 8. Answer-writing model: evidence before chronology

For a question on early historic Telangana, begin by locating the region within Deccan trade and settlement networks. Next distinguish the Satavahana political phase, the Ikshvaku-connected Buddhist world and the later Vishnukundin period. Use Kotilingala, Dhulikatta, Peddabankur and Phanigiri as named archaeological examples, and explain what their coins, inscriptions, brick structures and sculptures can actually prove. A table comparing material sources is more useful than a long list of unverified dates. End with the importance of interdisciplinary research and preservation.

A high-quality answer also identifies limitations. The administrative claim that Kotilingala was the “first capital” requires comparison with specialist numismatic and epigraphic scholarship. Phanigiri's Ikshvaku-style art should not be conflated with direct political control. Modern discovery dates must not be confused with ancient chronology. This source-critical approach helps students avoid common preliminary-exam traps and produces a credible mains discussion.

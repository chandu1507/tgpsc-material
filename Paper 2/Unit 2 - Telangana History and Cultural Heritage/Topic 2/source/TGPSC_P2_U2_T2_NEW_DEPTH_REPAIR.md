# Medieval Telangana: evidence, resistance, political ecology and cultural exchange

**TGPSC Group I · Paper II · Unit II · Topic 2 | New focused research supplement | 26 October 2026**

**Status:** Original study material, not an official previous-year question paper, not a replacement for the preserved older PDF, and not independently academically approved.

## Exact syllabus boundary and learning outcomes

The commission's Notification 02/2024 (19 February 2024), Paper II, Section II, Item 2, prescribes medieval Telangana and composite culture; Kakatiya and Velama contributions to social-cultural development, literature, music, dance, art and architecture; Qutb Shahi contributions to Telugu literature, art and architecture; and protest movements associated with Sammakka–Sarakka and Sarvai Papanna. This supplement is designed to deepen **source interpretation and historical argument**, not to reproduce the already published medieval-institutions supplement. After reading it, students should distinguish inscriptional, architectural, literary and oral evidence; connect water and fortification to political economy; explain linguistic patronage without claiming universal harmony; and treat resistance traditions according to the dates and genres of their sources.

## 1. A source hierarchy for medieval Telangana: what can actually be known?

An inscription fixed to a temple, copperplate or tomb can often establish a donor, title, grant, dedication or regnal date, but it is not a neutral census of social life. Its audience and purpose matter. A royal eulogy magnifies victory, while a land-grant charter may specify an institution's privileges and exclude ordinary cultivators from its narrative. Architecture can establish spatial planning and construction technology; a later restoration, however, cannot automatically be assigned to the original builders. Court poetry reveals language, aesthetic ideals and patronage, not an exact transcript of everyday speech. Oral accounts preserve community memory and moral claims; they do not supply precise medieval battle dates unless corroborated independently.

These differences matter most when a question joins Kakatiya authority, Sammakka–Sarakka resistance and Qutb Shahi cosmopolitanism. The three subjects are not evidenced in the same way. A student who treats a Medaram narrative, a dated Persian epitaph and a modern heritage notice as interchangeable sources commits a methodological error. Build a matrix: **claim → earliest source → source genre → corroboration → uncertainty**. Where no contemporary corroboration has been inspected, write 'tradition associates' rather than 'an inscription proves'. This protects historical dignity without manufacturing certainty.

## 2. Kakatiya chronology through political transitions, not memorised reign lists

The Kakatiyas emerged within the Western Chalukya political sphere before developing an independent centre around Hanamkonda and Warangal. Rudradeva's political consolidation, Ganapati Deva's expansion, Rudrama Devi's sovereignty and Prataparudra's confrontation with Delhi are stages in changing relations with chiefs, armies, religious institutions and revenue-producing territories. The 1323 conquest of Warangal by Ulugh Khan's forces ended Kakatiya dynastic rule, but did not end Telugu cultural production or tank-based agriculture. The capital's fall and the transformation of local authority are different historical processes.

Chronology must be tested against epigraphic dates and carefully edited narratives. The 1163 Hanamkonda inscription associated with Rudradeva is frequently used for the Thousand Pillar Temple context, while the Palampet inscription of 1213 associates the Ramappa complex with Recharla Rudra under Ganapati Deva. These are useful anchors, not proof that every surviving structure was built in exactly the same year. When a ruler's name appears in a source, ask whether it records construction, endowment, victory, repair or later commemoration. Avoid assuming that royal sovereignty meant complete territorial control at every moment.

## 3. Recharla Rudra, Ramappa and the difference between king and patron

The Rudreshwara temple at Palampet illustrates the gap between dynastic branding and actual patronage. Its building history belongs to the Kakatiya period, but Recharla Rudra, a commander under Ganapati Deva, is central to the patronage record. A textbook answer that credits every aspect of Ramappa directly to Ganapati Deva overlooks the role of military elites and local institutional networks. UNESCO's property description connects the temple with sculpted granite and dolerite pillars, a lightweight brick superstructure, dance imagery and the neighbouring reservoir landscape. The site demonstrates skilled labour and technical adaptation rather than a single ruler's isolated achievement.

Its 2021 World Heritage inscription is a **modern conservation designation**, not a medieval date. UNESCO's 2025 boundary modification is likewise contemporary heritage administration. The setting matters: temple, lake, fields and forest edge form an interdependent cultural landscape. Heritage conservation cannot focus only on the carved sanctum while allowing catchment changes to damage its historical environment. A mains answer should distinguish original inscription, construction phases, present-day restoration and heritage management, explaining what each source actually establishes.

## 4. Tanks as fiscal, ecological and social institutions

Telangana's rainfall seasonality made storage critical for cultivation. A reservoir increased the possibility of second crops or cultivation on land beyond immediate rainfall, but its effect depended on sluices, catchment health, bund maintenance and allocation rules. A tank could increase agricultural output, thereby raising the revenue potential of a region and the capacity of chiefs to fund temples, soldiers and urban centres. This relationship explains why irrigation works can be political documents even when they look like engineering projects.

Yet the distribution of benefits was never automatic. Landholders nearer the outlet might command more reliable access, while labouring households contributed to repairs without enjoying proportional gains. Inscriptions tend to preserve donor names, not a complete record of who excavated earth or desilted channels. Archaeological identification of Pakhal, Laknavaram and Ramappa tanks must be done site by site; a modern tourism description cannot establish identical medieval management for all three. Contemporary Mission Kakatiya provides a useful comparison about restoration needs, but not evidence of unbroken medieval administrative procedures. The strongest analysis joins hydrology, social hierarchy, labour and political authority.

## 5. Warangal: fortification, ceremonial space and changing urban layers

Warangal is often reduced to its stone gateways. The historical city, however, contained defensive lines, entrances, ritual spaces, roads and productive communities. The Kakatiya Kala Thoranams express elite ceremonial design; the fort's successive modifications reflect conflict and re-use. An intact gateway does not permit a confident reconstruction of a lost palace without survey evidence. Nor does a surviving wall establish the ethnic identity or wages of its builders. Urban history requires reading landscape and material layers together.

The 1323 conquest altered who claimed taxes and military allegiance, but local craft skills, waterworks and religious networks could persist. Later states appropriated or altered earlier urban fabric. A strong comparison asks what changed rapidly—dynastic sovereignty, garrisons and fiscal claims—and what changed more slowly—settlement, irrigation, language and occupational networks. Such a distinction is superior to both 'total destruction' and 'complete continuity' narratives. Archaeological uncertainty should be made explicit rather than filled with dramatic descriptions of a single battle.

## 6. Rudrama Devi and the political grammar of gender

Rudrama Devi's reign demonstrates that a woman could exercise sovereignty in a system whose military and genealogical vocabulary was often masculine. Regnal titles, succession strategies, relationships with commanders and the acceptance of authority by local elites were political resources. Her rule should not be described merely as a modern empowerment story; it unfolded within a specific aristocratic order. Inscriptions and later narrative sources do not carry equal weight for every episode of her life. Claims about precise battlefield circumstances, rebel lists or final moments require more than repetition in modern popular histories.

Gender analysis must distinguish the exceptional royal office from the position of ordinary women. A female sovereign does not establish equal inheritance, labour rights or access to education across the realm. Conversely, the existence of patriarchal structures does not negate Rudrama Devi's agency. Students should explain the institutional constraints under which she exercised power, then identify the evidence and its limitations. This yields a balanced answer on governance, not a slogan.

## 7. Velama polities: Rachakonda and Devarakonda after 1323

The post-Kakatiya era was not a simple relay in which one unified kingdom replaced another. Local chiefs, Musunuri leaders, Velama rulers and expanding Bahmani and Vijayanagara powers negotiated shifting alliances. Rachakonda and Devarakonda were distinct fortified centres associated with Padma Nayaka Velama lineages. Their location and fortifications indicate the importance of regional control, access routes and defensible terrain. District heritage accounts preserve useful site identifications, but sweeping claims of exact territorial boundaries or uninterrupted dates should be checked against inscriptions and critical political histories.

Velama patronage can be analysed through courtly Telugu literature, temple grants and fort architecture. A royal genealogy praises a lineage; it does not automatically reveal the status of every pastoralist, farmer or artisan under that court. Equally, alliances across religious or linguistic lines were strategic and varied by circumstance. Explain political fragmentation as a process of state formation, not merely as disorder. The forts illustrate how local sovereignty continued to matter between the Kakatiya and later Deccan sultanate periods.

## 8. The cultural economy of Telugu literary production

Telugu literary activity crossed dynastic boundaries. Palakuriki Somanatha's Vira Shaiva writings show devotional, vernacular and religious debates; Jayapa Senani's *Nrtta Ratnavali* documents categories of performance; other scholars worked through Sanskrit, Telugu and regional oral traditions. Patronage did not mean a king personally authored or supervised every text. Literary production required scribes, performers, religious teachers, manuscript transmission and audiences, many of whom are absent from dynastic narratives.

A good literary answer identifies **author, work, genre, approximate context, patronage and interpretive limit**. The existence of a courtly text does not prove that every village spoke its literary register. A modern printed edition may regularise spellings and remove textual variants. Consequently, claims about exact original wording need a critical edition, not a secondary paraphrase. This supplement does not assert that all cited works have been checked against their earliest manuscripts. The methodological distinction between original composition and modern reconstruction is part of the syllabus's cultural-history objective.

## 9. Perini, sculptural evidence and the limits of revival

Kakatiya-era performance history combines textual categories in *Nrtta Ratnavali*, sculptural figures at Ramappa and later performance traditions. These sources can illuminate one another, but cannot be equated. A sculpture freezes a pose; it cannot demonstrate the full sequence, rhythm, costume or social meaning of an entire performance. A prescriptive treatise records an intellectual classification that may not reflect every local performance. Modern Perini Shivatandavam revival has cultural value while involving interpretation and reconstruction.

To evaluate a claim of 'unbroken continuity', demand evidence across the intervening centuries. Without that chain, describe a modern revival drawing on medieval heritage. This is not disrespectful: it clarifies the distinction between historical documentation and creative cultural stewardship. Telangana cultural institutions can use dance heritage in education and tourism, but historical claims should be proportionate to the sources. In mains writing, contrast textual, sculptural and living evidence under separate subheadings.

## 10. Qutb Shahi transition and the evidence of built space

The Qutb Shahi dynasty developed within the Deccan sultanate world and ruled from the early sixteenth century until Mughal conquest in 1687. Golconda's fortifications, water systems, royal necropolis and commercial spaces reflect state power as well as urban labour. UNESCO's tentative-list dossier, submitted in 2010, describes the monuments as a connected cultural landscape and identifies dated Persian inscriptions on tombs and fort structures. It is an official nomination document with valuable evidence references, but **tentative listing is not World Heritage inscription**.

Hyderabad's foundation under Muhammad Quli Qutb Shah in 1591 added a planned urban centre linked to Charminar and axial streets. The city's founding should be analysed through administration, markets, ceremonial design and migration, not only a romantic legend. The existence of mosques, temples, tombs and bazaars in the wider region points to multiple communities but does not itself establish equal political power. A built environment is a record of aspirations, investment and adaptation, not a complete social survey.

## 11. Persian, Dakhni and Telugu: distinct functions, shared patronage

Qutb Shahi cultural exchange is clearest when languages are separated by function. Persian operated prominently in courtly and administrative culture; Dakhni developed as an important poetic and urban language; Telugu retained literary, devotional and regional significance. Ibrahim Qutb Shah is associated with Telugu patronage, while Muhammad Quli Qutb Shah is remembered for Dakhni poetry. Such examples demonstrate cross-linguistic sponsorship but cannot justify a claim that all languages enjoyed identical access to court office or wealth.

A text's patron and intended audience shape its language. Merchants, artisans and migrants could sustain multilingual practices beyond formal court culture. Translation, performance and material exchange created contact zones without erasing religious differences. The historian should distinguish literary cosmopolitanism from social equality: a multilingual court may coexist with revenue coercion and stratification. Avoid projecting today's language politics backwards or assuming that one courtly language completely replaced local speech. Comparative questions should name specific patrons, works and institutions instead of repeating 'unity in diversity'.

## 12. Diamond commerce, textiles and the labour behind Golconda's wealth

Golconda became associated with diamond commerce and trade routes linking inland production and the coast. UNESCO's nomination material places the fortified city within routes connected to Masulipatam and identifies commercial functions alongside royal and religious spaces. Trade wealth depended on miners, transport workers, merchants, textile producers, money handlers and state taxation. A famous gemstone's later story is not sufficient evidence of where, when and by whom it was extracted. Nor should every diamond sold through Golconda be assumed to have been mined directly beneath the fort.

Economic history must distinguish production site, commercial market and dynastic tax claim. Overseas traders' accounts can document prices and routes but may exaggerate novelty or omit local intermediaries. Comparing commercial growth with agrarian burdens reveals that prosperity was unevenly distributed. In a mains answer, explain how the court benefited from market concentration while craft and labour networks made exchange possible. This approach moves beyond monument-centred history to the region's social economy.

## 13. Sammakka–Sarakka: oral history, Koya memory and protest

Sammakka and Saralamma (Sarakka) are central to the Medaram Jatara and to Koya/Adivasi memory of resistance to coercive rule. Telangana district and tourism accounts explicitly present the conflict as a **tradition or legend** associated with the Kakatiya period. They are valuable documentation of living heritage and the meanings attached to the festival, not contemporary thirteenth-century eyewitness records. The precise date, dialogue and military sequence should not be taught as certified epigraphic fact unless a matching source is produced.

The Jatara is also a present-day institution involving ritual specialists, forest space, pilgrimage logistics and public administration. Its scale and official recognition show the continuing vitality of community memory, but contemporary crowd estimates cannot verify medieval battle details. Students should respect Adivasi agency by analysing the tradition's themes—forest autonomy, taxation, kinship and sacrifice—while distinguishing them from historical proof. The district page itself recounts a legend and separately notes that Jampanna Vagu's reddish colour has a soil explanation; these genres must not be collapsed into one scientific claim.

## 14. Sarvai Papanna: chronology, forts and social aspiration

Sarvai Papanna Goud belongs to the late seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries, in the period surrounding the Qutb Shahi state's absorption into Mughal rule. He cannot be placed in the Kakatiya thirteenth century simply because the syllabus pairs resistance traditions. Popular narratives emphasise his challenge to entrenched elites and his appeal as a Bahujan figure. Khilashapur Fort is a tangible heritage site associated with his memory; conservation notices confirm the existence of modern work on its walls and bastions, but do not authenticate every episode of a popular biography.

An evidence-led answer should separate contemporary or near-contemporary administrative records, later regional chronicles, fort archaeology and modern commemorations. The Telangana government's 2025 heritage-development announcement and 2026 public memorial are evidence of current recognition, not direct proof of early eighteenth-century campaigns. Historical importance includes both Papanna's political mobilisation and the later public memory of social dignity. Avoid simplistic caste essentialism: mobilisation, alliances and antagonists may have varied over time, and the available archive is uneven.

## 15. Two resistance traditions, two source regimes

The syllabus juxtaposes Sammakka–Sarakka and Sarvai Papanna to invite comparison, not merger. Sammakka–Sarakka's tradition is rooted in Adivasi ritual memory associated with Kakatiya-era coercion; Papanna's political career is placed centuries later amid late Qutb Shahi and Mughal realignments. The first is documented today through oral and festival practice; the second can also be approached through fort remains and administrative-history questions. Both can express resistance to hierarchical extraction, but the specific opponents, dates and forms of mobilisation differ.

Comparison should have four axes: **historical setting, social constituency, form of evidence and afterlife of memory**. A good answer explains that present-day commemoration shapes identity while not erasing the need for source criticism. It avoids inventing a direct organisational link between the movements. If asked to discuss their 'contribution to Telangana identity', address how memory survives through ritual, naming, museums, public policy and education, then acknowledge that identity is interpreted differently by communities and historians.

## 16. Applied heritage governance: from medieval sources to current choices

A district heritage officer considering repairs to a tank adjoining a temple must coordinate archaeology, catchment hydrology and local water users. A conservation proposal should document which structures are original, which are restored and which are modern additions. At Ramappa, UNESCO specifically flags development near the lake and the need for heritage impact assessment. A tourism officer planning Medaram facilities must consult community ritual authorities and forest administrators; increasing visitor capacity does not authorise rewriting the community's oral history as certified medieval chronology.

A curator explaining Golconda should display Persian, Dakhni and Telugu examples with their provenance and social context rather than a vague label of 'perfect harmony'. At Khilashapur, restoration documentation should distinguish conservation records from claims about Papanna's exact campaigns. These four cases illustrate a common governance principle: heritage policy can respect identity while making the evidence and uncertainty transparent. For examinations, connect a specific historical source to an administrative decision and a likely trade-off: access versus conservation, tourism versus local ritual control, or commemoration versus historical precision.

## 18. Original diagrams — interpretation keys

**Diagram A: Evidence confidence ladder.** A dated inscription or architectural fabric supports bounded material claims; an edited literary work supports authorial or cultural claims; oral tradition supports lived memory; a modern press release supports present-day policy. Cross-check before extending any of them to military events or social statistics.

**Diagram B: Political ecology of the Kakatiya landscape.** Seasonal rainfall → storage and maintenance → cultivated surplus → local power and royal claims → temples, markets and military support. Inequality in land and water access can alter each arrow.

**Diagram C: Separate timelines of resistance.** Kakatiya-period Sammakka–Sarakka association (oral tradition) ≠ late-seventeenth/early-eighteenth-century Papanna (fort and administrative-history questions). Both are commemorated today, but evidence types differ.

# Socio-Cultural Awakening in Telangana: Associations, Reform, Libraries, Women and Political Mobilization

**Paper II · Unit II · Topic 4 — expanded draft; not academically approved**

## 1. Why socio-cultural awakening matters in Telangana history

The awakening of twentieth-century Telangana was not a single organization or a simple prelude to statehood. It was a layered process in princely Hyderabad in which language associations, libraries, anti-caste reformers, women organizers, religious reform bodies, teachers, journalists and political activists widened the public sphere. Hyderabad State was multilingual and ruled by the Nizam under British paramountcy. Unequal land relations, restricted representative politics, uneven access to modern education and the dominance of elite languages in administration shaped public grievances. Cultural activity could therefore become political even when an association formally claimed to be literary or educational. Reading rooms, public lectures, schools and conferences created networks through which people debated language, caste, gender and civil rights.

## 2. Hyderabad State as the institutional setting

Movements must be located within Hyderabad State rather than projected backward from post-1956 Andhra Pradesh. Telugu speakers formed a major population in Telangana districts, while Marathi and Kannada speakers also belonged to the state. Urdu occupied an important official and educational position. This multilingual setting generated both cross-regional exchange and demands for mother-tongue education and literary recognition. Political organizations faced varying degrees of surveillance and restriction, so cultural bodies sometimes supplied the safer organizational infrastructure from which broader public mobilization later developed. The distinction between cultural and political work was therefore porous.

## 3. Print culture, education and the formation of a reading public

The spread of schools, periodicals, pamphlets and inexpensive printed books gradually enlarged the constituency for public debate. Literacy remained highly unequal by caste, gender, class and locality, but new readers included teachers, students, clerks and professionals. Print allowed reformers to criticize untouchability, promote education, discuss women’s rights and circulate historical or linguistic arguments. A printed text did not automatically create mass participation: oral readings, meetings and library gatherings often carried ideas to people who were not individually literate. For mains answers, connect print to association-building rather than treating newspapers as isolated publications.

## 4. The library movement as social infrastructure

Libraries and reading rooms were important because they combined books with organization. They provided venues for lectures, language classes, discussion circles and the circulation of Telugu literature. A library could nurture political consciousness without being formally constituted as a political party. The movement also strengthened networks among towns and villages and enabled activists to work around restrictions on overt political mobilization. Its significance lies less in a count of buildings than in the creation of a durable public sphere. At the same time, access was uneven; gender, caste and distance could determine who actually used these institutions.

## 5. Andhra Jana Sangham and linguistic-cultural assertion

The Nizam Rashtra Andhra Jana Sangham emerged in the context of Telugu-speaking elites and intellectuals seeking greater space for Telugu language, literature and education within Hyderabad State. Its activities helped connect linguistic pride with educational and social questions. The organization should not be described as a fully developed separatist political party: its early emphasis was cultural and associational. Yet by bringing activists together and sponsoring public activity it contributed to a more organized Telugu public in Telangana. This demonstrates an important historical sequence—cultural self-organization can precede explicit mass politics.

## 6. From Andhra Jana Sangham to Andhra Mahasabha

The Andhra Mahasabha became a larger forum through which language, education and social reform concerns could be discussed. Over time, especially as rural grievances sharpened, sections of the organization became more politically assertive. Internal ideological differences grew: moderates, nationalists and left-oriented activists did not always agree on methods or priorities. It is therefore misleading to treat the Mahasabha as a single fixed organization with one ideology throughout its history. Its importance lies in institutional continuity combined with political transformation. It linked intellectual and linguistic activism to rural meetings, peasant issues and broader anti-autocratic demands.

## 7. Andhra Mahasabha and rural politicization

As branches and conferences reached wider constituencies, questions of excessive levies, forced labour, landlord power and civil liberties gained importance. Organizers translated general reform language into concrete local demands. This provided a bridge between urban intellectual networks and agrarian mobilization. The process was uneven and contested: some leaders feared radicalization, while others increasingly cooperated with communists and peasant activists. In examination answers, separate the Mahasabha’s cultural origins from its later role in mass politics; both phases are part of the same institutional history but should not be collapsed.

## 8. Andhra Saraswatha Parishat and literary institution-building

Literary organizations strengthened Telugu through publications, lectures, examinations, teaching and the cultivation of writers and readers. Andhra Saraswatha Parishat belonged to this institutional world. Its historical importance is not simply that it celebrated literature; literary work could preserve vocabulary, standardize educational practices, create cultural confidence and link dispersed readers. Under conditions where linguistic questions were tied to access to schooling and administration, literary activity could have wider social consequences. Avoid claiming that one organization alone created modern Telugu consciousness; journals, libraries, schools, individual writers and other associations formed a larger ecosystem.

## 9. Brahmo Samaj: reform through reason and social critique

Brahmo Samaj influence in Hyderabad and the wider Telugu world belonged to nineteenth- and early twentieth-century currents of religious and social reform. Its ideas included monotheism, criticism of certain ritual practices and support among some reformers for education and changes in social custom. Its organizational reach in Telangana was not identical to Bengal, so an answer should emphasize influence and local adaptation rather than assume a mass movement of the same scale everywhere. Reformist networks often intersected with education and the emerging professional middle class.

## 10. Arya Samaj in Hyderabad State

Arya Samaj developed a more visible organizational presence in Hyderabad State and combined religious reform with education, social service and, in some periods, assertive public campaigns. It opposed caste discrimination in principle in several of its activities, promoted schools and participated in debates over religious identity and civil rights. Its relations with the Nizam’s administration and with other communities changed over time. The movement should neither be romanticized as purely inclusive nor reduced to later communal politics. Historical analysis should identify particular campaigns, institutions and periods.

## 11. Adi-Hindu mobilization and the making of Dalit public politics

Adi-Hindu activism challenged the assumption that oppressed castes had to accept inherited status within a hierarchical social order. By adopting the language of original inhabitants and dignity, activists created a counter-identity that rejected stigmatizing caste labels. Education, organization, public meetings and campaigns against untouchability were central. Telangana’s Dalit politics developed through local organizations and leaders while also interacting with wider anti-caste currents in India. The movement is best understood as both social reform and political self-assertion: marginalized communities sought not charity but recognition, access and collective voice.

## 12. Bhagyareddy Varma: education, dignity and organization

Bhagyareddy Varma is central to the history of Dalit awakening in Hyderabad. He organized around self-respect, education and opposition to untouchability and degrading practices. The Jagan Mitra Mandali and later Adi-Hindu organizational work helped build institutions through which Dalit communities articulated their own social claims. He used meetings, schools and culturally accessible forms of communication to reach wider audiences. His importance lies not only in personal biography but in institution-building: the movement created cadres, vocabulary and public visibility. A strong answer should place him within both Hyderabad’s caste structure and the broader twentieth-century anti-caste transformation.

## 13. Social reform and the problem of elite paternalism

Many reform movements spoke in the language of uplift, but “uplift” could be paternalistic if dominant groups defined the needs of marginalized communities without sharing power. Dalit-led organizations altered this relationship by insisting on self-representation. Similarly, women’s organizations increasingly moved from charitable activity toward education, legal awareness and public participation. Historical evaluation should therefore ask who led a reform, who set its agenda, and whether beneficiaries became decision-makers. This distinction turns a descriptive list of societies into analytical social history.

## 14. Women’s education and entry into public life

Women’s participation expanded through schools, hostels, social-service organizations, conferences and nationalist or regional associations. The gains were uneven: class, caste, religion, urban location and family resources shaped access to education. Women were not merely auxiliaries to male political leaders; they organized institutions, taught, wrote, raised funds, addressed social customs and later joined political campaigns. At the same time, organizations sometimes reproduced middle-class ideals of respectable femininity. A balanced answer recognizes both expanded agency and the continuing constraints on most women.

## 15. Andhra Mahila Sabha and organized women’s work

Andhra Mahila Sabha became an important institution in the wider Telugu public sphere through education, welfare, health and women’s organization. In a Telangana syllabus answer, connect women’s associations to the broader transformation of public life in Hyderabad and the Telugu-speaking world while avoiding geographical overstatement about where every institution originated. The key contribution was sustained institution-building: women’s public participation became linked to education, social work and professional training rather than being confined to episodic protest.

## 16. Students as political actors

Students were especially important because educational institutions concentrated literate young people who could circulate slogans, songs and political literature. Hostels and colleges formed networks that linked cultural identity with civil-liberty demands. Student protest also exposed the contradiction between modern education and restricted political expression. This became particularly visible during the Vande Mataram controversy in Hyderabad State, when the nationalist song became a symbol of the right to political expression.

## 17. The Vande Mataram movement in Hyderabad

The Vande Mataram movement in Hyderabad State is associated with student protest in the late 1930s, especially around restrictions on singing the nationalist song in educational institutions. The controversy was not merely about a song; it concerned freedom of expression, student discipline, nationalism and the authority of the princely state. Punitive action against students helped politicize educational spaces and connected Hyderabad’s youth to wider Indian nationalist currents. Precise institutional details and numbers vary in secondary accounts, so final notes should rely on archival or university records before asserting exact expulsion figures.

## 18. Hyderabad State Congress: from civil rights to political challenge

The Hyderabad State Congress represented a more explicitly political challenge to autocratic government than earlier literary associations. It demanded responsible government and civil liberties and faced restrictions from the Nizam’s administration. Its relationship with the Indian National Congress was politically important but should not erase the specific conditions of the princely state. The organization drew on earlier associational networks and on the new educated middle classes. Its history shows how socio-cultural awakening could culminate in constitutional and political demands.

## 19. Religious identity, reform and communal tension

Reform organizations sometimes operated through religious identities, and the expansion of organized politics could produce both solidarity and competition. Arya Samaj activity, Hindu and Muslim political associations, and state policies interacted in complex ways. Composite culture in Hyderabad coexisted with communal tensions; neither should be used to erase the other. The historian’s task is to identify specific institutions, policies and conflicts rather than project a simple harmony-versus-conflict binary across the entire period.

## 20. Middle classes and new occupations

Modern administration, railways, schools, courts and professional institutions expanded opportunities for clerks, teachers, lawyers and other educated groups. These middle strata helped sustain associations and periodicals because they possessed literacy, organizational skills and access to towns. Yet the rise of the middle class was socially selective and did not dissolve agrarian hierarchy. A strong answer connects middle-class expansion to public organizations while also explaining why peasant and Dalit movements developed distinct agendas.

## 21. From cultural awakening to mass politics

The sequence from libraries and literary bodies to political organizations was not inevitable, but it created organizational resources later movements could use: meeting halls, publications, speakers, district contacts, fundraising practices and an audience accustomed to public debate. Andhra Mahasabha illustrates this transition especially clearly. Cultural nationalism, social reform, anti-caste politics and demands for responsible government increasingly overlapped. By the 1940s, agrarian struggle and the crisis of princely rule pushed Telangana politics beyond the earlier reform framework.

## 22. Current-affairs connection: Bhagyareddy Varma in contemporary Telangana

The social-reform legacy remains part of official public memory. Telangana Prisons and Correctional Services reported observing Bhagyareddy Varma’s birth anniversary on 22 May 2025, emphasizing justice, equality and human dignity. More substantively, Telangana’s 2026 Socio, Economic, Educational, Employment, Political and Caste survey report discusses caste-feudal practices such as vetti and identifies Bhagyareddy Varma’s organizational role in anti-caste awakening. These recent government references can be used to connect historical reform movements with contemporary debates on social equality, but they should not replace historical primary sources.

## 23. Historiography and source criticism

The source base includes organizational proceedings, newspapers, memoirs, government surveillance reports, biographies, oral histories and later scholarship. Each has biases. Police reports may exaggerate threats or categorize activists through administrative assumptions; memoirs can reconstruct events through later political identities; commemorative biographies may turn complex leaders into flawless icons. Use cross-verification. For organizations, distinguish founding date, formal objective, actual social base and later transformation. For movements, separate a symbolic event from long-term institutional change.

## 24. Mains synthesis: how to write a high-scoring answer

Begin by defining socio-cultural awakening as expansion of organized public life under the Nizam, not simply as literary revival. Build the body around four channels: language/libraries; social and anti-caste reform; women/students; responsible-government politics. Use specific institutions—Andhra Jana Sangham, Andhra Mahasabha, Andhra Saraswatha Parishat, Adi-Hindu organizations, women’s bodies and Hyderabad State Congress. Explain transition and interaction rather than listing names. Add one paragraph on limitations: unequal literacy, elite dominance, caste and gender constraints, state repression. Conclude that awakening created social infrastructure for later democratic and mass movements.

# Complete Model Answers — Original Practice, NOT official TGPSC PYQs

## Q1. Explain how literary and library movements contributed to political awakening in Telangana.

Literary and library movements in Hyderabad State were important because they created an organized public sphere before open political mobilization became widespread. Their historical significance lay not merely in the number of books they circulated, but in the social networks they created.

Telugu-speaking intellectuals sought greater space for mother-tongue education and literature in a multilingual state in which Urdu had a major official role. Organizations such as the Nizam Rashtra Andhra Jana Sangham and later the Andhra Mahasabha brought teachers, students, writers and local activists into common forums. Libraries and reading rooms supplied venues for lectures, literacy activity and discussion. Periodicals and books circulated arguments about language, social customs, caste inequality and civil rights. Oral readings also allowed printed ideas to move beyond individually literate audiences.

The Andhra Mahasabha illustrates the transition from cultural organization to wider politics. Its early linguistic and educational agenda increasingly interacted with agrarian grievances, civil liberties and rural mobilization. Internal divisions between moderates and more radical activists show that this transition was contested rather than automatic.

The movements had limits. Literacy was unequal and access to institutions was shaped by caste, gender, class and geography. Nevertheless, literary and library networks provided speakers, meeting spaces, district contacts and habits of collective organization later used by political and peasant movements. Thus, they transformed cultural assertion into durable social infrastructure for democratic politics.

## Q2. Assess Bhagyareddy Varma’s role in the Adi-Hindu and Dalit movements of Hyderabad State.

Bhagyareddy Varma was a major figure in the emergence of autonomous Dalit public politics in Hyderabad State. His importance lies in linking social dignity with organization, education and self-representation.

He challenged untouchability and stigmatizing caste identities by advancing the language of Adi-Hindu dignity. Through bodies such as the Jagan Mitra Mandali and later organizational activity associated with the Adi-Hindu movement, he helped create forums in which marginalized communities could articulate their own demands. Education was central because literacy and schooling provided access to public employment, political information and social mobility. Meetings, cultural communication and collective action helped move anti-caste politics beyond private philanthropy.

Varma’s politics should be distinguished from elite “uplift” projects. While upper-caste reformers could condemn untouchability, Dalit-led organization changed the question of agency: those affected by exclusion increasingly sought to define priorities themselves. His work also belonged to wider twentieth-century anti-caste currents while retaining a specific Hyderabad context shaped by vetti, unequal access to land and restricted educational opportunity.

The movement did not erase caste inequality, and its reach should not be exaggerated. Yet it built institutions and a vocabulary of self-respect that influenced later Dalit mobilization. His legacy therefore lies less in one event than in making oppressed communities visible as organized political subjects.

## Q3. Why was the Vande Mataram movement important in Hyderabad State?

The Vande Mataram movement was important because a dispute over student expression became a larger confrontation over nationalism, civil liberties and princely authority. In the late 1930s, restrictions on singing the nationalist song in educational institutions generated student protest.

The issue had symbolic force. Modern educational institutions had created a generation exposed to all-India political debates, yet students remained subject to tight discipline and restricted political activity. Protest against the ban therefore questioned not only a campus rule but also the limits imposed on political expression in Hyderabad State. Administrative action against participating students further widened sympathy and connected the episode with the Indian nationalist movement.

The movement helped politicize students and demonstrated that Hyderabad could not be isolated from currents elsewhere in India. It also intersected with demands for civil rights and responsible government that were being raised by other organizations. However, historians should treat exact figures for expulsions or arrests cautiously unless tied to archival or university records.

Its significance was therefore qualitative as much as numerical: it turned educational space into a site of political contest and helped create activists who saw freedom of expression as inseparable from constitutional reform.

## Q4. Trace the transformation from socio-cultural awakening to demands for responsible government in Hyderabad.

Political awakening in Hyderabad did not begin suddenly with a party declaration. It developed through a chain of cultural, social and institutional changes. Modern education, print and literary associations first enlarged the public sphere. Telugu organizations and libraries created venues where language, educational access and social reform could be discussed.

The Nizam Rashtra Andhra Jana Sangham and Andhra Mahasabha helped organize Telugu-speaking activists. Over time, Andhra Mahasabha discussions widened from language and literature toward rural grievances and civil liberties. Parallel reform movements also transformed politics. Arya Samaj activism, Adi-Hindu mobilization under leaders such as Bhagyareddy Varma, and women’s organizations raised questions of caste, religious reform, education and gender. These movements brought new social groups into public life rather than leaving politics to court elites.

Student activism, especially the Vande Mataram controversy, exposed the contradiction between modern education and restrictions on political expression. Such experiences strengthened demands for civil rights. The Hyderabad State Congress subsequently articulated the more explicitly constitutional demand for responsible government and faced repression from the princely state.

The transition was neither linear nor unified: moderates, nationalists, communists, social reformers and community organizations differed sharply. Yet cultural associations supplied organizational skills, meeting networks and political language. Socio-cultural awakening therefore created the social infrastructure from which the struggle for representative government could expand.

# Depth-repair supplement — Vetti, Jogini, Devadasi and anti-caste reform in Telangana

**Paper II / Unit II / Topic 4 — Socio-Cultural Awakening in Telangana.** Prepared 25 October 2026. **Scope:** an original substantive appendix to an older shallow draft; the existing main chapter and original PDF remain unchanged. The supplement is not a substitute for a complete chapter rewrite and is not academically approved. It contains no purported official previous-year questions.

## 1. Why these social practices belong in a history of reform

A socio-cultural awakening is not simply the establishment of libraries or the appearance of new newspapers. It also involves struggles over who may read, own land, control labour, enter institutions, marry freely and claim dignity. Hyderabad State contained different religious, linguistic and caste communities; patterns of exclusion varied by region and community. The Telangana government's 2026 SEEEPC volume identifies *vetti*, *jeetham* and *jogini* servitude as practices associated with entrenched caste-feudal relations, with particular burdens on Dalit communities [R1]. That official summary is a starting point, not evidence that every village followed an identical system.

Reform should be studied through three linked questions: **(a)** What made the practice possible? **(b)** Who organised against it, through what institutions and cultural languages? **(c)** What changed legally, socially and materially? The same individual could face restrictions in labour, education, worship and public participation. Therefore reform cannot be reduced to a single law or leader. In mains answers, separate the social structure, the experience of those affected, the reform movement and the limits of reform. Avoid presenting victims only as passive recipients of benevolence: oppressed communities organised, petitioned, taught, resisted and built institutions.

## 2. Vetti — forced unpaid labour and the political economy of caste

**Definition and social setting.** *Vetti* refers to compelled labour rendered without fair payment or without a genuinely free choice to refuse. In Telangana's historical caste-feudal order, such obligations could be imposed through village power relations and land dependence. It should not be conflated automatically with every form of agricultural wage labour: the defining issue is coercion and the denial of meaningful consent. The 2026 Telangana SEEEPC survey volume specifically names vetti as forced unpaid labour and situates it among structural forms of caste-feudal exploitation [R1]. A textbook account should identify both the material extraction of labour and the degradation of status involved.

**How the system operated.** Control over land, credit, access to village resources and local authority could make refusal costly. Families dependent on landlords or village intermediaries might be expected to supply work for domestic service, agricultural operations, transport or other demands. The form and severity differed across localities. Labour obligations also reproduced social hierarchy: a community assigned compulsory tasks had less time for independent cultivation, schooling or paid work. This created a feedback loop in which poverty increased dependence, and dependence made coercion harder to escape. A single 'unpaid hours' estimate would not capture the loss of bargaining power or humiliation.

**Resistance and reform.** Anti-caste movements, peasant organisations and expanding educational networks challenged the legitimacy of hereditary servitude. Campaigns for self-respect made the issue political: the claim was not merely that a worker deserved a higher wage, but that no person should be bound by caste to another's service. The later constitutional and statutory framework against forced labour reflects a wider Indian rights transformation; Article 23 prohibits traffic in human beings, begar and similar forms of forced labour, while the Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act, 1976 addresses bonded labour. These later laws should not be projected backward as if they were already operative under the Nizam.

**Analytical distinction.** *Vetti* and *jeetham* can overlap but are not identical labels. Jeetham is often discussed in relation to debt-linked or bonded service, whereas vetti highlights unpaid compelled labour. Precise local usage requires source-specific evidence. The historian must distinguish debt, caste duty, landlord power and state demands instead of treating them as one undifferentiated category.

**Mains conclusion.** Vetti illustrates how economic exploitation and social subordination reinforced each other. Reform required both rights-based opposition to coercion and changes in access to land, education and collective organisation. The continuing exam lesson is to analyse power relations and lived experience, not merely memorise a definition.

## 3. Jogini — dedication, gendered vulnerability and the demand for dignity

**Meaning and context.** The *jogini* practice in parts of the Deccan involved the dedication of girls or women to a deity or religious institution, in settings where the social consequences could include restricted marital autonomy, stigma and sexual exploitation. The precise customs varied, and not every person described by a local religious label experienced identical circumstances. Nevertheless, the historical issue for social reform is whether religious language and caste hierarchy were used to normalise the denial of a woman's choice. Telangana's 2026 SEEEPC report explicitly identifies jogini servitude among the exploitative practices borne disproportionately by Dalit communities [R1].

**Root causes and mechanisms.** Poverty, unequal control over family resources, caste discrimination, lack of schooling, patriarchal expectations and the power of local patrons could make dedication difficult to resist. An explanation that blames only 'superstition' is incomplete: it ignores the economic and political incentives of people who benefited from women's restricted autonomy. The practice could affect a woman's ability to form a household on her own terms, pursue education, control earnings or secure social recognition. A historian must avoid treating religious practice itself as a sufficient explanation for exploitation; the coercive social arrangement is the object of critique.

**Organised opposition.** The Telangana SCERT social-studies textbook records that Bhagyareddy Varma and other Dalit reformers campaigned against the dedication of girls as devadasis or joginis, and linked this struggle to education and anti-untouchability work [R2]. This matters because reform was not confined to elite legislative debate. Activists used community meetings, schools, popular cultural forms and public arguments to challenge caste stigma and defend dignity. Their initiatives formed part of a wider movement for access to education and equal social standing.

**Law and implementation.** Modern legal prohibitions on dedication practices must be distinguished from historical campaigns that preceded them. The Andhra Pradesh Devadasis (Prohibition of Dedication) Act, 1988 is relevant to the legal history of the region, but a claim about its current Telangana application should be checked against authoritative state legal records and amendments before exam use. Law alone cannot resolve social exclusion if survivors lack schooling, income, healthcare, safe housing or access to justice. An administrative answer should include rehabilitation and community participation, not simply prosecution.

**Mains conclusion.** The jogini issue joins caste, gender, economic vulnerability and the politics of religious authority. Reform is best evaluated by women's actual freedom, education, safety and livelihood options rather than by the formal disappearance of a label.

## 4. Devadasi — distinguishing historical institutions from coercive dedication

**Terminology and historical caution.** *Devadasi* is a broad historical term associated with women dedicated to temple service in different regions and periods. It has included complex relationships to music, dance, patronage, ritual and social status. A responsible account does not claim that every devadasi in every period was a victim of the same practice, nor that all cultural performance traditions are reducible to sexual exploitation. However, some forms of dedication became entangled with caste hierarchy, economic dependence and denial of choice. In Telangana reform history, the relevant examination issue is the struggle against **coercive dedication and exploitation**, especially where girls lacked meaningful consent.

**Comparison with jogini.** The terms are related in reform literature but should not be used as perfect synonyms. 'Jogini' has a more specific regional usage in parts of Telangana and the Deccan; 'devadasi' is used more widely across South Asian temple-service histories. The SCERT Telangana textbook discusses campaigns against dedicating girls as devadasis or joginis [R2], which supports studying their overlap in social reform without erasing differences of place, ritual, class and historical period. In an exam answer, say 'related but context-dependent institutions' rather than inventing a universal definition.

**Social and economic dimensions.** The historical vulnerability of some dedicated women arose from restrictions on education, property, mobility, marriage and access to alternative livelihoods. These conditions could be reinforced by powerful patrons and social stigma. A moralising account that portrays women as responsible for their exploitation reproduces the injustice it claims to criticise. A better analysis asks who controlled the decision to dedicate, who received the benefits, whether exit was possible and what material resources survivors possessed.

**Reform and cultural politics.** Anti-caste organisers, women's advocates and educators questioned the legitimacy of hereditary obligations imposed on girls. At the same time, reform movements sometimes debated how to protect women without dismissing all forms of performance culture. The historian should distinguish preservation of artistic traditions from preservation of coercive social relations. Cultural history and human-rights analysis are complementary, not mutually exclusive.

**Mains conclusion.** Devadasi history requires nuance: examine diversity of institutions, then focus precisely on coercion, discrimination and restricted agency where evidence supports those claims. This distinction improves both factual accuracy and the ethical quality of an answer.

## 5. Bhagyareddy Varma, Adi-Hindu assertion and educational reform

**Background.** Telangana's SCERT social-studies text identifies Bhagyareddy Varma (1888–1939) as an important Dalit reformer and records his role in raising awareness, opposing untouchability and promoting education [R2]. It associates his work with the Jagan Mitra Mandali established in 1906. The government's 2026 SEEEPC volume also links Varma to the Adi Hindu Social Service League in 1922 and emphasises his use of public communication and cultural forms [R1]. These dates should be presented with the named source because organisations may appear under variant names in different historical accounts.

**Ideas and methods.** The language of 'Adi Hindu' asserted dignity and challenged caste ranking by contesting narratives of social inferiority. Reformers established educational initiatives, organised community discussion and used familiar cultural media to reach people excluded from formal institutions. This combination is significant: literacy could widen access to employment and public argument, while collective organisation gave individuals greater capacity to resist local power. The movement should not be described as a single-person achievement; Varma's role belongs within a network of community organisers, teachers and participants.

**Relation to women's reform.** The SCERT textbook explicitly connects Dalit reform campaigns to opposition against dedication of girls as joginis or devadasis [R2]. Thus anti-untouchability and women's dignity were linked, rather than separate chapters with no connection. The larger analytical lesson is that social hierarchies intersect: caste, gender, poverty and literacy jointly shaped the options available to individuals.

**Historical evaluation.** Do not treat a reform organisation's founding date as proof that exploitation ended. Measure outcomes through education, participation, changing social norms and the durability of collective institutions. A strong mains answer should identify the movement's ideas, concrete methods and constraints, then conclude with its contribution to Telangana's socio-cultural awakening.

## 6. Libraries, Telugu public discussion and the wider awakening

Libraries and reading rooms were important because they created spaces for language, debate and organisation outside narrow official channels. Telangana SCERT records the Andhra Jana Sangham's efforts under Madapati Hanumantha Rao and others to establish libraries, reading rooms, circulate Telugu manuscripts and publish booklets [R3]. Its account describes education and petitions as early objectives before wider social and political demands emerged. This is a sequence of expanding public participation, not a claim that every library was a revolutionary organisation.

Reading movements helped connect students, scholars, peasants, workers and other groups through shared texts and meetings. Yet the social reach of literacy was unequal: caste, gender, wealth and geography could limit who entered those spaces. A critical history therefore asks whether the library movement broadened participation among women and marginalised communities and how reformers addressed barriers. Its significance lies in the emergence of an informed public sphere and the circulation of claims about rights, education and social reform.

**Comparison and conclusion.** Vetti concerned coerced labour; jogini and some forms of devadasi dedication concerned gendered and caste-linked restrictions; the Adi-Hindu movement asserted social dignity; and libraries expanded the infrastructure of public argument. Together they reveal that Telangana's socio-cultural awakening was simultaneously economic, educational, gendered and political. This appendix expands these concepts, but the original chapter still needs a full independent academic audit and a deeper treatment of other syllabus components.

# Additional depth repair (25 October 2026)

# Supplementary depth repair — Library movement, Adi-Hindu mobilisation and social reform in Hyderabad State

**Status:** Original analytical supplement to an older shallow draft. This does not replace the existing chapter PDF or constitute independent academic approval. Verified core facts draw on Telangana SCERT Class VIII Social Studies (2022–23 edition) and the Telangana Government Oriental Manuscripts Library and Research Institute's institutional history, checked 25 October 2026.

## 1. Why a library movement was a social movement

In a princely state where official language, educational opportunity and public debate were unevenly distributed, a reading room was more than a place to store books. It created a relatively durable public space in which students, teachers, small traders and aspiring writers could encounter newspapers, discuss social problems and organise lectures. The political importance of such spaces depended on who could enter them, what languages they stocked and whether authorities permitted meetings. A library that circulated Telugu books could broaden access for some Telangana readers, while remaining inaccessible to women, non-literate labourers or people excluded by caste. Therefore the growth of libraries should be evaluated as a partial expansion of the public sphere, not automatically as mass democratisation.

Telangana SCERT identifies the Sri Krishnadevaraya Andhra Bhasha Nilayam, established in Hyderabad in 1901 by Komarraju Lakshmana Rao with Nayani Venkatarangarao and Ravichettu Ranga Rao, as a landmark in Telugu literary organisation. The historical point is not merely the year. A named institution shows how language-based cultural work acquired continuity through premises, collections and collective effort. The library connected printed works circulating from the Andhra region with readers in the Hyderabad dominion. That cross-regional circulation did not mean the two regions had identical political interests. Literary exchange and the later debate about employment safeguards operated on different planes.

## 2. Language, dignity and the 1921 episode

SCERT records an incident in 1921 at a meeting in Hyderabad's Viveka Vardhini College where a proposal made in Telugu was reportedly slighted in favour of English or Urdu. The incident matters because it illustrates how a language hierarchy could become a claim about dignity and equal participation. It should not be simplified into an assertion that every Urdu speaker opposed Telugu. Hyderabad was multilingual, and both Urdu and Telugu were used by internally diverse communities. The relevant political mechanism was exclusion from prestigious forums through unequal linguistic status.

The library movement answered that problem by circulating reading material and nurturing the ability to speak publicly in Telugu. But the creation of a Telugu-language public sphere was not the same as securing education for all classes. Reading rooms could be concentrated in towns and accessible mainly to educated men. A stronger historical evaluation asks which groups participated, which genres circulated, whether women had access, and whether literature addressed landlessness and caste discrimination. These questions make the movement socially intelligible rather than merely celebratory.

## 3. Andhra Jana Sangham and the diffusion of reading rooms

Telangana SCERT describes the Andhra Jana Sangham, associated with Madapati Hanumantha Rao and others in the 1920s, as encouraging libraries, reading rooms, students and Telugu manuscripts. Such activity joined cultural assertion to organisational skills: keeping membership lists, raising funds, inviting speakers and coordinating meetings. These skills could later be reused in broader social and political campaigns. However, the organisation's early educational objectives should not be retrospectively converted into a fully formed separate-Telangana demand. The historical meaning of an organisation must be anchored to its contemporary programme, not only its later legacy.

An analytical comparison helps. A newspaper can spread an argument quickly but may disappear after one issue; a library can sustain access to many publications over years; a public meeting allows collective deliberation but may be vulnerable to restrictions. The three forms reinforced each other. A movement with books but no readers remains narrow; readers without places to meet may remain isolated; meetings without durable records can struggle to build continuity. This is why libraries were important institutions in the social awakening of Hyderabad State.

## 4. Bhagya Reddy Varma and Adi-Hindu mobilisation

Bhagya Reddy Varma (1888–1939), born Maderi Bagaiah, is identified in Telangana SCERT as a prominent Dalit reformer. In 1906 he began Jagan Mitra Mandali, using popular performance to spread awareness and challenge caste humiliation. The Adi-Hindu framing asserted dignity and an alternative account of the place of oppressed communities in Indian society. The term should be explained as a reform movement's political vocabulary, not treated as an uncontested historical classification of ancient populations.

His work is especially important because it exposes the limits of a narrative focused only on elite language associations. For communities denied access to schools, public wells, temples or respectable employment, reform required changes in everyday social power. Educational initiatives, collective organisation and campaigns against the dedication of girls as devadasis or joginis were connected to the struggle against untouchability. SCERT notes efforts to obtain special educational funds from the Nizam's government. A balanced answer should examine both the movement's achievements and the institutional obstacles that limited access.

The gender dimension deserves its own analysis. A campaign against dedication practices addressed coercion and sexual exploitation, but meaningful emancipation also required schooling, livelihood alternatives, social protection and freedom from stigma. Repression of a harmful practice without support for affected women and girls could shift vulnerability rather than remove it. This is a general policy lesson drawn from the history; it is not a claim that all these measures were adopted in the period.

## 5. Different reform strategies and their social reach

Language associations, caste reform movements and agrarian mobilisation were neither identical nor wholly separate. A Telugu library could challenge linguistic marginalisation; an Adi-Hindu organisation could challenge caste exclusion; a peasant association could contest landlord power. A participant might be involved in more than one movement, yet the distribution of benefits could differ. For example, gaining permission to speak Telugu in an urban meeting would not necessarily eliminate forced labour in a village. Conversely, ending a coercive labour practice would not automatically make government education linguistically accessible.

The historian should therefore ask five questions of each organisation: Who founded it? What grievance did it identify? Which methods did it use? Who could participate? What evidence shows its actual reach? This method prevents a list of names and dates from being mistaken for a social history. It also allows comparison without ranking movements solely by the later fame of their leaders.

## 6. Libraries as archives and the limits of retrospective evidence

The Telangana Government Oriental Manuscripts Library and Research Institute traces its institutional roots to an 1891 collection and the Asafia Library, later associated with the State Central Library. Its current holdings span multiple languages and demonstrate that Hyderabad's knowledge institutions cannot be described as exclusively Telugu or Urdu. However, the present institute's collection size and administrative status are contemporary facts; they cannot be projected backwards as exact holdings in 1901 or 1935.

A reliable answer distinguishes institutional continuity from numerical continuity. The existence of an old library does not prove that every village had reading facilities; a modern archive's manuscript collection does not establish how many people could read those manuscripts a century ago. Use official institutional histories for names and chronology, and supplement them with contemporary catalogues, membership records and newspapers when making claims about audience or political influence.

## 7. A structured mains synthesis

**Background:** Hyderabad's multilingual society had uneven educational and social access. **Cultural mobilisation:** the 1901 Bhasha Nilayam and later reading-room networks expanded Telugu print culture and spaces for discussion. **Social reform:** Bhagya Reddy Varma's Adi-Hindu mobilisation challenged caste humiliation, promoted education and opposed coercive dedication practices. **Contrast:** language, caste and gender inequalities required different but potentially complementary strategies. **Limits:** libraries were not universally accessible, and legal or cultural recognition did not by itself change material power. **Conclusion:** Telangana's socio-cultural awakening was a layered transformation of public communication and social claims, not a single homogeneous movement.

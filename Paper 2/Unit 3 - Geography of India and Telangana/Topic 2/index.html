Syllabus map

Crops and agro-climatic regions; Green Revolution and recent agriculture; irrigation and command areas; major industries and localisation; industrial corridors; roads, rail and ports; trade/WTO and Indian Ocean location; population, demographic transition and dividend; urbanisation, megacities and Smart Cities.

1. Crop geography

Indian crop patterns reflect temperature, rainfall, soils and terrain, but also irrigation, procurement, prices, market access and technology. Rice and wheat illustrate how state support and water can extend cultivation beyond simple climatic regions; millets and pulses highlight dryland resilience and nutritional diversity.

2. Commercial and non-food crops

Cotton, sugarcane, jute, tea, coffee, rubber and spices each combine specialised ecological requirements with processing and market networks. Sugar mills tend to remain close to cane because cane is bulky and perishable, while cotton-textile manufacturing has become less tightly tied to cotton fields as logistics and technology improved.

3. Agro-climatic regionalisation

Agro-climatic regions combine climate, soil, water and physiography to guide crop and farming-system choices. They are planning tools rather than rigid natural boundaries. Irrigation expansion, climate variability, market changes and new technology can change the economically appropriate production system within the same region.

4. Green Revolution

The Green Revolution combined improved seed, irrigation, fertiliser, plant protection, credit, procurement and extension. It helped overcome national food constraints, but benefits were spatially uneven and intensive rice-wheat systems generated groundwater, nutrient and crop-diversity pressures in some areas.

5. Recent agricultural transition

Agriculture increasingly includes horticulture, livestock, fisheries, processing, cold chains, farmer organisations, digital services and value chains. Government Third Advance Estimates placed 2025–26 foodgrain production at about 376.56 million tonnes, but aggregate output does not by itself measure farm income, resilience or ecological sustainability.

6. Irrigation sources

Canals suit many plains and command areas; groundwater provides flexible local irrigation but can exceed recharge; tanks remain important in peninsular landscapes; large reservoirs provide multipurpose storage but create ecological and displacement trade-offs. Reliability, drainage and equitable delivery matter as much as gross irrigated area.

7. Command Area Development

A reservoir and canal create potential, not automatically productive irrigation. Field channels, land levelling, drainage, rotational supply and farmer participation determine whether water reaches farms efficiently. Poor drainage can produce waterlogging and salinity, showing why on-farm management is part of irrigation geography.

8. Industrial localisation

Industry location is determined by changing weights of raw materials, energy, water, labour, skills, markets, land, transport, agglomeration and policy. Heavy industries remain freight sensitive, knowledge industries emphasise talent and connectivity, and food processing is strongly influenced by perishability and supply chains.

9. Steel, cement and sugar

Integrated steel plants handle large bulk flows and historically favour mineral belts, ports and rail access. Cement remains strongly tied to limestone and energy, although grinding can move closer to markets. Sugar mills cluster near cane-growing regions because harvesting-to-crushing time affects recovery and economics.

10. Textiles, automobiles and IT

Textiles moved from classical cotton-port clusters toward dispersed powerloom and garment networks shaped by labour, markets and supply chains. Automobile production benefits from dense component ecosystems, highways and ports. IT requires little bulk material but depends heavily on skilled labour, digital connectivity, airports and urban-business ecosystems.

11. Food processing and value chains

Food processing connects agriculture to storage, standards, packaging, cold chains and consumer markets. Perishable produce often favours processing near farms, while packaged products can locate closer to large markets. Value addition can reduce losses but requires reliable electricity, logistics and farmer aggregation.

12. Industrial corridors

Industrial corridors combine freight infrastructure with planned industrial nodes, logistics, utilities and urban development. Agglomeration can lower costs and attract investment, but benefits are not automatic. Feeder connectivity, skills, environmental services and opportunities for local firms determine whether corridor-led growth is regionally inclusive.

13. Road networks

Roads provide route flexibility and last-mile access and are central to agricultural marketing and passenger mobility. A 2026 Government of India factsheet reported about 146,572 km of National Highways by March 2026. Network quality, road safety and connection to local roads matter more than length alone.

14. Railways and freight

Railways are particularly efficient for high-volume long-distance passenger and freight movement. Electrification and freight corridors can reduce bottlenecks and logistics costs; a 2026 government factsheet reported 99.6% rail electrification by July. Rail connectivity can reshape industrial location by connecting inland clusters to ports and markets.

15. Ports, trade and WTO

Ports connect industrial and agricultural hinterlands to global shipping networks. Major ports handled about 915.17 million tonnes in FY2025–26. Trade geography also reflects energy imports, global value chains, exchange rates and WTO rules, which should be connected to transport and strategic geography rather than memorised separately.

16. Indian Ocean location

India projects into the northern Indian Ocean near sea lanes linking West Asia, Africa, Europe and East Asia. Island territories add strategic depth near important passages. Ports, energy flows, undersea cables, maritime security and disaster response make this location both an economic opportunity and a strategic responsibility.

17. Population distribution

Fertile plains, water, jobs, transport and historical settlement create dense population belts, while mountains, deserts and remote forested areas are generally less dense. State averages conceal strong metropolitan-rural and regional contrasts. Distribution, density, growth and accessibility should not be used as interchangeable concepts.

18. Demographic transition and dividend

Declining mortality followed by declining fertility changes age structure and can increase the working-age share. A demographic dividend is conditional on nutrition, health, education, skills, productive jobs and women’s participation. Regional differences mean India can simultaneously face youth-employment pressure and emerging ageing.

19. Migration and metropolitan growth

Migration redistributes labour from regions with fewer opportunities toward agricultural, industrial and urban labour markets. Metropolitan expansion increasingly crosses municipal boundaries, producing peri-urban zones where housing, industry, wetlands, groundwater and commuting systems change faster than governance.

20. Smart and sustainable urbanisation

Smart Cities should not be reduced to sensors and command centres. Digital technology is valuable when it improves water, transport, safety and citizen access, but basic infrastructure, affordable housing, drainage, municipal finance, transit-oriented land use and accountable metropolitan governance remain foundational.

Original mains model answers

Green Revolution: gains and costs

The Green Revolution was a technological-institutional package rather than a seed-only change. Improved varieties combined with assured irrigation, fertiliser, credit, procurement and extension to raise wheat and rice output and reduce chronic food-import dependence. Benefits were strongest where irrigation and market institutions already existed, making regional outcomes uneven. Intensive monocropping and subsidised input use later contributed in some areas to groundwater depletion, nutrient imbalance, residue problems and reduced crop diversity. The next agricultural transition should preserve productivity while improving water and nutrient efficiency, diversifying toward suitable pulses, millets, horticulture and allied activities, and strengthening storage, processing and markets.

Industrial localisation

Industrial geography changes because each sector weights location factors differently. Steel and cement remain bulk-material and freight sensitive; sugar depends on nearby cane because the crop is perishable; textiles have become more mobile as modern conditioning and transport reduce older constraints; automobiles depend heavily on supplier clusters, skills, highways and ports; IT prioritises skilled labour, universities, digital networks and urban amenities; and food processing responds to perishability, cold chains and market access. Falling transport costs do not eliminate geography—they shift the relative importance of different spatial advantages.

Transport and development

Transport infrastructure is both an outcome and a cause of economic geography. Roads link farms and smaller towns to markets; railways handle long-distance high-volume movement; ports connect hinterlands to international routes; and corridors integrate these networks with industry. Lower time and logistics costs enlarge labour and consumer markets, allowing firms to specialise and cluster. Yet a new expressway can bypass local economies or fragment habitats, and corridors can concentrate gains in already-advantaged nodes. Development effects therefore depend on feeder roads, public transport, logistics terminals, local skills, environmental safeguards and land-use planning.

Demographic dividend

A large working-age population creates only a potential demographic dividend. Young people need nutrition, health, education and market-relevant skills; the economy needs enough productive employment; women need genuine access to paid work; and growing cities need affordable housing and transport. India’s age structure is regionally uneven, so labour mobility can connect younger states with ageing or labour-short regions. Without jobs, the same age structure can generate underemployment rather than a dividend. Population policy must therefore shift from simple fertility concerns toward capabilities, migration, labour productivity and eventual ageing.

Exam caution

Do not equate record production with farmer welfare, irrigation efficiency with basin-wide water savings, infrastructure length with accessibility, or a young population with an automatic demographic dividend. These distinctions are the analytical core of the topic.

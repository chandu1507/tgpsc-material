## 1. Reading the syllabus as an interconnected social history

The syllabus names Vetti, Jogini and Devadasi systems, the girl child, fluorosis, child labour, migrant labour, child marriage and social movements in Telangana. These are not eight disconnected facts. They ask how economic dependence, caste authority, gendered control, environmental exposure and unequal access to public institutions produce vulnerability; and how collective action alters the distribution of voice and resources. A high-scoring answer should identify the specific mechanism rather than use poverty as a universal explanation. Poverty may make a family susceptible to coercion, but an institution that permits caste-based forced service requires a theory of social power; excessive fluoride in groundwater requires hydrogeological investigation; a child marriage requires a gender and legal-capacity analysis.

A useful framework has five stages: **historical institution → present-day mechanism → affected capabilities → applicable law and delivery institution → testable outcome**. For example, Vetti historically meant coerced services to local powerholders; the present-day warning sign may be an advance-linked work obligation that a worker cannot freely exit; the lost capability is freedom of labour; Article 23 and labour protections supply legal routes; a valid outcome is independently verified release and sustained livelihood, not merely registration of a complaint. The same structure can be used to distinguish an effective social movement from a temporary protest: durable organisation must change incentives, institutions or the distribution of rights.

## 2. Vetti, agrarian hierarchy and the meaning of freedom of labour

### 2.1 How coercion was embedded in the village economy

Under parts of the Hyderabad State's agrarian system, local landholders and intermediary authorities could demand unpaid or underpaid labour, transport, domestic service and ceremonial work. The local vocabulary of Vetti is historically important because the obligation was not always framed as a market contract. A household might be compelled to perform work because of inherited service status, caste hierarchy, debt, dependence on a landlord for tenancy or fear of eviction. Such relationships blurred the distinction between economic dependence and overt compulsion. Not every unpaid family activity is Vetti, and historical obligations differed across villages; a serious answer avoids the claim that every landlord exercised identical powers.

The economic consequences were cumulative. Days spent on compulsory service reduced the time available to cultivate one's own plot, learn a skill or seek wages elsewhere. Coercion could therefore reproduce indebtedness: the worker lost earning capacity and then became more dependent on credit or grain advances. The political consequences were equally serious because a household unable to refuse service had little practical ability to complain about rent, land records or police abuse. The mechanism illustrates the difference between **formal liberty** (a law says labour is free) and **substantive liberty** (a worker can actually refuse without losing food, housing or safety).

### 2.2 Resistance and legal change are separate historical stages

Telangana's peasant mobilisation of 1946–51 opposed coercive labour, arbitrary levies, eviction and landlord domination. It is analytically wrong to treat the 1948 integration of Hyderabad, the agrarian struggle and the 1976 bonded-labour legislation as a single event. Political integration changed sovereignty; agrarian organisation challenged village power; later law established enforceable statutory rights. Their effects were related but neither immediate nor identical. The movement also combined different constituencies and strategies, so its achievements and violence require evidence-sensitive assessment rather than celebration by default.

Article 23 of the Constitution prohibits traffic in human beings, begar and other similar forms of forced labour. The Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act, 1976 addresses debt-linked and other bonded labour relations, including extinguishment of bonded debt and release from obligations. Section 2(b), Explanation, of the Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act, 1976 expressly names **Vetti** among customary forms of forced labour for which an agreement is ordinarily presumed. Section 4 abolishes the bonded labour system, and section 5 renders bonded-service customs and agreements void. This textual recognition does not mean that every historically described Vetti episode automatically establishes all facts of an individual bonded-labour case; assess coercion, obligations, remuneration and loss of freedom using the statutory criteria. A district officer must identify the actual restriction on movement, wage withholding, threats, advances, tenancy dependence and the worker's ability to leave. Release without rehabilitation, land access, wages and a safe complaint channel may leave the person vulnerable to the same employer or a different recruiter.

### 2.3 Administrative case: brick kiln workers

Suppose a family working at a kiln near Hyderabad received a cash advance from a recruiter and is told it cannot leave until an opaque account is settled. Children are helping to meet a family output target. A competent response separates urgent protection from later adjudication: confidentially interview adults and children away from the recruiter; record recruitment and wage evidence; assess bonded labour and child labour independently; coordinate labour, police, child protection and district administration; and arrange safe transport, identity documents, school re-entry and follow-up. The critical question is whether consent was meaningful when refusal meant debt threats or confinement. Counting rescue operations alone is not a measure of durable freedom.

## 3. Jogini and Devadasi: regional forms, caste-gender domination and law

### 3.1 Distinguish practices without erasing survivors

Jogini is a regional form of ritual dedication associated in some localities with caste and gender inequality. Devadasi is a broader historical term whose social meanings varied by period and place; not every historical performer or temple-linked artist had the same experience. A defensible textbook account therefore distinguishes a coercive dedication institution from the full diversity of South Indian performance history. The issue for contemporary public policy is not to stereotype a community or religion. It is whether girls and women are denied autonomy, schooling, safety, property and freedom to marry because an obligation is imposed in the name of custom.

Social mechanisms can include vows made during illness, local ritual authority, caste pressure, family insecurity and dependence on patrons. These mechanisms may reinforce each other without being present in every case. A girl cannot be treated as having freely chosen a life-defining status because an adult relative consented or a community approved. The gendered harm may persist after the public ceremony stops: social stigma, exclusion from marriage, poor access to healthcare and income insecurity can continue. An effective programme must therefore be survivor-centred, confidential and long-term, not a one-day rescue photograph.

### 3.2 Read the 1988 Act precisely

The original Andhra Pradesh Devadasis (Prohibition of Dedication) Act, 1988, adapted for Telangana in 2016, is Act No. 10 of 1988. Its definition expressly includes Jogini among local labels. Section 3 declares dedication unlawful and void regardless of whether the woman was said to consent; the legislation also protects marriage validity and children from stigma arising solely from the dedication. The Act places enforcement responsibilities on designated officers, including the Collector. A strong mains answer names the legislation and explains its logic: **abolish the imposed status, do not punish the dedicated woman, and restore civil capacity and dignity**. The statutory text is available through the official India Code PDF; the particular rules and enforcement data must be checked separately.

A prohibition-only strategy may fail if survivors lack land, income, counselling and safe housing. Community education is necessary, but it should not become public identification of survivors. Police action against organisers of dedication and welfare support for affected women serve different purposes and must be coordinated. One can measure effectiveness through school retention among at-risk girls, survivors' access to bank accounts and housing, confidential complaints resolved, and absence of re-dedication—not through a claim that a village is 'free of the practice' without a reliable method of verification.

### 3.3 Administrative case: threatened dedication

A teacher hears that a 14-year-old may be dedicated during a village festival. The appropriate response is to protect the child first: discreetly contact child-protection authorities, establish her immediate safety and schooling, document evidence without exposing her publicly, and coordinate with police and the revenue authorities empowered under the relevant statute. A health or counselling referral should not be conditional on testimony. The family may need livelihood assistance, but economic support is not a substitute for enforcement. The ethical test is whether the child gains sustained choice and safety, not whether an official ceremony was prevented in front of cameras.

## 4. Girl child: discrimination across the life course

### 4.1 Why birth, schooling and work must be analysed together

Disadvantage to the girl child is not a single indicator. It can begin with prenatal sex preference, unequal nutrition and health spending, and continue through unpaid household care, unsafe travel, early withdrawal from school and marriage before adulthood. These pathways interact: a girl who spends hours collecting water or caring for younger siblings may attend school nominally yet lose learning time; a family concerned about harassment may restrict her mobility rather than demand safer transport. Consequently, enrollment rates alone do not establish gender equality. Retention, learning, menstrual hygiene, safe commuting and adolescent decision-making matter.

The child-rights framework includes Articles 14, 15, 21 and 21A of the Constitution, the Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009, the Protection of Children from Sexual Offences Act, 2012, and the Prohibition of Child Marriage Act, 2006. These laws address different risks; they cannot be replaced by a generic reference to 'women's empowerment'. District administrations must align schools, health services, child protection, police and local bodies. Adolescent girls should be consulted about transport, toilets, harassment and access to digital learning without making participation unsafe.

### 4.2 Child marriage is not automatically void in every case

The Prohibition of Child Marriage Act, 2006 generally treats child marriage as voidable at the option of the contracting party who was a child, subject to statutory conditions; specified circumstances can make marriages void. The distinction is examination-relevant. A child marriage prohibition officer may prevent an impending marriage, but legal assistance, shelter, protection from retaliation and continuity of education are required if the child refuses family pressure. A crude target such as 'number of marriages stopped' can incentivise concealment. Better indicators include re-enrolment, access to counselling and sustained safety after intervention.

A useful Telangana example is a seasonal-migration household that fears an adolescent girl will be left without supervision while adults travel for work. The risk is not solved by moral lectures. Safe hostels where appropriate, local schooling, reliable public transport, income support and trusted complaint routes reduce the pressure to arrange early marriage. Policy must address both social norms and material constraints.

## 5. Fluorosis in Nalgonda: environmental injustice and public-health governance

### 5.1 Understand the exposure pathway

Fluorosis results from prolonged excessive fluoride intake, frequently through drinking water. Groundwater fluoride varies with rock chemistry, residence time, pH and local hydrogeology; a district label does not prove that every borewell is unsafe. Dental fluorosis and skeletal fluorosis have different manifestations and severity. The correct analytical chain is **source chemistry → exposure duration and quantity → health effects → household costs → service response**. A government water-supply scheme is not evidence of exposure elimination unless the water actually consumed by households is tested and reliably delivered.

The Directorate General of Health Services identifies a desirable drinking-water fluoride level of 1.0 mg/L in the 2014 programme guidance; the widely used BIS permissible limit in the absence of an alternative source is 1.5 mg/L. These thresholds must not be confused. The National Programme for Prevention and Control of Fluorosis (NPPCF) supports surveillance, diagnostic capacity, community education, treatment and rehabilitation. Telangana's Nalgonda district administration reports establishing its District Fluoride Monitoring Centre on 14 November 2013 to coordinate prevention and response. Its historical risk figures should not be recycled as the current prevalence without new testing.

### 5.2 Why the burden is socially unequal

A low-income household may depend on a nearby borewell because piped supply is intermittent, purchased water is costly and carrying water consumes time. People with chronic skeletal disease may lose wages and require travel for treatment, creating an additional poverty trap. Women and girls may bear disproportionate collection and caregiving burdens. This is why fluorosis belongs in social issues as well as geography: a naturally occurring chemical hazard becomes a distributive injustice when safe alternatives are unequal.

The 'Nalgonda technique' of chemical defluoridation is a historical engineering approach; it should not be described as a universally optimal household solution. Its effectiveness depends on correct dosing, sludge management, operation and monitoring. Sustainable solutions require source substitution where feasible, tested piped supply, transparent public water-quality data and clinical follow-up. In mains answers, distinguish **source-level prevention** from **individual treatment**: medical care cannot undo continued consumption of unsafe water.

### 5.3 Administrative case: apparently safe village

A village has a new tap-water network, but residents continue to use an old borewell during outages. A district officer should sample actual drinking points across seasons, disclose results in accessible language, ensure alternative safe supply, track downtime, and link suspected cases to health services. A report that 'all habitations are covered' is a service-infrastructure measure, not proof that all people receive safe water throughout the year. Equity requires identifying households without functional connections and people with disabilities who cannot collect water from distant standposts.

## 6. Child labour: schooling, household debt and supply chains

### 6.1 What law prohibits and what vulnerability looks like

The Child and Adolescent Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Act, 1986, as amended, generally prohibits employment of children below 14 in occupations and processes, subject to limited statutory exceptions, and prohibits adolescents aged 14–18 from hazardous occupations and processes. The existence of exceptions is not a licence to employ children in dangerous or school-disrupting work. Constitutional Article 24, the Right to Education Act, juvenile-justice and child-protection frameworks operate alongside labour enforcement. The exact age, activity, hazard and effect on schooling must be established rather than inferred from appearance.

Telangana risk settings may include small workshops, construction subcontracting, informal retail, household work and seasonal agricultural operations. It is unsafe to assume a particular sector has a fixed prevalence without a current survey. Family debt and migration can increase risk, but employer demand for cheap and compliant labour is equally important. Some children appear in workplaces after school because care arrangements are unavailable; others are engaged in sustained economic production. Investigators must distinguish these situations while prioritising protection and education.

### 6.2 Why rescue and rehabilitation are different

An enforcement drive can remove children from a workplace yet leave the family without wages, documents or school access. Without follow-up, the child may return to another employer. Effective rehabilitation requires age-appropriate education or bridge learning, household livelihood assessment, counselling, coordination across source and destination districts, and inspection of the supply chain. For adolescents legally allowed to perform non-hazardous work under applicable law, safe conditions and schooling or skills pathways remain critical. Public dashboards should track re-entry to education and sustained attendance, not only raids.

A district magistrate confronting a subcontracted workshop must ask who controls wages, who recruited the children, whether identity documents are withheld and whether parents can be contacted safely. Employer responsibility should not disappear merely because the visible workshop is one tier below a larger purchaser. For mains, present this as an accountability chain: **contractor → principal business → labour inspectorate → child welfare services → school**.

## 7. Migrant labour: mobility is a right, exploitation is not

### 7.1 Migration patterns and layered vulnerability

Migration may be seasonal, circular or long-term, and can involve movement within Telangana, from Telangana to other states or into Hyderabad from elsewhere. Workers may move voluntarily for better earnings; migration itself must not be described as a social evil. The vulnerability arises when recruiters conceal terms, wages are withheld, housing is unsafe, childcare is unavailable, or workers cannot access health care and ration benefits at destination. Linguistic difference, lack of documents and fear of dismissal make complaints difficult. Women migrants can face particular risks in domestic work and informal services.

The Inter-State Migrant Workmen (Regulation of Employment and Conditions of Service) Act, 1979 historically provided registration and contractor duties, including wage, journey and displacement protections under its specified conditions. Its relationship to the newer labour-code framework must be checked against the applicable commencement and transition notifications before making a current legal claim. Do not present an unverified code-commencement date as settled. For an administrative answer, name the legal framework but focus on verified worker protections: wage records, recruitment transparency, safe accommodation, accessible grievance systems and portability of entitlements.

### 7.2 Destination-city governance

Hyderabad's construction, logistics and service sectors can create demand for workers while imposing high housing and commuting costs. Municipal authorities may regard migrant settlements as temporary encroachments, whereas workers see them as the only affordable route to employment. A rights-based approach combines safe rental accommodation, sanitation, childcare, school admission, public-health outreach and transparent occupational safety inspection. The goal is not to prevent mobility but to make its risks manageable and benefits equitable.

Consider a group of workers whose children have missed three school terms because the family moves between brick kilns. A response limited to a one-time school admission fails. Source and destination education departments should coordinate portable records, bridge classes and continuity plans. Worker organisations and self-help groups can help communicate rights, but they cannot replace enforceable employer obligations. A high-quality answer distinguishes **social protection portability**, **labour-law enforcement** and **urban planning** as three separate but complementary policy tracks.

## 8. Telangana social movements: a comparative map rather than a single story

### 8.1 Agrarian and anti-Vetti mobilisation

The 1940s peasant struggle was rooted in land relations, coercive service, tenancy and the power of local landlords. Sanghams and political organisations offered collective bargaining and protection where individual resistance was dangerous. The movement's importance is not exhausted by military events: it shifted public discussion toward land, dignity and village power. However, claims about village counts, casualties or land redistribution must be tied to dated archival or scholarly sources; this supplement deliberately avoids unverified totals. Different groups experienced the movement differently, and later debates about violence and political strategy remain part of historical interpretation.

### 8.2 Women's anti-arrack organising and the politics of everyday life

The early-1990s anti-arrack movement in undivided Andhra Pradesh was prominently associated with rural women and literacy mobilisation in Nellore, while anti-liquor organising also had earlier and distinct strands in northern Telangana. These histories should not be collapsed into a claim that the entire movement began in Telangana. Women's protests made domestic violence, household spending and state excise revenue matters of public policy rather than 'private family problems'. Campaigns used petitions, village meetings, public demonstrations and pressure on licensing. A critical answer recognises both the agency of women and the limitations of prohibition when enforcement, illicit supply and livelihood transition are ignored.

The movement demonstrates a causal chain from literacy and discussion to collective organisation and policy change. Literacy was not simply the ability to read; it created meeting spaces where women compared experiences and named shared grievances. But not every participant held the same political position, and the political consequences of prohibition varied. For a mains answer, separate **movement origins**, **forms of mobilisation**, **government response** and **unintended consequences**.

### 8.3 Dalit dignity, caste discrimination and assertion

Dalit mobilisation in Telangana cannot be reduced to one organisation or election. It includes resistance to humiliating occupations, campaigns for access to land and education, legal action against atrocities, and demands for equitable distribution within reservation categories. Caste discrimination operates through social exclusion, economic opportunity and public authority. A movement may therefore seek recognition, representation, redistribution or protection, sometimes simultaneously. Avoid treating Scheduled Castes as socially uniform: internal differences in occupational history, region and access to state benefits shape political claims.

The legal context includes constitutional equality guarantees, abolition of untouchability under Article 17, and the Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989. Legal remedies matter, but a prosecution statistic alone does not establish social equality. A serious assessment considers whether victims can report without retaliation, whether schools and workplaces are inclusive, and whether public institutions act impartially. A social-movement answer should explain the distinction between a demand for legal enforcement and a demand to redesign resource distribution.

### 8.4 Adivasi land, forest and self-governance claims

Adivasi resistance in Telangana concerns alienation of land, forest access, recognition of customary livelihoods and control over local decisions. The slogan **Jal, Jungle, Zamin** expresses a claim to water, forests and land, but it is not itself a legal title document. In Scheduled Areas, the Fifth Schedule, relevant land-transfer protections, PESA and the Forest Rights Act, 2006 must be analysed in their respective jurisdictions. PESA's provisions concern Scheduled Areas; they cannot be assumed to apply identically to every Telangana village. Forest rights recognition and land-transfer enforcement require documentary and community processes, not merely symbolic recognition.

An administrator reviewing a disputed land transfer should establish whether the parcel is within a Scheduled Area, the applicable regulation, the parties' status, the transaction history and the relevant gram sabha process. Adivasi women and landless households should not be excluded from consultation by relying only on prominent male representatives. Social movements can improve visibility and oversight, but outcomes should be measured in recognised rights, restored possession and functioning local institutions.

### 8.5 Telangana statehood and cultural mobilisation

The Telangana statehood movement connected employment, irrigation, budget allocation and regional identity with student, employee, cultural and political mobilisation. Its historical phases must be distinguished: the 1969 agitation, later civil-society discussions and the mass mobilisation culminating in state formation in 2014 were not one uninterrupted campaign with identical leadership. Cultural performances, local-language expression and historical remembrance made complex distributional grievances accessible to broader audiences. The movement also included disagreement over strategy, party alliances and the future of Hyderabad; these should be acknowledged rather than erased.

Statehood is a constitutional-political outcome, not proof that all pre-existing social inequalities were solved. A balanced evaluation asks whether regional representation improved and whether women, Dalits, Adivasis, migrant workers and rural households experienced substantive gains. It is possible to recognise the legitimacy of regional identity while still evaluating post-formation public policy through measurable service delivery and social inclusion.

### 8.6 Women's self-help groups as collective institutions

Self-help groups and their village, mandal and district federations are not identical to protest movements, but they can build bargaining power, savings, credit access and collective voice. Telangana's Society for Elimination of Rural Poverty (SERP) describes community-based organisations organised from SHGs through village and higher federations. Such institutions may enable livelihood enterprise and social action, including responses to domestic violence or exclusion. However, loan disbursement does not automatically imply empowerment: debt stress, unequal decision-making and limited market access can undermine gains. A credible assessment examines who controls earnings, whether members can refuse loans and whether poorer women participate meaningfully.

## 10. Quick revision and exam-writing blueprint

**Ten distinctions to retain:** Vetti is not merely low pay; Jogini is expressly included in the statutory Devadasi definition; dedication is not legitimised by alleged consent; child marriage is generally voidable rather than automatically void; child labour and adolescent hazardous work have different legal tests; migrant mobility is not itself trafficking; groundwater coverage is not the same as safe consumption; the anti-arrack movement had geographically distinct strands; self-help groups are not always protest movements; state formation is not equivalent to eradication of social inequality.

For a 200–250-word mains answer, use six headed blocks: **Context (25–30 words); Historical or structural causes (45); Mechanism and consequences (45); Law and institutional response (45); Critical limitations (35); Way forward/conclusion (25)**. Include a named Telangana case and one precise legal distinction. Avoid invented statistics and unsupported official-PYQ labels.

# 30 original explained practice MCQs

## 1. Which constitutional provision directly prohibits begar and comparable forced labour?
A. Article 23
B. Article 356
C. Article 30
D. Article 19(1)(a)
**Answer: A.** Article 23 prohibits forced labour; Article 24 separately addresses child employment in factories, mines and hazardous employment.

## 2. What is the central feature distinguishing Vetti from merely low-paid work?
A. Payment by the day
B. Agricultural setting
C. Absence of meaningful freedom to refuse imposed service
D. Seasonal timing
**Answer: C.** Vetti refers to coerced service embedded in local social power; not all low wages meet the same test.

## 3. Which Telangana historical episode prominently challenged forced agrarian service?
A. Quit India movement of 1942 alone
B. The 1956 linguistic reorganisation alone
C. The 2014 state election alone
D. Telangana peasant mobilisation of 1946–51
**Answer: D.** The peasant struggle challenged forced service, landlord levies and tenancy coercion, while later legal reforms are distinct events.

## 4. Which statute provides a specific legal framework for abolishing bonded labour?
A. Companies Act, 2013
B. Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act, 1976
C. Indian Forest Act, 1927
D. Official Languages Act, 1963
**Answer: B.** The Act abolishes bonded labour (section 4) and expressly names Vetti in the explanation to section 2(b). Other options do not provide that framework; the statutory mention does not establish facts of any individual case.

## 5. Why is rehabilitation needed after release from coercive labour?
A. Dependence and threats can reproduce coercion
B. Release cancels all poverty automatically
C. Workers must return to recruiters
D. Rehabilitation is only a police duty
**Answer: A.** Safe livelihoods, wages and housing are needed to make legal freedom sustainable.

## 6. Which regional practice is explicitly included in the 1988 Devadasis prohibition statute?
A. PESA
B. Panchayat Samiti
C. Jogini
D. Gram Sabha
**Answer: C.** The official Telangana-adapted text lists Jogini among labels covered by the definition.

## 7. What is the legal status of dedication under section 3 of the 1988 Act?
A. Unlawful and void even when consent is alleged
B. Valid with parental permission
C. Only prohibited in cities
D. Valid after adulthood
**Answer: A.** Section 3 rejects dedication regardless of purported consent and protects the woman’s legal status.

## 8. Which approach best protects a survivor of ritual dedication?
A. Confidential protection, legal action and sustained livelihood support
B. Punishment of the dedicated woman
C. A ceremony without follow-up
D. Public identification at a village meeting
**Answer: A.** Survivor autonomy and safety are the outcomes; public exposure can create further harm.

## 9. Why should Devadasi history not be treated as one uniform institution?
A. Only one region used the term
B. All participants had identical legal status
C. Historical roles varied by place and period
D. No historical performance tradition existed
**Answer: C.** A rigorous account distinguishes historical performance traditions from contemporary coercive dedication.

## 10. Which official document establishes the adaptation of the 1988 Act for Telangana?
A. The 1973 Six-Point Formula
B. The 2009 December statement
C. G.O.Ms.No.14 dated 28 April 2016
D. G.O.610 of 1985
**Answer: C.** The Telangana-adapted India Code text identifies the 2016 adaptation under the Reorganisation Act.

## 11. Which condition is most closely associated with prolonged excessive fluoride exposure?
A. Fluorosis
B. Malaria
C. Tuberculosis
D. Goitre from iodine deficiency
**Answer: A.** Fluorosis affects teeth and bones; laboratory water testing and exposure history are central.

## 12. Which Telangana district is a historically documented fluorosis case?
A. All districts uniformly
B. Nalgonda
C. Only Medchal
D. Only Hyderabad
**Answer: B.** Nalgonda district government documents groundwater fluoride and its monitoring centre; not every borewell is equally affected.

## 13. What was established in Nalgonda on 14 November 2013?
A. Hyderabad Metropolitan Development Authority
B. District Fluoride Monitoring Centre
C. National Green Tribunal
D. State Election Commission
**Answer: B.** The district government dates its coordination centre to 14 November 2013.

## 14. What does the DGHS NPPCF guidance identify as the desirable fluoride level?
A. 0.001 mg/L
B. 1.0 mg/L
C. 10 mg/L
D. 50 mg/L
**Answer: B.** DGHS describes 1.0 mg/L as desirable; distinguish it from the BIS permissible value under specified conditions.

## 15. What is the strongest evidence that a piped-water scheme reduces fluorosis exposure?
A. Repeated testing of water actually consumed
B. Budget allocation alone
C. Ribbon-cutting photographs

## Question: Vetti was more than an unpaid labour practice. Analyse.

### Introduction
Vetti in historical Telangana signifies compelled or unpaid service embedded in unequal agrarian and caste relations. It was a system of domination because workers often lacked meaningful freedom to refuse.

### Structural causes
Land dependence, credit obligations, hereditary service expectations and local power of landlords could reinforce one another. Loss of tenancy or housing was a threat that converted customary duty into coerced labour.

### Social consequences
Vetti curtailed bargaining power, dignity and opportunities for schooling and alternative work. It also maintained the wider landlord structure by providing cheap services beyond ordinary agriculture.

### Resistance and law
The 1946–51 Telangana agrarian struggle challenged forced labour alongside levies and evictions. Article 23 prohibits forced labour. The explanation to section 2(b) of the Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act, 1976 expressly names Vetti among customary forced-labour forms; section 4 abolishes the bonded-labour system. That express naming is not a shortcut to proving the facts of an individual case.

### Critical insight
The examiner should distinguish involuntary service from ordinary low wages and show how caste rank, tenancy and credit generated a coercive bargaining environment. An anti-Vetti answer must specify what changed after agrarian resistance and what present-day accountability mechanisms prevent re-emergence.

### Conclusion
Durable eradication requires livelihood independence, enforcement, safe complaints and rehabilitation. Ending a formal obligation without changing dependency may merely conceal coercion.

### Implementation and outcome test
**Implementation test:** A field inquiry should separately document worker testimony, advance accounts, threatened eviction and access to independent wages. Release orders, housing and a safe complaint route matter more than raids alone.

## Question: Explain the factors sustaining Jogini dedication and critically evaluate its prohibition.

### Background
Jogini is a regional dedication practice involving girls or women. Its significance for contemporary policy lies in the intersection of ritual pressure, caste hierarchy, gender inequality and inadequate economic choice.

### Root causes
Household poverty, limited schooling, vows linked to illness and unequal community norms can make refusal difficult. These are structural explanations, not cultural justifications.

### Law
The Telangana Devadasis (Prohibition of Dedication) Act, 1988 treats dedication as unlawful and expressly includes Jogini within its statutory definition. Different local labels cannot create a lawful exemption.

### Challenges
Survivors may experience stigma, health burdens and limited independent income after prohibition. Criminalisation without trusted counselling, privacy, education and livelihood rehabilitation risks pushing practices into invisibility.

### Critical insight
The useful analytical contrast is between the ceremony's social label and the enforceable legal definition: the 1988 Telangana Act includes Jogini despite regional naming differences. Success must be measured through individual choice, school completion, rehabilitation and freedom from stigma.

### Way forward and conclusion
Combine survivor-led prevention, legal enforcement, schooling, confidential healthcare and secure economic assets. The success indicator is actual freedom, not merely the number of ceremonies interrupted.

### Implementation and outcome test
**Implementation test:** Monitor school attendance, confidential survivor follow-up and independent access to livelihood assets. Enforcement must target those who organise dedication, not the dedicated woman. Community education should avoid naming survivors publicly. The core outcome is sustained personal autonomy rather than the number of ceremonies stopped in an official campaign.

## Question: Fluorosis in Telangana demonstrates the link between geography and social inequality. Discuss.

### Introduction
Fluorosis arises from prolonged excessive fluoride exposure, typically through drinking water. The Nalgonda region illustrates how groundwater chemistry intersects with public health.

### Geographic mechanism
Fluoride-bearing rocks, groundwater residence time and geochemical conditions may elevate dissolved fluoride. Contamination varies by source; borewell water cannot be assumed uniformly unsafe or safe.

### Human consequences
Dental fluorosis affects enamel; skeletal fluorosis can damage bones and joints. Where households lack alternatives, chronic exposure may cause disability, schooling losses and reduced earning capacity.

### Measures
A safe response combines periodic testing, alternative low-fluoride supply, properly operated defluoridation, school screening and clinical referral. Piped-water installation does not prove that safe water consistently reaches every home.

### Critical insight
A policy answer must distinguish geological risk mapping from health surveillance. Safe drinking-water access is measured at the tap over time; treatment-plant construction totals cannot substitute for water-quality compliance, independent testing and follow-up of people already affected.

### Conclusion
Fluorosis is preventable when geoscience, public-health follow-up and accountable local water service function together; otherwise natural groundwater risk becomes an inequitable social burden.

### Implementation and outcome test
**Implementation test:** Compare laboratory results at the actual household drinking point with delivery reliability, not only source-level sampling. Track whether households return to unsafe borewells during supply outages and whether diagnosed patients receive rehabilitation. This distinguishes nominal infrastructure coverage from reduced exposure and improved capabilities.

## Question: Child marriage persists despite legislation. Examine the Telangana policy response.

### Introduction
Child marriage restricts agency, schooling and adolescent health. Its persistence is linked to gender inequality and uneven educational and economic opportunity.

### Root causes
Household insecurity, dowry expectations, family-honour norms, safety concerns and weak secondary-school access can push families toward early marriage.

### Law and safeguards
Under the Prohibition of Child Marriage Act, 2006, a child is a female below 18 or male below 21. Section 3 generally makes the marriage voidable at the option of the child party; specified other circumstances can make a marriage void. Child Marriage Prohibition Officers support prevention.

### Policy gaps
Cancellation alone may expose a girl to retaliation or school dropout if safe accommodation, income and continuing education are missing. The elementary RTE framework covers ages 6–14, leaving a vital secondary-school retention challenge.

### Critical insight
A distinctive implementation recommendation is to identify girls at risk of transition-related dropout before a wedding is arranged. Tracking secondary-school completion, safe travel, household support and repeat-risk cases better measures prevention than a simple count of cancelled marriages.

### Conclusion
Focus on early detection, safe transport and residential schooling, confidential case support, parental engagement and long-term education outcomes rather than only prosecutions.

### Implementation and outcome test
**Implementation test:** Protection officers should document the child’s wishes, immediate safety, continued schooling and any retaliation risk. Administrative targets based solely on prevented ceremonies can conceal coercion. Distinguish voidable and specified void marriages.

## Question: Discuss why migrant labour requires portable social protection in Telangana.

### Introduction
Hyderabad attracts workers while rural households may undertake seasonal and circular migration. Migrants contribute to growth but face service gaps when benefits remain tied to a fixed location.

### Causes and patterns
Construction, services, manufacturing and brick kilns attract contractors and migrant workers. Intra-state migration differs from inter-state migration; not every movement is distress-driven.

### Vulnerabilities
Informal housing, delayed pay, workplace injury, language barriers, lack of creches and interruptions to children’s schooling can undermine mobility gains.

### Institutional measures
Ration portability is useful, but it cannot by itself assure health access, labour standards or school transfer. Wage records, safety inspections, bilingual grievance systems and affordable rental housing must travel with workers.

### Critical insight
Migrant workers are not a homogeneous category. Age, gender, duration, occupation, home-state documentation and contractor arrangements alter vulnerability. Strong administration connects wage enforcement and social-security portability to schools, childcare and safe rental accommodation at actual worksites.

### Conclusion
Public policy should protect the right to move safely and voluntarily, measuring wages received, injury risks, school continuity and benefits actually accessed.

### Implementation and outcome test
**Implementation test:** A destination city should coordinate wage complaints, safe housing, ration portability, school admission and health outreach. Contracting chains must be mapped before responsibility is assigned. Worker mobility can improve income; policy should remove coercion and exclusion rather than treating all migration as trafficking.

## Question: Explain the relationship between social movements and structural change in Telangana.

### Introduction
Social movements convert persistent private grievances into collective claims for land, dignity, political voice and recognition.

### Historical background
Andhra Mahasabha networks and village mobilisation addressed language and agrarian grievances. The 1946–51 Telangana peasant struggle challenged coercive land relations, levies and Vetti.

### Different strands
Tribal struggles associated with Ramji Gond and Komaram Bheem occurred in distinct historical contexts. The 1969 agitation and subsequent regional mobilisation culminating in statehood in 2014 concerned safeguards, resources and self-government.

### Critical assessment
Movements can secure legislation or political reorganisation without immediately eliminating caste exclusion, agrarian inequality or gender-based exploitation. Social mobilisation and public administration must therefore be evaluated separately.

### Critical insight
Avoid claiming that political statehood automatically solved rural social inequality. An evidence-led assessment separates movement objectives, institutional concessions and longer-term outcomes for tenants, tribal communities, women and landless workers, using named organisations and distinct historical phases.

### Conclusion
The lasting measure of social movement success is not mobilisation alone but enforceable access to land, welfare, schooling, safe work, dignity and democratic participation.

### Implementation and outcome test
**Implementation test:** Compare movement demands with verifiable changes in land access, women’s safety, education and local decision-making. Recognise disagreements between organisations and distinguish symbolic gains from service outcomes. Telangana statehood and anti-arrack histories cannot be collapsed into one undifferentiated social-movement narrative. An outcome audit must distinguish immediate announcements from sustained enforcement, accessible grievance channels, representation of excluded groups, and independently measurable improvements in livelihoods and safety.

## Exact syllabus map
(a) Social issues in Telangana: Vetti; Jogini and Devadasi system; girl child; fluorosis; child labour; migrant labour; child marriages. (b) Social movements in Telangana.

## Topic summary
The subject links caste, class, gender, water quality, childhood vulnerabilities and labour mobility to Telangana social reform and collective action. The organising argument is that legal prohibitions are necessary but insufficient where material dependence and implementation gaps persist.

## Exam-important distinctions
- **Vetti vs bonded labour:** historical customary coerced service versus a particular statutory regime under the 1976 Act.
- **Jogini vs Devadasi:** sociologically distinguish local dedication from the historically varied umbrella term; the Telangana 1988 statute explicitly includes Jogini.
- **Fluorosis:** high-fluoride chronic exposure is not the same as all groundwater contamination; safe-source tests matter.
- **Child marriage:** often voidable at the option of the child party, not universally void automatically.
- **Migrant labour:** mobility differs from trafficking and exploitation; portability of rights is the institutional challenge.

## Vetti: forced labour and feudal domination

### 1. Meaning and historical context
Vetti refers to compelled or unpaid labour imposed through unequal power, historically associated with landlord dominance, hereditary social status, indebtedness and administrative coercion. It should not be reduced to ordinary low wages: the central issue is the worker’s lack of effective freedom to refuse service, negotiate the terms of labour or leave. Under the Hyderabad State’s agrarian order, village-level authority could be mediated through deshmukhs, doras and other local powerholders, although local forms varied. Households might have to supply agricultural work, domestic service, cartage, repairs or ceremonial services. The burden could fall especially on subordinated caste groups, but the precise obligations should be established for a particular locality rather than assumed universal.

### 2. Underlying mechanisms
The structure of exploitation operated through three reinforcing channels. **Economic dependence** meant that access to land, grain, loans and tenancy was controlled by more powerful groups. **Social hierarchy** made resistance costly because caste-based service was represented as custom or obligation. **Political and administrative asymmetry** made complaints difficult: local coercion, threatened eviction, withheld wages or violence could enforce compliance. These mechanisms demonstrate why a purely contractual understanding of labour misses the reality of structural coercion. Vetti was therefore simultaneously a labour issue, a land issue and a human-dignity issue.

### 3. Regional evidence and causal analysis
Telangana peasant mobilisation against oppressive land relations in the 1940s placed forced labour among central grievances. Agricultural struggles, village sanghams and resistance to arbitrary levies cannot be explained by a single cause; debt, rack-renting, evictions, violence, social humiliation and forced labour interacted. Exam answers should distinguish the **1946–51 Telangana armed peasant struggle**, the **1948 accession of Hyderabad**, and later statutory and land-reform developments. These events overlap but are not interchangeable. The abolition of a princely political order by itself does not prove that caste coercion or informal labour dependence disappeared.

### 4. Law and institutional response
The **Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act, 1976** is crucial for understanding the present legal framework, alongside **Article 23** of the Constitution prohibiting trafficking and forced labour. The Act addresses bonded labour, extinguishes specified obligations and provides for identification and rehabilitation. The explanation to section 2(b) of the Act **expressly lists Vetti** among customary forced-labour forms for which an agreement is ordinarily presumed; section 4 abolishes the bonded-labour system and section 5 voids relevant customs and agreements. Express statutory recognition is not automatic proof of every historical incident: examine coercion, obligations, wages and restrictions in each case. Nor is all unpaid work automatically bonded labour.

### 5. Critical evaluation and way forward
The policy challenge is implementation. An affected family may fear losing housing, informal credit, farmland access or social relationships after formal release. Rehabilitation therefore needs land or livelihood security, legal aid, schooling, safe housing, transparent identification, accessible complaints and protection against retaliation. Panchayats and district administrations must track actual freedom and income after release, not merely issue certificates. **High-value conclusion:** Vetti shows how caste, property and local state capacity combine to turn economic dependency into unfree labour.

## Jogini: ritual dedication, caste-gender inequality and rehabilitation

### 1. Meaning and historical context
The Jogini system is a regional form of dedication involving girls or women and a deity or religious setting. Its contemporary significance lies not in sensational descriptions of rituals but in the intersection of coercion, vulnerability, gendered sexuality, community stigma and unequal life chances. Practices differed between settlements, castes and periods. A scholarly answer must avoid claiming that every Jogini experienced an identical sequence or that affected women lacked agency in every circumstance. Structural analysis instead asks who makes the dedication decision, who holds economic resources, what alternatives exist and who benefits from the arrangement.

### 2. Underlying mechanisms
The system can persist through a combination of **caste-linked exclusion**, household poverty, illness-related vows or beliefs, limited girls’ schooling and fear of violating community custom. These explanations are not excuses for exploitation. They show why criminal prohibition alone may be ineffective unless families can access credible health services, education and livelihoods. Dedication can constrain marriage choices, bodily autonomy, economic independence and the status of children. Stigma may be reproduced even after official intervention, making counselling, survivor-led organisations, pensions and housing significant parts of rehabilitation.

### 3. Regional evidence and causal analysis
The controlling law in Telangana is the **Telangana Devadasis (Prohibition of Dedication) Act, 1988**, originally an Andhra Pradesh enactment adapted after state formation. The Act expressly includes **Jogini** in its definition of Devadasi and treats acts of dedication as unlawful. This is the critical legal fact: **Jogini and Devadasi may be distinguished historically or sociologically, yet both are covered by the prohibition statute**. It recognises dedication by different names, addressing attempts to escape legal consequences through local terminology. Candidates should not confuse a ceremonial description with a legally valid religious exemption.

### 4. Law and institutional response
The social consequences include discontinuity in school attendance, exposure to sexual exploitation, restricted livelihood choice, reproductive-health risks and intergenerational stigma. Not every effect is quantifiable through a single statewide statistic. Reliable empirical evaluation needs district-level identification, survivors’ consent and evidence about outcomes. Interventions should respect privacy: publishing lists identifying affected women can aggravate harms. The role of health workers, teachers, local women’s collectives and community advocates is to prevent new dedication and enable voluntary, confidential access to remedies.

### 5. Critical evaluation and way forward
A strong Telangana policy response has five pillars: **prevention** through schooling and community dialogue; **enforcement** of the 1988 law; **rehabilitation** with assets, jobs and social security; **health and legal services** delivered without discrimination; and **monitoring** of actual choice and dignity rather than only ceremonies prevented. The constitutional basis lies in equality, protection against exploitation, personal liberty and dignity. The key mains argument is that criminalisation and rehabilitation must be designed together; otherwise a prohibited practice can continue invisibly.

## Devadasi institution: historical diversity and legal prohibition

### 1. Meaning and historical context
The term Devadasi historically encompassed women connected to temple institutions through ritual, artistic, property or service relationships, with considerable regional and temporal diversity. Medieval epigraphic and literary material indicates contexts in which temple performers held established social or artistic positions; later colonial and postcolonial discussions often treated disparate practices as a single timeless institution. This creates a historiographical problem: a historical temple performer should not automatically be described as a victim of the exact exploitative pattern found in every modern dedication practice. An examination answer must differentiate **historical institution**, **regional ritual practice**, and **contemporary rights violation**.

### 2. Underlying mechanisms
Temple economies connected ritual performance, land grants, patronage and local elites. Changes in political patronage, colonial legal treatment, reform politics and economic relations transformed the place of women associated with these institutions. Nineteenth- and twentieth-century debates included concerns about sexual exploitation, moral policing, women’s autonomy and preservation of performing arts. The complex history of dance reform should not be flattened into either uncritical celebration of the institution or the claim that its cultural output justified coercion. An analytical answer foregrounds consent and the unequal access to social power.

### 3. Regional evidence and causal analysis
In Telangana the operational legal reference is the **Telangana Devadasis (Prohibition of Dedication) Act, 1988**. Its statutory definition extends to named local variants including **Basavi, Jogini, Parvathi, Mathamma and Thyamma**. Thus, the law’s coverage is broader than one popular label. Candidates should write that dedication is prohibited, marriages of women formerly dedicated are recognised under the relevant law, and penal and administrative provisions support enforcement. Avoid making unsupported assertions about exact punishment terms unless checked against the operative amended text.

### 4. Law and institutional response
An effective comparison with Jogini has four dimensions. **History:** the Devadasi label includes diverse temple-service and artistic histories, while Jogini commonly denotes a local dedication form. **Regionality:** nomenclature and practices differ. **Present harms:** involuntary dedication, discrimination and exploitation may intersect but should be documented in each setting. **Law:** the Telangana legislation covers both categories. This four-part comparison is an easy way to earn marks without repeating two nearly identical paragraphs.

### 5. Critical evaluation and way forward
Reform must centre survivors rather than merely symbols. Protective schooling, land or housing access, income generation, anti-discrimination enforcement, counselling and reintegration require sustained financing. Where former practitioners preserve an artistic lineage, cultural recognition must be separate from any pressure to submit girls to dedication. **Conclusion:** the historical evolution of a cultural institution cannot override modern constitutional standards of consent, equality and bodily autonomy.

## Fluorosis in Telangana: a groundwater and public-health case

### 1. Meaning and historical context
Fluorosis is a chronic health condition linked to excessive fluoride exposure, particularly through drinking water over time. It must be distinguished from occasional ingestion and from diseases that have similar clinical presentations. **Dental fluorosis** may produce enamel changes during tooth development; **skeletal fluorosis** can involve bones and joints following prolonged exposure. Severe outcomes depend on concentration, duration, nutrition, water consumption and other factors. The **WHO guideline value of 1.5 mg/L fluoride in drinking water** is an important reference point, though national standards and risk-management settings should be checked for the exact regulatory use.

### 2. Underlying mechanisms
The Nalgonda region is a well-known Telangana case because groundwater flowing through fluoride-bearing geological formations can dissolve fluoride under favourable geochemical conditions. The exposure is not caused by the mere presence of groundwater or by every borewell in a district. Rock chemistry, groundwater residence time, alkalinity, hydrogeological conditions, well depth and competing water sources affect measured concentrations. Students must therefore separate **geogenic contamination** from pollution associated with industrial discharge. Testing the actual source is necessary; appearance, taste and the word “borewell” cannot establish safety.

### 3. Regional evidence and causal analysis
Fluorosis is also a social-development issue. A household unable to pay for alternative water may continue using a contaminated source even with adequate awareness. Long distances to safe sources impose a time cost on women and children. Chronic disability can reduce income, educational attendance and participation in community life. Poor nutrition and delayed diagnosis can worsen vulnerability. A mains answer that merely recommends water filters misses institutional access, maintenance, behaviour change and health-system follow-up.

### 4. Law and institutional response
Options include safe surface-water supply, verified low-fluoride alternative sources, blending where technically appropriate, and suitable **defluoridation technologies** such as activated alumina or the **Nalgonda technique**. Treatment requires trained operators, sludge disposal, regular quality checks and financing. The Nalgonda technique, developed for fluoride removal using chemical coagulation and precipitation, should not be confused with the geographical disease burden in Nalgonda district. Each approach should be evaluated for lifecycle cost and reliable actual delivery, not merely installation.

### 5. Critical evaluation and way forward
Mission Bhagiratha has sought wider rural piped-water access in Telangana; the relevant outcome, however, is **continuously available water that meets tested quality standards at household points of use**. A rigorous answer recommends source mapping, periodically published fluoride test results, school and anganwadi screening, referral for dental and skeletal health, accessible safe supply and grievance redress. **Conclusion:** fluorosis is an example of how geology becomes a public-health injustice when water governance and household choices are constrained.

## Girl child and child marriage: capabilities, protection and autonomy

### 1. Meaning and historical context
“Girl child” is not a single demographic disadvantage. It covers unequal nutrition and healthcare, schooling interruptions, unpaid care, safety restrictions, early marriage and the unequal distribution of family resources. Risks differ by class, caste, disability, region and migration status. A meaningful social analysis therefore studies **life-course disadvantages**: a school dropout can trigger greater economic dependence, reducing the ability to resist early marriage or unsafe work. Conversely, secondary education, safe mobility and employment aspirations can alter bargaining power within households.

### 2. Underlying mechanisms
**Child marriage** is legally defined under the **Prohibition of Child Marriage Act, 2006** with age thresholds of **under 18 for females** and **under 21 for males** in the Act’s definition. Section 3 generally makes a child marriage **voidable at the option of the party who was a child**, while the Act specifies additional situations in which marriages are void. Writing that *all child marriages are automatically void* is therefore a major exam error. The law also provides mechanisms for preventive injunctions, protection, maintenance and Child Marriage Prohibition Officers.

### 3. Regional evidence and causal analysis
Root causes include poverty and debt, dowry concerns, family honour norms, safety fears, limited secondary schools and the perceived economic burden of an unmarried daughter. Families may frame early marriage as protection, but the decision can instead increase risks of interrupted education, adolescent pregnancy, restricted autonomy and dependence. A well-evidenced answer distinguishes **causation** from stereotypes: poverty is a factor but cannot by itself explain why some equally poor communities have much lower child-marriage prevalence.

### 4. Law and institutional response
Institutional responses require connecting the **Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009**, the **POCSO Act, 2012**, the Prohibition of Child Marriage Act and public-health services without conflating their legal scopes. The RTE compulsory elementary-education framework covers ages **6–14**; that alone is insufficient to keep an adolescent girl in secondary school. Transport, toilets, menstrual-health support, safe hostels, scholarships, digital access and completion-oriented monitoring are important for older girls. Helplines and reporting mechanisms should protect privacy.

### 5. Critical evaluation and way forward
Telangana policy should measure transition from upper primary to secondary and higher secondary, school attendance, early pregnancy risks, access for migrant families and district variation. Teachers, accredited health workers, anganwadi systems, Child Marriage Prohibition Officers and women’s groups can coordinate prevention before wedding preparations escalate. **Conclusion:** girls’ empowerment depends on long-term capabilities and meaningful autonomy, not only cancellation of individual ceremonies.

## Child labour: hazards, law and the schooling–livelihood link

### 1. Meaning and historical context
Child labour is work that deprives children of education, health, development or dignity, and the legal framework further defines prohibited employment. Not all age-appropriate assistance in the home is identical to commercial exploitation. The **Child and Adolescent Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Act, 1986**, substantially amended in **2016**, broadly prohibits employing children under **14** across occupations, subject to limited statutory exceptions, and prohibits adolescents aged **14–18** from specified hazardous occupations and processes. The family-enterprise exception is not a blanket authorisation to miss school or expose a child to dangerous work.

### 2. Underlying mechanisms
In Telangana and neighbouring urban labour markets, risk settings can include small workshops, domestic labour, roadside commerce, brick kilns, agricultural operations, construction support and informal supply chains. These are **possible risk settings**, not proof that every establishment employs children. Labour subcontracting obscures the ultimate employer and complicates inspections. An enforcement strategy should follow supply chains and distinguish actual rescued cases, surveys and estimates; reporting an arrest count as the prevalence of child labour is a statistical mistake.

### 3. Regional evidence and causal analysis
Drivers can be household indebtedness, irregular adult earnings, seasonal migration, school distance, poor instruction, discrimination, absence of childcare and employer demand for cheap, controllable workers. Children may perform paid, unpaid or family-based labour. Girls’ domestic work is especially vulnerable to undercounting. A child who is technically enrolled but works long hours can still experience educational deprivation. This shows why school enrolment figures cannot substitute for attendance, learning and time-use evidence.

### 4. Law and institutional response
Prevention should combine adult livelihood protections, residential or bridge education when necessary, flexible school transfers, strong inspection capacity, employer accountability and case-specific rehabilitation. **Article 24** forbids child employment in factories, mines and hazardous occupations under the constitutional wording; **Article 21A** supports education in the 6–14 age group. POCSO and trafficking laws may apply in specific cases but should not be invoked automatically for every child-labour complaint. Protecting children requires coordinated rather than indiscriminate legal classification.

### 5. Critical evaluation and way forward
A strong mains answer proposes outcome measures: sustained school re-entry, age-appropriate learning, physical and mental health follow-up, family income security, prevention of re-trafficking and successful prosecution where warranted. **Conclusion:** labour inspections are necessary but insufficient if the household economic pressure and schooling failures that enable exploitation remain unchanged.

## Migrant labour: mobility, precarity and portability of rights

### 1. Meaning and historical context
Migration is movement across a place of usual residence for work or other reasons; migrant labour is a subset defined by labour mobility. Telangana contains both migration **into Hyderabad and industrial districts** and movement out of dryland or low-opportunity villages. Seasonal migrants, circular migrants and more permanent migrants have different housing, schooling and benefit needs. Do not assume that every migrant is an interstate migrant: intra-district, intrastate and interstate movements must be identified separately.

### 2. Underlying mechanisms
Contractors and labour intermediaries may connect workers with construction sites, brick kilns, transport, services or manufacturing. These arrangements can reduce search costs but also create recruitment debts, wage deductions, weak documentation and obscured accountability. A power imbalance increases when a worker depends on the employer for both wages and housing. Women migrant workers may face additional constraints in sanitation, childcare, harassment reporting and access to reproductive-health services. Children may drop out when school records and instruction do not follow the family.

### 3. Regional evidence and causal analysis
The **Inter-State Migrant Workmen (Regulation of Employment and Conditions of Service) Act, 1979** is a historic statutory reference; contemporary answer writing must check the actual commencement and operation of newer labour codes before asserting that older provisions have ceased to apply. The **One Nation One Ration Card** programme illustrates benefit portability, but ration portability does not automatically guarantee migrant access to primary healthcare, schools, provident-fund entitlements or affordable rental housing.

### 4. Law and institutional response
Urban Telangana examples should connect the employment pull of the Hyderabad metropolitan economy with informal rental markets, worker settlements, long commutes and exposure to occupational injury. Rural examples should consider rainfall variability, agricultural indebtedness, landlessness and weak nonfarm opportunities. Rather than label all movement “distress migration”, distinguish productive opportunity-seeking mobility from forced or constrained movement. The policy purpose should be to make mobility **safe, voluntary and rights-protected**, not to stop every migration flow.

### 5. Critical evaluation and way forward
Priority measures include registration without invasive surveillance, enforceable wage records, occupational-safety inspections, multilingual grievance channels, portable social protection, accessible creches, inter-state school transfer and dignified rental accommodation. Evaluate outcomes through actual wage receipt, accident rates, school continuity and benefit access. **Conclusion:** migration is compatible with development, but exploitation emerges where employment is mobile and social rights remain tied to a fixed address.

## Telangana social movements: organisations, grievances and change

### 1. Meaning and historical context
Social movements are sustained collective efforts to alter unequal social relations, distribute resources or win recognition. They should be differentiated from a one-day protest, an electoral campaign or an individual grievance. Telangana’s movements have operated across intersecting axes: agrarian class, caste hierarchy, tribal rights, women’s dignity, language and regional autonomy. A strong answer identifies the **social base**, **organisations and leaders**, **repertoire of collective action**, **claims** and **outcomes**, while avoiding the impression that all movements had the same membership or ideology.

### 2. Underlying mechanisms
In the late Hyderabad State, **Andhra Mahasabha** and related organisational networks enabled demands around education, language and village grievances. The **Telangana peasant armed struggle (1946–51)** challenged oppressive agrarian relations, landlord violence, levies and forced labour. Its participants and goals changed across political phases; **Operation Polo in 1948**, the Indian Union’s accession process and the end of the armed struggle in 1951 mark different events. Women such as **Chakali Ailamma**, whose resistance has become emblematic of agrarian mobilisation, should be situated in the relevant tenancy and local power context rather than converted into unsourced legends.

### 3. Regional evidence and causal analysis
Tribal resistance also has distinct histories. **Komaram Bheem** is associated with Gond struggles against exploitation in the Asifabad area and the slogan popularly remembered as **Jal, Jangal, Zameen**; the wording and timing of quoted slogans should be treated carefully if the original contemporary documentation is uncertain. **Ramji Gond** belongs to an earlier nineteenth-century resistance context. A well-written answer should analyse forests, customary resources, coercive extraction and political autonomy without merging different centuries.

### 4. Law and institutional response
After Independence, regional mobilisation reflected grievances over public employment, resource allocation, political safeguards and recognition of Telangana. The **1969 Telangana movement**, later mobilisation through civil-society platforms, and the movement culminating in **state formation on 2 June 2014** must be distinguished by leadership, institutions and political conditions. Regional identity was not one unchanging demand: employment guarantees, irrigation, perceived fiscal inequity and self-government acquired varying importance in different phases.

### 5. Critical evaluation and way forward
Women’s movements, anti-caste struggles, anti-arrack campaigns and campaigns against Jogini dedication demonstrate that social reform is not only a question of state policy. They frequently combine legal demands, consciousness-raising, local support networks and material rehabilitation. Movements can win new formal rights while leaving implementation unfinished. **Mains synthesis:** Telangana social movements show the transformation of individual deprivation into collective political claims; the test of success is whether institutions materially alter access to land, labour freedom, education, resources and dignity.

## Synthesis: linking social issues to movements
Vetti and land coercion contributed to agrarian collective organisation; the persistence of Jogini dedication illustrates a caste–gender problem requiring both prohibition and rehabilitation; fluorosis illustrates the conversion of a geogenic hazard into unequal health outcomes; and migration illustrates how mobile livelihoods collide with place-bound welfare administration. Across these problems, the hallmark of an exam-quality argument is naming the relevant mechanism, law and historical organisation before recommending solutions.

## Question: Vetti was more than an unpaid labour practice. Analyse.

### Introduction
Vetti in historical Telangana signifies compelled or unpaid service embedded in unequal agrarian and caste relations. It was a system of domination because workers often lacked meaningful freedom to refuse.

### Structural causes
Land dependence, credit obligations, hereditary service expectations and local power of landlords could reinforce one another. Loss of tenancy or housing was a threat that converted customary duty into coerced labour.

### Social consequences
Vetti curtailed bargaining power, dignity and opportunities for schooling and alternative work. It also maintained the wider landlord structure by providing cheap services beyond ordinary agriculture.

### Resistance and law
The 1946–51 Telangana agrarian struggle challenged forced labour alongside levies and evictions. Article 23 prohibits forced labour. The explanation to section 2(b) of the Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act, 1976 expressly names Vetti among customary forced-labour forms; section 4 abolishes the bonded-labour system. That express naming is not a shortcut to proving the facts of an individual case.

### Critical insight
The examiner should distinguish involuntary service from ordinary low wages and show how caste rank, tenancy and credit generated a coercive bargaining environment. An anti-Vetti answer must specify what changed after agrarian resistance and what present-day accountability mechanisms prevent re-emergence.

### Conclusion
Durable eradication requires livelihood independence, enforcement, safe complaints and rehabilitation. Ending a formal obligation without changing dependency may merely conceal coercion.

## Question: Explain the factors sustaining Jogini dedication and critically evaluate its prohibition.

### Background
Jogini is a regional dedication practice involving girls or women. Its significance for contemporary policy lies in the intersection of ritual pressure, caste hierarchy, gender inequality and inadequate economic choice.

### Root causes
Household poverty, limited schooling, vows linked to illness and unequal community norms can make refusal difficult. These are structural explanations, not cultural justifications.

### Law
The Telangana Devadasis (Prohibition of Dedication) Act, 1988 treats dedication as unlawful and expressly includes Jogini within its statutory definition. Different local labels cannot create a lawful exemption.

### Challenges
Survivors may experience stigma, health burdens and limited independent income after prohibition. Criminalisation without trusted counselling, privacy, education and livelihood rehabilitation risks pushing practices into invisibility.

### Critical insight
The useful analytical contrast is between the ceremony's social label and the enforceable legal definition: the 1988 Telangana Act includes Jogini despite regional naming differences. Success must be measured through individual choice, school completion, rehabilitation and freedom from stigma.

### Way forward and conclusion
Combine survivor-led prevention, legal enforcement, schooling, confidential healthcare and secure economic assets. The success indicator is actual freedom, not merely the number of ceremonies interrupted.

## Question: Fluorosis in Telangana demonstrates the link between geography and social inequality. Discuss.

### Introduction
Fluorosis arises from prolonged excessive fluoride exposure, typically through drinking water. The Nalgonda region illustrates how groundwater chemistry intersects with public health.

### Geographic mechanism
Fluoride-bearing rocks, groundwater residence time and geochemical conditions may elevate dissolved fluoride. Contamination varies by source; borewell water cannot be assumed uniformly unsafe or safe.

### Human consequences
Dental fluorosis affects enamel; skeletal fluorosis can damage bones and joints. Where households lack alternatives, chronic exposure may cause disability, schooling losses and reduced earning capacity.

### Measures
A safe response combines periodic testing, alternative low-fluoride supply, properly operated defluoridation, school screening and clinical referral. Piped-water installation does not prove that safe water consistently reaches every home.

### Critical insight
A policy answer must distinguish geological risk mapping from health surveillance. Safe drinking-water access is measured at the tap over time; treatment-plant construction totals cannot substitute for water-quality compliance, independent testing and follow-up of people already affected.

### Conclusion
Fluorosis is preventable when geoscience, public-health follow-up and accountable local water service function together; otherwise natural groundwater risk becomes an inequitable social burden.

## Question: Child marriage persists despite legislation. Examine the Telangana policy response.

### Introduction
Child marriage restricts agency, schooling and adolescent health. Its persistence is linked to gender inequality and uneven educational and economic opportunity.

### Root causes
Household insecurity, dowry expectations, family-honour norms, safety concerns and weak secondary-school access can push families toward early marriage.

### Law and safeguards
Under the Prohibition of Child Marriage Act, 2006, a child is a female below 18 or male below 21. Section 3 generally makes the marriage voidable at the option of the child party; specified other circumstances can make a marriage void. Child Marriage Prohibition Officers support prevention.

### Policy gaps
Cancellation alone may expose a girl to retaliation or school dropout if safe accommodation, income and continuing education are missing. The elementary RTE framework covers ages 6–14, leaving a vital secondary-school retention challenge.

### Critical insight
A distinctive implementation recommendation is to identify girls at risk of transition-related dropout before a wedding is arranged. Tracking secondary-school completion, safe travel, household support and repeat-risk cases better measures prevention than a simple count of cancelled marriages.

### Conclusion
Focus on early detection, safe transport and residential schooling, confidential case support, parental engagement and long-term education outcomes rather than only prosecutions.

## Question: Discuss why migrant labour requires portable social protection in Telangana.

### Introduction
Hyderabad attracts workers while rural households may undertake seasonal and circular migration. Migrants contribute to growth but face service gaps when benefits remain tied to a fixed location.

### Causes and patterns
Construction, services, manufacturing and brick kilns attract contractors and migrant workers. Intra-state migration differs from inter-state migration; not every movement is distress-driven.

### Vulnerabilities
Informal housing, delayed pay, workplace injury, language barriers, lack of creches and interruptions to children’s schooling can undermine mobility gains.

### Institutional measures
Ration portability is useful, but it cannot by itself assure health access, labour standards or school transfer. Wage records, safety inspections, bilingual grievance systems and affordable rental housing must travel with workers.

### Critical insight
Migrant workers are not a homogeneous category. Age, gender, duration, occupation, home-state documentation and contractor arrangements alter vulnerability. Strong administration connects wage enforcement and social-security portability to schools, childcare and safe rental accommodation at actual worksites.

### Conclusion
Public policy should protect the right to move safely and voluntarily, measuring wages received, injury risks, school continuity and benefits actually accessed.

## Question: Explain the relationship between social movements and structural change in Telangana.

### Introduction
Social movements convert persistent private grievances into collective claims for land, dignity, political voice and recognition.

### Historical background
Andhra Mahasabha networks and village mobilisation addressed language and agrarian grievances. The 1946–51 Telangana peasant struggle challenged coercive land relations, levies and Vetti.

### Different strands
Tribal struggles associated with Ramji Gond and Komaram Bheem occurred in distinct historical contexts. The 1969 agitation and subsequent regional mobilisation culminating in statehood in 2014 concerned safeguards, resources and self-government.

### Critical assessment
Movements can secure legislation or political reorganisation without immediately eliminating caste exclusion, agrarian inequality or gender-based exploitation. Social mobilisation and public administration must therefore be evaluated separately.

### Critical insight
Avoid claiming that political statehood automatically solved rural social inequality. An evidence-led assessment separates movement objectives, institutional concessions and longer-term outcomes for tenants, tribal communities, women and landless workers, using named organisations and distinct historical phases.

### Conclusion
The lasting measure of social movement success is not mobilisation alone but enforceable access to land, welfare, schooling, safe work, dignity and democratic participation.

# TGPSC Paper III — Unit I — Topic 5: Social Policies and Programmes in India and Telangana

**Status:** Expanded draft for academic review, not a certified official answer key. **Official syllabus:** Social Policies and Programmes in India and Telangana: Policies for Women, Children, Aged and Disabled; Policies for Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes, Backward Classes and Minorities; Environmental Policy; Population Policy; Policy on Education; Policy on Health; Poverty Alleviation Programmes; Welfare Schemes for Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes, Backward Classes, Women, Children, Minorities, Aged and Disabled.

## Orientation: what an examiner expects

A serious answer distinguishes constitutional justification, the precise policy instrument, the responsible implementing institution, beneficiary eligibility, actual delivery and measured impact. Avoid empty phrases such as “government should create awareness” unless paired with an accountable authority, a delivery mechanism and an outcome metric. A scheme’s announced objective is not proof that the promised service reached beneficiaries.

## 01 Policy analysis: rights, social justice and delivery architecture

### Meaning and policy background

**Rights-based and distributive policy.** India’s welfare architecture is best understood as a sequence: constitutional equality and Directive Principles establish public purposes; Parliament and State legislatures establish enforceable entitlements or statutory bodies; budgets and administrative rules finance services; frontline institutions translate rules into benefits; grievance and judicial mechanisms correct exclusion. A cash benefit, a reservation, a school meal, an accessible building and a prohibition of discrimination solve different problems. Distinguishing policy instruments prevents shallow answers.

### Legal and institutional mechanisms

Analyse every scheme through five questions: Who qualifies? What legal entitlement or administrative discretion exists? Which office delivers the service? What evidence measures outcomes rather than merely expenditure? What group might be systematically left out? Digital identity or banking requirements can streamline payments while generating last-mile exclusion when names, documents and addresses do not match. Social audits, multilingual facilitation, independent appeals and privacy safeguards are therefore substantive design principles.

### Telangana application, challenges and outcome measurement

For Telangana, compare state programmes with central frameworks without assuming funding or beneficiary definitions are identical. For instance, programme announcements should not be described as fully implemented statewide without an official order and performance report. In mains writing, distinguish intended objectives, notified rules, operational reach and verified impact.

## 02 Policies for women and girls

### Meaning and policy background

**Safety, agency and economic empowerment.** Constitutional equality under Articles 14–16, protection against sex discrimination under Article 15 and the directive for maternity relief under Article 42 form a layered foundation. Article 15(3) permits special provisions for women and children, but constitutional legitimacy is not by itself proof of programme success. Economic empowerment means control over income, property, mobility, time and decisions, not only enrolment in a savings group.

### Legal and institutional mechanisms

Mission Shakti combines Sambal (safety and protection, including One Stop Centres and Women Helpline) with Samarthya (empowerment and care-oriented interventions). The Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005 creates civil protective remedies; the POSH Act, 2013 deals with workplace sexual harassment, internal committees and local committees. These legal regimes have distinct complaint pathways. Maternal benefit programmes must be evaluated jointly with nutrition, antenatal services and unpaid care burdens.

### Telangana application, challenges and outcome measurement

A Telangana answer can examine women’s Self-Help Group networks, mobility-related initiatives and district-level service access but must distinguish scheme eligibility and actual benefit receipt. Strong indicators include violence-response timeliness, women’s paid work participation, maternal health continuity, unpaid-care load and legal-aid access; a count of awareness camps alone is inadequate.

## 03 Children: survival, learning and protection

### Meaning and policy background

**Lifecycle approach.** Child welfare begins before birth and continues through early childhood, school years and adolescence. Policies that address only school enrolment miss birth weight, early stimulation, nutrition, safe transport, disability inclusion, violence and transitions to secondary education. Article 21A establishes free compulsory education for children aged six to fourteen, while the Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009 operationalises standards and obligations.

### Legal and institutional mechanisms

Saksham Anganwadi and Poshan 2.0 integrates early childhood nutrition services, Anganwadi infrastructure and related programmes. Mission Vatsalya concerns children requiring care and protection or in conflict with law, operating with the Juvenile Justice Act framework. POCSO, 2012 criminalises specified sexual offences against children and provides specialised procedures. Child labour law and rehabilitation must be coupled with family income and school reintegration, or enforcement can shift exploitation into less visible workplaces.

### Telangana application, challenges and outcome measurement

Telangana district planning should connect Anganwadi centres, schools, healthcare, Child Welfare Committees, District Child Protection Units and police child-help mechanisms. Measures should distinguish stunting, wasting and anaemia; each has a different meaning and intervention pathway. A strong answer highlights protection and nutritional outcomes as well as attendance.

### Implementation case: continuity across child services

Consider a hypothetical child who moves with a construction-worker household from a Telangana mandal to Hyderabad. The child's Anganwadi record, school admission and immunisation history may be held by different offices. A district response should arrange immediate access to age-appropriate services while records are transferred, rather than treating missing documents as proof of ineligibility. Nutrition teams can monitor growth and refer suspected illness; teachers must assess learning needs; child-protection staff should investigate specific risks without automatically treating poverty or migration as grounds for institutional care.

Mission Vatsalya supports the Juvenile Justice care-and-protection system. A Child Welfare Committee handles statutory decisions for children in need of care and protection, whereas a Juvenile Justice Board has a different role for children in conflict with law. Ministry guidance favours family-based non-institutional care where safe and appropriate. The child helpline 1098 is linked to emergency response 112, but a helpline contact is not itself evidence of a resolved case. District Child Protection Units should coordinate referrals with safeguards for privacy, consent where appropriate, and follow-up.

For evaluation, separately record uninterrupted nutrition service, learning progression, safe resolution of protection concerns and family reunification where appropriate. These outcomes cannot be inferred from registrations or expenditure alone. [Sources: Ministry of Women and Child Development, Mission Vatsalya programme guidance; Mission Saksham Anganwadi and Poshan 2.0 programme description, accessed 27 October 2026.]

## 04 Older persons: income, care and dignity

### Meaning and policy background

**Ageing and social protection.** Ageing policy is not merely an old-age pension. It addresses income replacement, chronic illness, disability, loneliness, elder abuse, assistive devices, accessible transport and long-term care. India’s demographic transition increases the importance of household structures, gendered widowhood and the distribution of unpaid care. Article 41 recognises public assistance in cases including old age subject to State capacity.

### Legal and institutional mechanisms

The Maintenance and Welfare of Parents and Senior Citizens Act, 2007 provides a maintenance framework and institutions for welfare. NSAP includes Indira Gandhi National Old Age Pension Scheme for eligible groups. Eligibility and payment amounts differ between Union support and state top-ups; never merge the two figures without verifying a current government order. Primary healthcare and home/community care may be more appropriate than institutions for many older people.

### Telangana application, challenges and outcome measurement

In Telangana rural areas, transport distance and digital beneficiary verification may be particularly relevant; in Hyderabad, isolation and elder abuse may coexist with nearby hospitals. Evaluating pensions requires inflation-adjusted adequacy, uninterrupted payments, accessible grievance redress and access to appropriate geriatric care.

## 05 Disability: reasonable accommodation and inclusion

### Meaning and policy background

**Social model versus medical model.** A medical model emphasises individual impairment and treatment; the social or rights model asks how inaccessible buildings, discriminatory attitudes, non-adaptive teaching and unaccommodating jobs produce disability-related exclusion. Persons with Disabilities are not a homogeneous group. The Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016 recognises specified disabilities and introduces accessibility duties, non-discrimination and reasonable accommodation principles.

### Legal and institutional mechanisms

For persons with benchmark disabilities, the Act includes at least 4% reservation in government employment and not less than 5% seats in government and government-aided higher educational institutions, subject to the applicable statutory framework. These figures should not be casually applied to every private job or educational programme. Accessible India Campaign, assistive technology, disability certificates and UDID mechanisms can improve access only if assessments are fair and alternatives exist for people facing documentation barriers.

### Telangana application, challenges and outcome measurement

Telangana answers should cover ramps and lifts, accessible Telugu/Urdu information, sign-language support, adaptive examinations, school inclusion and rural transport. Outcomes include actual school progression, employment retention, equal wage opportunity and accessible public services—not merely the count of certificates issued.

### Distinguish legal thresholds before quoting reservation figures

Four frequently confused provisions have different scopes. Article 21A establishes the general six-to-fourteen-year constitutional guarantee of free and compulsory education. Section 31 of the Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016 provides a specific six-to-eighteen-year education right for children with benchmark disabilities. Section 32 requires at least five per cent reservation of seats in government and government-aided higher educational institutions for persons with benchmark disabilities. Section 34 addresses not less than four per cent of specified vacancies in government establishments, subject to the statutory categories and conditions. These are not interchangeable percentages or universal private-sector quotas. A mains answer should identify the legal beneficiary, institution and entitlement before comparing policies.

A district implementation audit should follow an applicant's complete journey: accessible information, usable transport, non-discriminatory admission, reasonable examination accommodation, accessible toilets and digital services, timely decisions, and a functioning grievance channel. For example, a wheelchair ramp at a college is inadequate if the examination room is upstairs or an online form cannot be used with assistive technology. Measure successful participation and retention, not simply the number of disability certificates or ramps. [Sources: India Code, Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act 2016, sections 31–34; Ministry of Education, Article 21A guidance, accessed 27 October 2026.]

## 06 Scheduled Castes: discrimination, mobility and entitlements

### Meaning and policy background

**Anti-discrimination plus capability expansion.** Articles 15 and 17, protective legislation and public reservation measures operate in different but complementary spheres. The Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955 addresses offences arising from untouchability; the Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989 provides a stronger framework for specified caste- and tribe-based offences and relief mechanisms. The policy challenge is not exhausted by allocating benefits to a category.

### Legal and institutional mechanisms

Educational scholarships, hostels, coaching, skill training, entrepreneurship finance, land-related measures and targeted budget planning work only when institutional access and quality are also addressed. Reservation secures representation in designated opportunities; it does not automatically eliminate occupational discrimination or unsafe housing. A scholarship should be judged by continuation and graduation, not the number of applications approved.

### Telangana application, challenges and outcome measurement

In Telangana, local evidence on rural landlessness, urban employment, residential education and caste-based violence should be linked to particular institutions and accurate denominators. Distinguish state-specific SC classification proposals from enacted constitutional or statutory provisions and do not assume pending decisions have taken effect.

## 07 Scheduled Tribes: forest, land, autonomy and services

### Meaning and policy background

**Location and collective rights.** ST policy cannot be reduced to scholarships or a generic tribal-development allocation. Many communities’ livelihoods are tied to customary forests, seasonal mobility, common resources and culturally rooted institutions. Article 244 and the Fifth Schedule concern Scheduled Areas; PESA, 1996 addresses panchayat self-government in Scheduled Areas; the Forest Rights Act, 2006 recognises qualifying individual and community forest rights through defined procedures.

### Legal and institutional mechanisms

PESA and FRA must not be presented as equivalent. PESA establishes specific governance responsibilities and Gram Sabha roles in applicable Scheduled Areas; FRA concerns recognition of rights and community forest resource governance. PM-JANMAN targets Particularly Vulnerable Tribal Groups, with infrastructure and service gaps as important concerns. Development-induced displacement, mining and conservation restrictions require due process, rights recognition and genuinely informed local participation.

### Telangana application, challenges and outcome measurement

For Telangana, compare experiences of Gond, Koya, Kolam and Chenchu communities without pretending they all have identical ecology or administrative status. Study school language, seasonal transport, sickle-cell screening, community resources and recognition of land rights. Record district-specific and tribal-specific evidence rather than unsupported state-wide claims.

## 08 Backward Classes: social and educational disadvantage

### Meaning and policy background

**Classification and access.** Backward Class policies respond to historically structured social and educational disadvantage; they cannot be reduced to a single measure of household income. The Mandal Commission, Indra Sawhney judgment (1992), constitutional provisions and evolving State/Union lists illustrate the connection between political representation, equality and administrative classification. Central OBC lists and state BC lists are not always identical; eligibility for a Union recruitment programme depends on its own legal framework.

### Legal and institutional mechanisms

Policies can include reservations, hostels, professional scholarships, artisan support and educational facilities. Creamy-layer rules apply in specified settings and should not be described as uniform across all categories or jurisdictions. Telangana’s BC welfare and artisan schemes require careful checking of current government orders, approved budgets and beneficiary criteria. A caste survey measures composition and socioeconomic characteristics but is not itself a legal reservation order.

### Telangana application, challenges and outcome measurement

Evaluation should trace benefits to school retention, higher-education progression, skilled occupations, earnings and representation. Intersection with gender, disability and geographic location is critical: two individuals in a broad category may face quite different obstacles.

## 09 Minorities: cultural rights and substantive equality

### Meaning and policy background

**Religious and linguistic dimensions.** Articles 29 and 30 protect specified cultural and educational interests; Article 30(1) protects religious and linguistic minorities’ rights to establish and administer educational institutions. Not every cultural right in Article 29 is confined to minorities. The National Commission for Minorities Act, 1992 establishes a national institutional framework; state institutions and scholarship programmes vary.

### Legal and institutional mechanisms

Educational assistance, minority financial development, credit access, hostels, language preservation and anti-discrimination responses must be studied as distinct instruments. Poverty in a minority neighbourhood cannot be understood entirely through identity; housing, labour market segmentation, public services, geographic exclusion and discrimination interact. A genuine evaluation avoids both stereotyping and the assumption that formal equality ensures equal opportunity.

### Telangana application, challenges and outcome measurement

Telangana’s Telugu–Urdu–Dakhni language ecology and the historical diversity of Hyderabad make useful context. Assess school access, urban neighbourhood facilities and employment connectivity; do not attribute a programme’s effectiveness to its label without beneficiary data and disaggregated outcomes.

## 10 Environmental policy: precaution, protection and justice

### Meaning and policy background

**Regulation and ecological governance.** Environmental policy brings together the constitutional duty to protect the environment under Article 48A and citizens’ fundamental duty under Article 51A(g), the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986, Water Act, 1974, Air Act, 1981 and specific forest and biodiversity frameworks. The precautionary and polluter-pays principles influence environmental jurisprudence but require institutional implementation and monitoring.

### Legal and institutional mechanisms

Regulatory instruments include consent systems, environmental impact assessment, emission standards, environmental clearance, protected-area management, waste rules and economic incentives. An environmental clearance is not a declaration that a project has no ecological cost; impact predictions, cumulative effects, public participation and post-clearance monitoring are essential. Environmental justice examines who receives development benefits and who bears pollution, land loss and health risks.

### Telangana application, challenges and outcome measurement

Telangana examples include Musi River pollution and flooding, urban lake catchments, coal mining in the Godavari basin and groundwater stress. The best answer distinguishes source control, sewer networks, industrial effluent compliance and ecosystem restoration; a one-time riverfront beautification project is not pollution control.

## 11 Population policy and demographic change

### Meaning and policy background

**Fertility, rights and services.** Population policy must distinguish population size, growth rate, fertility, mortality, migration and age structure. Replacement-level fertility is not the same as zero population growth, because population momentum can continue as large youthful cohorts enter reproductive ages. India’s National Population Policy 2000 promoted reproductive and child health, education and voluntary informed choice; coercive interpretations are inconsistent with a rights-centred approach.

### Legal and institutional mechanisms

The demographic dividend is a potential economic advantage, not an automatic result of population structure. Employment creation, gender equality, maternal health, education and urban planning condition whether working-age growth raises wellbeing. An ageing society faces different policy needs: pensions, accessible transport, chronic disease management and caregiving support.

### Telangana application, challenges and outcome measurement

In Telangana, district-level population changes can be affected by migration and urban reclassification. Use census-year labels and official estimation methods; do not present a 2011 census share as a measured 2026 outcome. Discuss Hyderabad’s in-migration separately from fertility patterns.

## 12 Education policy: access, learning and equity

### Meaning and policy background

**From enrolment to foundational capability.** The Right to Education framework makes elementary access a legal obligation. National Education Policy 2020 emphasises foundational literacy and numeracy, curricular flexibility, multilingual pedagogy, teacher development and broader reforms. Education policy should be examined through access, participation, learning, transitions and employment relevance; high enrolment alone cannot guarantee learning.

### Legal and institutional mechanisms

Samagra Shiksha combines school-education support across stages; PM POSHAN addresses school nutrition and attendance incentives in eligible schools. Students from remote tribal habitations, migrants, children with disabilities and first-generation learners may face different instructional barriers. Teacher vacancies, infrastructure, internet access and medium-of-instruction transitions need direct attention.

### Telangana application, challenges and outcome measurement

In Telangana, analyse residential schools and hostels alongside neighbourhood schooling, teacher allocation, Telugu and Urdu instruction and digital access. Review enrolment-to-completion, foundational skills and girls’ transitions to higher classes rather than relying solely on physical school construction.

## 13 Health policy: public systems, insurance and prevention

### Meaning and policy background

**Health as system, not only treatment.** Public health policy spans prevention, primary care, disease surveillance, maternal and child services, workforce, pharmaceuticals and financial risk protection. National Health Policy 2017 seeks universal access to quality care while reducing financial hardship. Ayushman Bharat includes Health and Wellness Centre / Ayushman Arogya Mandir primary-care interventions and PM-JAY financial protection for eligible hospital care; these components are related but distinct.

### Legal and institutional mechanisms

Insurance reimbursement can finance hospital episodes but does not substitute for immunisation, safe drinking water, nutrition, outpatient care or workforce distribution. National Health Mission supports service-system strengthening, while disease programmes target tuberculosis, vector-borne illness and other priority burdens. Good policy uses outcome indicators and quality audits while controlling unnecessary procedures and medicine shortages.

### Telangana application, challenges and outcome measurement

Telangana-specific discussion may include public hospitals, Basti Dawakhanas, urban primary care, Aarogyasri state arrangements and fluorosis prevention. Eligibility, benefit ceilings and integration between schemes change over time and require a current official order before numerical claims are used.

## 14 Poverty alleviation: income, employment and public goods

### Meaning and policy background

**Multidimensional deprivation.** Income poverty, consumption poverty and multidimensional poverty capture different shortfalls. A household may have income above a threshold while lacking sanitation, education or health access. Poverty alleviation therefore uses several instruments: employment guarantee, food security, housing, livelihoods and essential services. The current VB–G RAM G Act guarantee is employment-based and demand-driven, with 125 days per eligible rural household per financial year from 1 July 2026; PMAY housing support is a distinct asset policy.

### Legal and institutional mechanisms

National Food Security Act, 2013 creates eligible food entitlements, with identification and distribution problems requiring operational safeguards. Self-help groups and livelihood missions attempt credit and market access, but debt or repeated training without viable demand is not sustainable income generation. DBT can reduce some leakages but should retain transparent alternatives and functioning grievance systems.

### Telangana application, challenges and outcome measurement

Telangana evaluation needs village-level access to work, wage delays, household food security, rural housing quality and urban informal-labour support. Crucially, programme success is lower vulnerability and sustained improvements—not only the amount disbursed or the number of administrative approvals.

## 15 Telangana welfare architecture and comparative evaluation

### Meaning and policy background

**Federal roles and accountability.** National, state and local governments play different roles in social provision. Union schemes may be centrally sponsored, central-sector or statutory; state policies may add benefits, reshape delivery or implement distinct eligibility criteria. A name such as Mahalakshmi, Aasara, Indiramma or Aarogyasri identifies a policy family, not a timeless guaranteed benefit amount. Always record date, eligibility, government order and whether the initiative is announced, operational or changed.

### Legal and institutional mechanisms

A high-quality policy comparison includes target population, legal basis, implementing department, delivery channel, exclusion risk, budget sustainability and outcomes. For Telangana examine women’s mobility and livelihoods, caste- and tribe-linked residential education, Anganwadi quality, senior pensions, rural and urban housing, disability access, primary healthcare and environmental conditions together. Intersections matter: a tribal girl with a disability may need coordinated transport, teaching support, documentation and protection services across agencies.

### Telangana application, challenges and outcome measurement

Conclude with five operational reforms: publish disaggregated programme data and grievance outcomes; periodically audit exclusion and wrongful denial; maintain accessible offline help; strengthen local government and frontline staff; and link cash transfers to high-quality public services. The most defensible mains conclusion is that welfare succeeds when entitlements become reliable, dignified capabilities.

## Detailed case study A — From a woman’s safety complaint to enforceable support

### The service chain

Consider a woman facing coercive control and violence while also depending economically on the household. A policy answer must not simply state that a helpline exists. At the first stage she needs safe, confidential information in a language she understands and a choice about contacting police. A One Stop Centre may provide an entry point for coordinated support; legal remedies under the Domestic Violence Act can include protection and residence-related orders according to the facts and procedure. If the complaint concerns sexual harassment in employment, the POSH Act has a distinct internal-committee or local-committee architecture. Conflating the two mechanisms creates incorrect mains analysis.

### Root causes and policy failures

Patriarchal control, economic dependency, threats to children, inaccessible transport, victim blaming and weak institutional follow-through can prevent reporting. Even a formally functioning programme is ineffective if protection is delayed, staff lack training, interpretation is unavailable or follow-up violates confidentiality. A limited focus on prosecutions also misses housing, counselling, childcare and livelihood needs. To test effectiveness, ask whether the survivor remains safe, exercises informed choice and obtains stable access to work and education.

### Telangana application and evaluation

District service mapping should identify referral paths between health facilities, protection officers, police, legal-services authorities, shelters and livelihood programmes. Compare access in Hyderabad with travel barriers in remote mandals. Track waiting times, case progression, repeated violence and the availability of interpreters. Avoid presenting registration counts as prevalence: stronger trust can increase reporting even if incidence is unchanged. Policy success is survivor-centred, rights-protecting and accountable.

## Detailed case study B — Tribal development, territorial rights and education

### Beyond a generic welfare package

A forest-dependent family may be eligible for a school hostel or health outreach but still lack secure access to customary forest resources. The Fifth Schedule provides a special constitutional governance framework for Scheduled Areas; PESA seeks to strengthen self-governance within its scope, while FRA recognises certain individual and community forest rights following statutory procedures. Schooling, livelihoods, land security and self-government are interconnected and cannot be assessed as one scholarship programme.

### Implementation mechanism

The Gram Sabha’s role, community claims, document availability and transparent decision-making matter for forest-rights recognition. A rejected claim may reflect evidentiary difficulties, administrative interpretation or a substantive failure to establish eligibility; policy design requires a fair opportunity to submit evidence and appeal or review according to law. Development projects introduce a further question: have affected people been heard, has displacement been minimised, and will rehabilitation restore livelihoods rather than supply temporary compensation?

### Telangana-specific dimensions

For Gond, Koya, Kolam and Chenchu communities, settlement patterns, ecological reliance and language needs differ. Multilingual education with locally appropriate materials can help first-generation learners, while poorly planned hostel relocation may interrupt family relationships. Health access depends on transport, trained staff, medicines and trust, not merely building a clinic. A mains answer should distinguish each community and avoid implying uniform administrative status across every district.

### Outcome-oriented assessment

Useful indicators include secure community-resource governance, reduction of wrongful claim rejection, regular school attendance with learning attainment, seasonal access to healthcare and participation in local decisions. The policy goal is equal citizenship and institutional agency, rather than cultural assimilation presented as development.

## Detailed case study C — Telangana’s Musi basin as a social-policy problem

### Why this is not just an environmental problem

The Musi River catchment combines urban runoff, altered lake networks, sewage discharge, industrial effluent risks and occupation of vulnerable areas. These are also questions of settlement inequality, land use, public health and local government accountability. Poorer households often face higher exposure to unsafe housing locations and fewer means of relocating voluntarily. Reducing pollution or flood risk without planning for livelihood and housing protections may shift harms rather than eliminate them.

### Distinguish the policy instruments

Water-quality standards and consent conditions target specific polluting activities. Sewage interception and treatment require capital works plus continued operation and maintenance. Flood-risk management relies on rainfall monitoring, preserving drainage corridors and evaluating lake interconnections. Rehabilitation requires fair identification of affected persons, consultation, housing access and livelihood continuity. An EIA addresses defined environmental impacts of specified projects; it is not a substitute for catchment-wide cumulative risk assessment.

### Evaluating success

A restored river must demonstrate improved monitored water quality over time, reduced untreated wastewater discharge, preserved lake storage, fewer households exposed to flooding and fair treatment of relocated households. Investment size, photograph-based beautification and announcements are not sufficient indicators. In a structured mains answer, use headings such as Background, Causes, Legal Instruments, Social Impacts, Implementation Challenges and Conclusion, linking environmental governance to social justice.

## Detailed case study D — Why poverty programmes require complementary public services

### A household pathway

A rainfed agricultural labour household may face volatile wages, medical expenses and irregular access to affordable food. The VB–G RAM G employment guarantee can stabilise a portion of rural wage income when work is demanded and provided under legal conditions. NFSA food entitlements help protect nutrition; primary healthcare can prevent illness-driven debt; residential schooling and transport may support children’s continuation. Housing support reduces shelter insecurity, but can fail if the location is disconnected from work or utilities.

### Risks of fragmentation

Programme databases may use inconsistent names or household definitions. Migrant families can face residence-linked exclusions; digital-only workflows can burden older people and persons with disabilities. Wage delays reduce employment guarantee value, while poor job quality and weak local asset planning may diminish durable gains. Similarly, access to subsidised food cannot guarantee nutritional diversity if meals lack proteins and micronutrients. These are structural weaknesses, not mere awareness deficits.

### Telangana-focused reforms

At Gram Panchayat and urban ward levels, link grievance officers, frontline workers and programme records without assuming a single identifier always proves entitlement. Monitor the time from application to benefit, reasons for rejection, wage-payment timeliness, learning progress and out-of-pocket medical costs. Community organisations and women’s groups can help identify gaps if privacy is protected. The strongest conclusion is that reducing multidimensional poverty needs predictable income support together with reliable schools, healthcare, water and accountable administration.

## Integrative comparison: match the problem to its instrument

| Policy problem | Principal intervention | Typical exclusion risk | Better outcome indicator |
|---|---|---|---|
| Violence against women | OSCs, helplines, police and legal protection | Unsafe reporting; inaccessible services | Response time and survivor-centred remedy |
| Child undernutrition | Anganwadi nutrition and healthcare | Irregular services or erroneous registration | Anthropometric improvement and dietary diversity |
| Tribal land insecurity | FRA recognition and Gram Sabha process | Delayed claims or improper rejection | Recognised, secure rights with due process |
| Disability exclusion | Reasonable accommodation and accessibility | Inaccessible documents, tests or buildings | Usable facilities and retained employment |
| Rural income shock | VB–G RAM G and livelihoods services | Late wages and non-provision of requested work | Days of timely paid work and income stability |
| Urban river pollution | Sewage, industrial effluent and catchment management | Fragmented agencies and diffuse sources | Monitored water quality and flood-risk reduction |

## Answer-writing method

For 10 marks, keep roughly 200 words but ensure visible headings and at least four discriminating factual anchors. State an evaluative judgment in the introduction or conclusion, and show the distinction between policy intent and delivered outcome.

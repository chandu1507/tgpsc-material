# TGPSC P3 U2 T1 — Detailed Notes

## 4. Detailed textbook-grade notes

### Concept A. Colonial constitutional evolution: inherited machinery versus democratic sovereignty

#### Background: the colonial state was a constitutional laboratory, not a democracy

The Constitution did not emerge from a single event in 1949. Colonial enactments gradually developed offices, courts, legislative procedures, public-service systems and centre–province relations that independent India later retained, modified or rejected. This history matters because a familiar institutional form can serve different political purposes. A district collector under the Raj was an instrument of imperial administration; a collector under the Republic is constitutionally subject to elected governments, judicial review, statutory rights and public accountability. Similarly, the 1935 Act supplied administrative techniques without supplying the Constitution's democratic legitimacy. The analytical task is to separate **continuity of machinery** from **change in the source and purpose of power**.

The Regulating Act of 1773 responded to Company misgovernment by placing Bengal's administration under a Governor-General and council and providing for a Supreme Court at Calcutta. Pitt's India Act, 1784 established a dual arrangement of Company administration and British governmental supervision. The Charter Act of 1833 consolidated legislative centralisation and renamed the Governor-General of Bengal as Governor-General of India. The 1858 Act ended East India Company rule after the revolt of 1857, transferring governance to the Crown. None of these measures recognised sovereignty of the Indian people; their governing question was how imperial power could be supervised, coordinated and defended.

#### Councils, representation and the problem of accountability

The Indian Councils Acts of 1861 and 1892 modestly enlarged consultation, but the executive remained substantially unanswerable to Indian representatives. The 1909 Morley–Minto reforms expanded council membership and introduced separate Muslim electorates, institutionalising a form of communal political representation. Supporters described separate electorates as minority safeguards; critics argued that they encouraged communities to bargain as separate political blocs and strengthened colonial divide-and-rule practices. A serious answer should not pretend that all minority protection is inherently communal: the constitutional question is which design protects vulnerable groups while sustaining equal citizenship and common political institutions.

The Government of India Act, 1919 introduced **dyarchy in provinces**: transferred subjects were handled by ministers responsible to legislatures, while reserved subjects remained under governors and executive councillors. The arrangement exposed the tension between ministerial responsibility and executive veto. Restricted property- and qualification-based franchise meant that even representative institutions remained socially narrow. Nationalist criticism therefore targeted both the quantum of self-government and the exclusion of most adults from political participation.

The Government of India Act, 1935 was the largest pre-independence constitutional framework. It provided provincial autonomy, envisaged an all-India federation and distributed subjects into federal, provincial and concurrent lists. The proposed federation never operated in its intended form because the requisite princely-state accessions did not materialise. Provincial ministries nevertheless acquired significant experience in legislation and administration after the 1937 elections. The 1935 Act influenced the Constitution's federal lists, public-service institutions, emergency design and gubernatorial arrangements. But it is misleading to say the Constitution simply copied the Act: adult franchise, enforceable rights, a republican head of state and accountability to the people mark a fundamental break.

#### Nationalist alternatives and the route to an elected constitution-making body

Indian political thought offered alternatives to piecemeal imperial concessions. The Nehru Report of 1928 proposed a dominion-status constitutional scheme with rights protections; debates over minority representation exposed unresolved questions about the political community. The 1931 Karachi Resolution linked civil liberties with socio-economic commitments, foreshadowing the later combination of Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles. Nationalists increasingly insisted that Indians, rather than Westminster, should make India's constitutional rules. The August Offer of 1940 and Cripps proposals of 1942 acknowledged versions of post-war constitution-making but failed to produce agreement over authority and unity.

The Cabinet Mission Plan of 1946 created the practical route to the Constituent Assembly. Its original scheme tried to reconcile a common Indian union with provincial groupings and substantial provincial autonomy. Partition transformed that context: the Assembly had to establish a workable Union, integrate princely States and confront mass displacement while drafting rules for a plural society. The institutional choices of 1946–49 were therefore responses to real security, integration and social-justice problems, not abstract borrowing exercises.

#### Evaluation and Telangana application

The colonial inheritance produced a capable but centralised administrative state. Retaining district administration, all-India services and central legislative tools could help build capacity across a vast new Republic; the same choices could perpetuate hierarchy, secrecy and a bias toward the executive. Telangana offers a concrete lens: the constitutional power to reorganise States and the continuing role of centrally created services illustrate the interaction of inherited administrative centralisation with democratic regional demands. A high-quality answer should specify whether it is discussing a **colonial Act**, a **Constitutional Article**, or a **post-independence statute**, because each has a different source of legal authority. ### Concept B. Constituent Assembly: authority, representativeness and decision-making

#### Origin and composition

The Constituent Assembly first met on 9 December 1946 under the Cabinet Mission framework. It was not elected by universal adult suffrage: members from British Indian provinces were elected indirectly by provincial legislative assemblies, while princely States had their own arrangements for representation. The initial planned strength was 389; Partition reduced the effective size of the body that framed independent India's Constitution to about 299. The indirect election and limited franchise create a genuine democratic objection. Yet representativeness cannot be judged by electoral origin alone: one must also examine the range of political positions voiced, the openness of debate, the publicity of draft proposals and the democratic institutions established for the future.

Rajendra Prasad became permanent President of the Assembly on 11 December 1946. Jawaharlal Nehru moved the Objectives Resolution on 13 December 1946; it was adopted on 22 January 1947. This Resolution committed the Assembly to an independent sovereign republic, authority derived from the people, justice, equality, freedom and safeguards for minorities and disadvantaged groups. The eventual Preamble drew upon this moral programme but was not a verbatim reproduction. The Assembly had to decide how political freedom would coexist with caste inequality, economic deprivation, religious diversity and the territorial anxieties intensified by Partition.

#### Committee system and the difference between proposals and final law

The Assembly divided specialised work among committees. Nehru chaired the Union Powers Committee and Union Constitution Committee. Vallabhbhai Patel chaired the Provincial Constitution Committee and the Advisory Committee on Fundamental Rights, Minorities and Tribal and Excluded Areas. The latter included specialist subcommittees, such as the Fundamental Rights Sub-Committee under J. B. Kripalani. Committee reports did not automatically become constitutional text. They were discussed, altered or rejected by the full Assembly, then translated into draft articles and debated again. This sequence is essential to evaluating the role of the Drafting Committee: neither an expert committee nor its chair possessed a free-standing mandate to impose a Constitution.

#### Deliberation, dissent and compromise

Debates addressed whether the Union should be weak or strong, whether minority safeguards should use separate electorates, how property and land reform should coexist with individual rights, and whether social and economic commitments should be judicially enforceable. The Assembly ultimately rejected separate communal electorates while retaining certain forms of affirmative representation. It adopted enforceable civil-political rights and non-justiciable Directive Principles as complementary constitutional techniques. Members including Hansa Mehta, Dakshayani Velayudhan, Jaipal Singh Munda and others raised concerns about gender equality, caste hierarchy, tribal self-government and substantive democracy; it is inaccurate to portray the proceedings as the work of a single leader or homogeneous elite.

Some compromises had costs. The strong Centre was justified by fears of fragmentation, external threat and the immediate experience of Partition; it also created enduring Union–State tensions. Administrative continuity could secure order, but it also left colonial policing and bureaucratic habits requiring later reform. The Constitution's original property-rights settlement generated conflicts with redistributive land reform, leading to early amendments and litigation. These are examples of constitutionalism as institutional conflict management rather than an assumption that every problem was settled in 1949.

#### Democratic legitimacy: a balanced assessment

A fair assessment acknowledges the Assembly's restricted electoral base and the absence of direct universal-franchise election. It also recognises that the adopted Constitution introduced universal adult franchise at the Republic's birth, a radical move in a poor, unequal and largely non-literate society. Public discussion, clause-by-clause scrutiny and a range of ideological disagreements strengthened deliberative legitimacy. The most defensible position is neither to dismiss the Assembly as illegitimate nor to treat its decisions as beyond criticism. Its legitimacy rested partly on creating a framework through which later generations could contest and revise constitutional choices.

#### Telangana illustration

The subsequent creation of Telangana in 2014 shows that the territorial arrangement adopted by the Assembly was deliberately adaptable. Article 3 provides Parliament with power to reorganise States, while requiring a presidential recommendation and referral to the affected State legislature for its views. The State legislature does not hold a constitutional veto. This can accommodate regional identity and administrative claims, but critics worry that the procedure underprotects the autonomy of existing States. The debate reflects the original Assembly's balance between a durable Union and changing territorial demands. [S1, S2, S4]

### Concept C. The Drafting Committee: legal craftsmanship and its institutional limits

#### Formation, membership and preparatory material

On 29 August 1947 the Constituent Assembly appointed a seven-member Drafting Committee. Its original members were B. R. Ambedkar, Alladi Krishnaswami Ayyar, N. Gopalaswami Ayyangar, K. M. Munshi, Syed Mohammad Saadulla, B. L. Mitter and D. P. Khaitan. Ambedkar chaired the Committee. Mitter was replaced by N. Madhava Rau; following Khaitan's death, T. T. Krishnamachari joined. The exact names matter in objective examinations, but the larger point is institutional: a committee of legal specialists converted political choices into enforceable and administrable provisions.

Constitutional Adviser **B. N. Rau** gathered comparative material and prepared an initial draft for the Assembly's office. He was not a member of the Drafting Committee. Treating Rau, Ambedkar and the Assembly as interchangeable obscures their distinct contributions. Rau offered comparative and drafting foundations; the Drafting Committee revised and systematised; the full Assembly retained the authority to accept, alter or reject the text. The official debate of 29 August 1947 is particularly useful because the resolution itself described the Committee's function as scrutinising a draft based on decisions already taken in the Assembly.

#### What constitutional drafting actually required

The Committee did far more than edit grammar. It had to connect principles that could pull in different directions: individual liberty and public order; equality and affirmative action; elected government and administrative continuity; national integration and provincial autonomy; parliamentary lawmaking and judicial review. A Constitution cannot merely promise rights; it must identify duty-bearers, legal procedures, exceptions, institutional jurisdictions and remedies. For example, a right to equality requires not only a statement of principle but a workable relationship between non-discrimination, public employment, reservations and judicial scrutiny. Federalism requires lists of legislative subjects, rules for conflicting laws, fiscal arrangements and emergency procedures.

The Committee's work also involved anticipating litigation. Broad concepts such as liberty, equality and reasonableness need text capable of guiding courts without eliminating legislative choice. Over-detailed constitutional drafting can reduce adaptability; excessive generality can transfer difficult decisions to judges. The resulting Indian Constitution is relatively detailed because the framers sought to stabilise a complex transition and pre-empt some institutional disputes. Its length is therefore both a governance resource and a source of rigidity or complexity, depending on the issue.

#### Ambedkar's distinctive contribution

Ambedkar's importance lies in legal reasoning, management of the drafting process and insistence that formal political equality would be hollow without social democracy. In his closing address of 25 November 1949, he warned of contradictions between political equality and continuing social-economic inequality, and emphasised constitutional methods and safeguards against personality cults. These were not decorative speeches: they identified risks to constitutional survival that legislation alone could not solve. A constitutional order depends on political parties, officials, judges and citizens respecting restraints even when formal text permits aggressive conduct.

Ambedkar also defended an institutional design that could operate as federal in ordinary times yet give the Union substantial authority in emergencies. His position should not be reduced to an uncritical endorsement of centralisation; he recognised the need for a working constitutional order capable of responding to instability. Likewise, the Assembly did not simply accept every committee preference. Debates over minority rights, executive power and the role of the judiciary continued after draft publication.

#### Criticism, accountability and a method for exam answers

One criticism is that an expert committee might have insulated political decisions from democratic scrutiny. The 29 August debate records precisely this anxiety: members demanded that the Assembly remain free to revisit both text and principle. The counterargument is that expert legal drafting was indispensable to turn hundreds of political decisions into a coherent constitutional instrument. The correct evaluation separates **expertise in formulating legal language** from **authority to make constitutional choices**. The Committee was indispensable but subordinate to the Assembly's deliberative process.

For a mains answer, describe four stages: committee reports and Assembly principles; Rau's preparatory draft; Drafting Committee's revised legal text; and Assembly's clause-by-clause deliberation and adoption. This avoids the two opposite errors of calling Ambedkar the sole author or denying his exceptional contribution. Telangana's constitutional administration illustrates why this distinction remains practical: lawyers, officials and expert commissions can recommend institutional arrangements, but the legally empowered body must take the final decision under the Constitution. ### Concept D. Constitutional philosophy and the Preamble: ends, means and interpretation

#### Popular sovereignty and the character of the Republic

The Preamble begins with the people as the source of constitutional authority. This is the sharpest break with colonial governance: governmental power is no longer justified by imperial statute or dynastic title, but by a Constitution enacted in the people's name. **Sovereign** means the Republic possesses independent constitutional authority, subject to obligations it voluntarily undertakes under international law; it does not imply freedom from every international commitment. **Democratic** refers to government ultimately answerable to the people through representative institutions, political competition and rights. **Republic** means the head of state is not a hereditary monarch. These terms are related but not synonymous: an elected head of state does not by itself establish meaningful democracy.

The Preamble's moral vocabulary is deliberately interconnected. **Justice** is social, economic and political; social justice confronts entrenched caste, gender and other hierarchies, economic justice addresses material opportunity and deprivation, and political justice requires effective equal citizenship. **Liberty** includes thought, expression, belief, faith and worship. **Equality** concerns status and opportunity rather than an assurance that all outcomes will be identical. **Fraternity** requires a civic bond compatible with individual dignity and national unity. A policy may advance one value while putting pressure on another; constitutional reasoning explains how institutions mediate that conflict.

#### Social transformation versus restrained government

The Constitution is often described as transformative because it aims to change inherited social relations, not merely restrain the State. Article 17 abolishes untouchability; equality guarantees and affirmative-action provisions address exclusion; Directive Principles orient public action toward welfare, education, health and material justice. At the same time, fundamental rights constrain majoritarian and administrative overreach. The tension is productive when institutions make trade-offs transparently and reviewably. A welfare policy that improves access but arbitrarily excludes eligible people may offend equality; an absolute insistence on formal sameness can ignore historical disadvantage. Exam answers should distinguish **formal equality** from **substantive equality** and show how both appear in constitutional practice.

The terms **socialist**, **secular** and **integrity** were inserted in the Preamble by the 42nd Amendment in 1976. Their insertion date is important, but it does not mean that before 1976 the Constitution lacked all social-justice or religious-freedom commitments. Equality, freedom of religion, Directive Principles and the non-theocratic structure were already part of the constitutional settlement. Indian secularism is not adequately described as either hostility to religion or automatic equal funding of all religions. It combines individual freedom of conscience, non-discrimination and a State capable of reforming secular aspects of religious institutions within constitutional limits. Disputes over personal law, educational institutions and religious freedom show why the concept remains contested.

#### Is the Preamble enforceable law?

The Preamble forms part of the Constitution and is an important interpretive guide, as recognised in the basic-structure jurisprudence. It is not an independent source of legislative power and does not by itself create a remedy that overrides the operative Articles. Its value is especially visible when courts must choose among plausible interpretations of ambiguous constitutional provisions. The Preamble cannot be invoked to ignore explicit constitutional text; equally, the text should not be read as if the Preamble's commitments were meaningless decoration.

The objectives of liberty and social justice sometimes generate competing accounts of judicial review. One approach emphasises restraint and the democratic legitimacy of elected legislators. Another argues that courts must enforce constitutional morality against popular majorities when rights and institutional checks are threatened. Both have risks: excessive judicial control can displace democratic choice, while excessive deference can hollow out rights. A disciplined answer identifies the constitutional provision, the actual rights affected and the institutional competence of the decision-maker instead of simply declaring one branch superior.

#### Telangana illustrations and contemporary relevance

Telangana's statehood debates invoke dignity, political voice, regional equality and equitable access to public resources. These are consonant with the Preamble's justice and democratic-participation values, but statehood itself was legally effected through Articles 3 and 4 and the 2014 Reorganisation Act. The distinction between **moral justification** and **legal mechanism** is exam-critical. Similarly, policies for disadvantaged communities must be evaluated for real access and procedural fairness, not solely by their stated social-justice aims. [S2, S5, S6]

### Concept E. Salient features: a negotiated constitutional architecture

#### Written supremacy, republicanism and parliamentary accountability

India has a written, supreme Constitution that allocates governmental powers and provides standards for their review. Parliamentary government places the effective executive in a Council of Ministers collectively responsible to the directly elected Lok Sabha at Union level and to the Legislative Assembly at State level. The President and Governors are constitutional heads with specified functions, while ministers normally exercise political executive authority. This differs from a presidential system in which executive tenure is independently fixed. Parliamentary accountability enables legislative questioning and loss-of-confidence mechanisms; its weakness is that a disciplined majority may dominate the legislature and reduce scrutiny unless committees, opposition rights and public transparency function well.

The Constitution establishes a single citizenship, universal adult franchise and an independent Election Commission for elections within its constitutional mandate. Universal franchise was adopted from the start rather than introduced only after a long period of property-based voting. That choice converted a deeply unequal society into a political community of equal adult voters, although unequal wealth, information and social power still affect actual political influence. The distinction between **one person, one vote** and **equal effective voice** is useful in questions about constitutional democracy.

#### Federalism with a strong Union

Article 1 describes India as a Union of States. Legislative powers are divided through the Seventh Schedule's Union, State and Concurrent Lists; Article 246 structures their distribution. Article 254 addresses inconsistency between Union and State laws on concurrent subjects, subject to its conditions and exceptions. Fiscal transfers, centrally sponsored programmes, the Finance Commission and intergovernmental forums shape practical autonomy beyond the formal lists. The Union has significant powers in emergencies and in certain State-related matters. Thus India is federal in the distribution of constitutionally recognised powers but not a compact of sovereign States with a unilateral right to secede.

The practical merits of this model include common markets, national rights protections and coordination during disasters or economic shocks. Its risks include central overreach, financial dependency and diminished space for local experimentation. The correct response is not to classify India as wholly unitary or perfectly symmetric federalism. One must ask which subject is involved, which Article governs it and what procedural safeguards apply. State autonomy varies by subject and institutional setting.

#### Rights, Directive Principles and constitutional remedies

Part III provides enforceable Fundamental Rights, including equality, freedoms, protection of life and personal liberty, religious freedom and constitutional remedies. Part IV provides Directive Principles that are not enforceable by courts as such but are fundamental in governance and guide lawmaking. Part IVA sets out Fundamental Duties. The distinction is not rights versus welfare: rights may require positive state action, and welfare measures must respect rights. Article 32 empowers Supreme Court remedies for Fundamental Rights, while Article 226 permits High Court writs for Fundamental Rights and for other purposes. The High Courts' broader writ jurisdiction is a frequent objective-exam distinction.

Independent judicial review prevents ordinary legislation and executive action from overriding constitutional limits. It also creates a tension with parliamentary supremacy: elected institutions make policy, but courts determine whether the Constitution permits particular methods. The basic-structure doctrine adds a limit even to formal constitutional amendments. Judicial independence must therefore coexist with judicial accountability, transparent reasoning and institutional restraint.

#### Local self-government and social inclusion

The 73rd and 74th Amendments constitutionalised the basic framework of rural and urban local government through Parts IX and IXA and the Eleventh and Twelfth Schedules. They created a more durable institutional basis for local representation, reservations and periodic elections, but actual authority still depends heavily on State laws, funds, functions and functionaries. In Telangana, the functioning of Gram Panchayats and urban local bodies is therefore a constitutional governance question as well as a State administrative question. Local elections without staff, predictable funds or accountable service delivery do not complete decentralisation.

#### Telangana statehood as a constitutional stress test

Article 3 permits Parliament to create a new State, alter boundaries or change a State's name through an ordinary law after the specified process. The President refers the proposal to the affected State legislature for views, but those views are not binding. Article 4 permits incidental changes to the First and Fourth Schedules without treating the Act as an Article 368 constitutional amendment. The Andhra Pradesh Reorganisation Act, 2014 created Telangana and dealt with representation, institutions, assets and transition arrangements. This shows both constitutional adaptability and the potential controversy when an affected State's elected legislature opposes reorganisation. In an exam, cite the Act and Articles rather than claiming Telangana was created by a constitutional amendment. [S2, S7]

### Concept F. Basic structure: the constitutional identity that amendment cannot destroy

#### The question: can an amendment abolish constitutionalism?

Article 368 confers power to amend the Constitution, but what if a formally valid amendment abolishes competitive elections, judicial review or the ability of future Parliaments to amend? The basic-structure doctrine answers that Parliament's amending power is **constituted and limited**, not the unlimited original authority of a sovereign constitution-making assembly. The doctrine is not a closed list written in one Article. It is a judicially developed limitation derived from the nature and architecture of the Constitution. This distinction matters: an amendment can substantially change institutions without necessarily destroying a basic feature, but a change that extinguishes the feature's essential identity is impermissible.

Early cases framed the problem differently. **Shankari Prasad v. Union of India (1951)** accepted that constitutional amendments could affect Fundamental Rights and distinguished an Article 368 amendment from ordinary law under Article 13. **I. C. Golaknath v. State of Punjab (1967)** restricted Parliament's power to amend Fundamental Rights, intensifying conflict over land reform and redistribution. The **24th Amendment (1971)** affirmed Parliament's constituent power under Article 368 and made presidential assent mandatory. These developments led to the landmark challenge in **Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (24 April 1973)**.

#### Kesavananda: power to amend, not power to destroy

A 13-judge bench decided Kesavananda by a narrow 7–6 majority on the core limitation. The Court accepted that Parliament could amend any part of the Constitution, including Fundamental Rights, but not alter or destroy its basic structure. This rejected both extremes: Fundamental Rights were not absolutely unamendable, and parliamentary power was not limitless. The opinions did not provide one exhaustive catalogue. Frequently recognised features include supremacy of the Constitution, republican and democratic government, secularism, federalism, separation of powers, judicial review, rule of law and the independence of the judiciary. Their precise scope depends on later judgments and the amendment under review.

The distinction between an **ordinary policy choice** and a **structural injury** is critical. Parliament may change electoral procedures, subject to constitutional constraints; it cannot constitutionally eliminate meaningful free and fair elections. It may adjust court jurisdiction in lawful ways; it cannot dismantle judicial review in a manner that destroys the constitutional scheme. Not every adverse effect on a right is a basic-structure violation; the doctrine asks whether a foundational feature has been damaged at the level of constitutional identity.

#### Development after 1973

In **Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975)** the Court applied basic-structure reasoning to provisions seeking to insulate a high-level election dispute from ordinary judicial scrutiny. In **Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980)** it struck down parts of the 42nd Amendment that attempted to make amending power unlimited and disturb the balance between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles. The Court emphasised limited amending power and constitutional harmony rather than a simple hierarchy in which one Part defeats the other. In **I. R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu (2007)** the Court held that laws placed in the Ninth Schedule after 24 April 1973 are subject to basic-structure scrutiny through the impact on relevant rights and principles. In the **NJAC case (2015)** the 99th Amendment was invalidated because the majority found that the new judicial appointments architecture impaired judicial independence; critics argued that the decision entrenched insufficiently accountable judicial self-selection. This is a useful example of a contested application rather than a slogan.

#### Justification and democratic criticism

Defenders argue that without a substantive limit, a transient supermajority could use amendment procedure to remove the very conditions of future democracy. Basic structure protects constitutional continuity, minorities and the institutions that make peaceful political alternation possible. Critics respond that unelected judges lack a democratically enacted textual list of unamendable features, creating uncertainty and potential judicial overreach. They also question whether courts consistently distinguish constitutional essentials from preferred institutional designs. Both perspectives have force. A careful evaluation asks whether a particular ruling protects the constitutional conditions of democracy or merely substitutes judicial policy preference for legislative judgment.

#### Telangana and federal implications

Federalism is recognised as a basic feature, yet this does not make State boundaries immutable. Article 3 explicitly permits territorial reorganisation; therefore the creation of Telangana does not itself offend federalism. The sharper question is whether procedures and institutional arrangements preserve meaningful State government within the Union. The doctrine does not transform the Article 3 consultation into a State veto. For Telangana mains, connect basic structure to federal distribution of authority, independent courts and representative institutions while distinguishing the constitutional text from normative criticism of how it operates. [S2, S8, S9]

### Concept G. Constitutional amendments: flexibility, entrenchment and political bargaining

#### Article 368: a special lawmaking route

An amendment is a formal change to constitutional text made through a constitutionally prescribed process. Under Article 368, an amendment Bill may be introduced in either House of Parliament. Each House must pass it by a **majority of the total membership of that House** and by **not less than two-thirds of members present and voting**. There is no joint sitting to resolve a deadlock over an Article 368 amendment Bill. Where the proposal concerns specified federal provisions—such as election of the President, the extent of Union and State executive power, certain Supreme Court/High Court provisions, distribution of legislative powers, representation of States in Parliament or Article 368 itself—ratification by legislatures of **not less than half of the States** is additionally required before presentation for presidential assent. The President must give assent to a duly passed amendment Bill.

The phrase 'special majority' is often used loosely. It is not merely two-thirds of those who turn up, because the separate requirement of a majority of the House's **total membership** also applies. Nor does every amendment require State ratification. Conversely, an ordinary parliamentary law that changes the Constitution's First and Fourth Schedules pursuant to Articles 3 and 4 is expressly outside Article 368. Students should learn the constitutional trigger before memorising voting arithmetic.

#### Three different routes that candidates often confuse

First, many constitutional changes require the Article 368 special majority alone. Second, changes to specified federal provisions require that special majority **plus State ratification**. Third, certain constitutional arrangements are alterable by **ordinary parliamentary law** under an express constitutional authorisation; Articles 3 and 4 on State reorganisation are the clearest Telangana example. Calling all three 'constitutional amendments' without procedural distinction leads to wrong MCQ answers. Also distinguish amendments to constitutional text from court interpretations that change constitutional meaning without rewriting words.

#### Major amendments and their constitutional significance

The **1st Amendment (1951)** addressed, among other matters, freedom-of-speech restrictions and land-reform protections, showing the early tension between rights and redistribution. The **7th Amendment (1956)** reorganised State categories and related institutions after the States Reorganisation process. The **24th Amendment (1971)** responded to Golaknath by clarifying Article 368's constituent power. The **42nd Amendment (1976)** made extensive changes during the Emergency, including Preamble terminology and attempts to curtail judicial checks; several provisions were later reversed or invalidated. The **44th Amendment (1978)** strengthened certain safeguards following the Emergency and removed the former Fundamental Right to property, replacing it with constitutional protection under Article 300A. The **73rd and 74th Amendments (1992)** supplied constitutional frameworks for panchayats and municipalities. The **86th Amendment (2002)** introduced Article 21A concerning education of children aged six to fourteen. The **101st Amendment (2016)** created the constitutional framework for Goods and Services Tax and the GST Council. The **103rd Amendment (2019)** introduced EWS reservation provisions; the Supreme Court upheld it in **Janhit Abhiyan (2022)** by majority. The **106th Amendment (2023)** provided for reservation of seats for women in Lok Sabha, State Legislative Assemblies and Delhi's Assembly, subject to its census/delimitation-linked implementation mechanism.

#### 2026 current-affairs application: a defeated Bill is not law

In April 2026 the Union government introduced the proposed **Constitution (131st Amendment) Bill, 2026** as part of a package relating to delimitation and implementation of women's legislative reservation. The Bill failed to secure the required constitutional majority in the Lok Sabha on **17 April 2026**. The related proposals were not taken forward. It would be factually wrong to describe the proposal as an enacted 131st Amendment. This episode is valuable because it demonstrates Article 368's special-majority requirement in action: even a government with a substantial ordinary legislative majority may lack the votes needed to alter constitutional fundamentals. Political disagreement concerned the method and timing of delimitation, distribution of representation among States and the implementation path for women's representation; it should not be flattened into a simple pro-women versus anti-women binary.

#### Amendment versus basic structure: two gates, not one

An amendment must pass a **procedural gate** (Article 368 and any necessary State ratification) and a **substantive gate** (it must not damage the basic structure). Passing by an overwhelming majority cannot save a measure that destroys a basic feature. Equally, a socially desirable proposal that lacks the prescribed majority cannot become constitutional law. This dual control balances democratic change with constitutional continuity. It can be criticised as giving courts substantial final authority, but it also prevents the rules of democratic competition from being abolished by those temporarily holding office.

#### Telangana applications and policy consequences

The formation of Telangana is the strongest local comparison: the **Andhra Pradesh Reorganisation Act, 2014** was an ordinary parliamentary Act operating under Articles 3 and 4, not a constitutional amendment under Article 368. By contrast, an alteration to constitutionally protected federal powers within Article 368's ratification proviso would require a different process. The **101st Amendment** and GST Council also illustrate how federal financial relations can be reshaped by constitutional agreement, while practical debates continue over State fiscal room and intergovernmental bargaining. A useful administrative lesson is that constitutional flexibility does not mean any policy announcement can override the text: officials must identify the correct legislative competence, procedure and judicial limits before implementation.

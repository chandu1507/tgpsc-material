## 2. Topic summary: argument in one view

India's Constitution is simultaneously an outcome of colonial institutional experience, an anti-colonial demand for self-government and a transformative social charter. The Government of India Act, 1935 supplied much administrative scaffolding, but the Constitution displaced imperial sovereignty with popular sovereignty, universal adult franchise, enforceable fundamental rights, parliamentary accountability and an independent judiciary. The Constituent Assembly—not the Drafting Committee alone—made the final political choices; the Committee under B. R. Ambedkar translated committee decisions and debates into legal text, aided by Constitutional Adviser B. N. Rau. The Preamble announces the moral destination: justice, liberty, equality and fraternity within a sovereign democratic republic; later amendments added the express words socialist, secular and integrity. The Constitution combines federal division of powers with a comparatively strong Union, judicially enforceable rights with non-justiciable Directive Principles, and flexibility with entrenchment. Article 368 allows constitutional change through prescribed majorities and, for specified federal subjects, ratification by at least half the States; the basic-structure doctrine prevents destruction of the constitutional identity even through procedurally valid amendments. Telangana's creation by the Andhra Pradesh Reorganisation Act, 2014 demonstrates an important distinction: reorganisation under Articles 3 and 4 is accomplished by ordinary parliamentary law and is not an Article 368 amendment. The best mains answers explain why an institution or rule exists, identify its legal basis, trace a concrete dispute and weigh the costs of alternative designs.

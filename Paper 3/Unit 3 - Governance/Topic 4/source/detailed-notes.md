## 1. What counts as development? Growth, capabilities, redistribution and sustainability

### A concept with competing standards

Economic growth is an increase in real output, normally measured by inflation-adjusted GDP or GSDP. Development is broader: it concerns whether people live longer, learn, enjoy secure livelihoods, exercise choices and have effective voice over decisions affecting them. The distinction matters because a fast-growing Hyderabad services economy can coexist with informal settlements, unreliable water, unprotected platform workers and educational inequalities. Conversely, improved immunisation or girls' school completion can represent substantial development even before it appears as a dramatic increase in income. A mains answer must state **what is being measured, for whom, over what period, and with what distribution**.

The conventional modernisation perspective argues that investment, infrastructure, urbanisation, technology and structural movement from low-productivity agriculture to higher-productivity industry and services raise incomes and fiscal capacity. Its strength is recognising the productive foundation required to finance welfare. Its weakness is assuming benefits will trickle down without attention to bargaining power, ownership and unequal access. A political-economy or dependency perspective asks who controls capital, land, intellectual property and supply chains; it draws attention to extraction and regional asymmetry, but can underestimate local agency and the value of trade. The human-development and capability approach, associated with Amartya Sen, treats health, education, bodily security and freedom as ends rather than mere inputs into output. The sustainable-development approach adds intergenerational limits: a project that raises present output while destroying groundwater or raising flood exposure may reduce future welfare.

### From measurement to administrative decisions

Indicators encode political choices. Per-capita income averages conceal concentration; the Human Development Index combines health, schooling and income but does not by itself explain caste exclusion or ecological stress. Multidimensional poverty measures overlapping deprivations; the SDG framework links social, economic and environmental targets. NITI Aayog's **SDG India Index 2023–24**, published in July 2024, reports that India's composite score increased from **66 in 2020–21 to 71 in 2023–24**. These are index scores, not percentage shares of citizens who have achieved all goals. Their interpretation depends on the chosen indicators, normalisation and comparability across editions [S03].

Consider a district reporting 100% tap connections. This is an *asset/output* measure. Development requires testing whether water is available daily, potable, affordable, safe for women to collect and maintained after construction. Likewise, announcing new hospital beds measures capacity, whereas avoidable mortality, financial hardship and referral completion measure outcomes. Indicators must be disaggregated by rural/urban residence, gender, caste, disability and remote habitation, with privacy safeguards. Administrative dashboards are useful but cannot substitute for household verification and public hearings.

### Telangana's development dilemma

Telangana's technology and pharmaceutical clusters create skilled employment and revenue, but the gains do not automatically flow to dryland farmers, migrant workers or Scheduled Tribe habitations. A state can use growth-generated revenue to finance primary care, school quality, social protection and climate-resilient water systems. Yet poorly targeted subsidies, land-use decisions and pollution can erode that same fiscal and ecological base. The correct policy debate is not “growth versus welfare” as mutually exclusive choices; it is about **sequencing, incidence, financing and accountability**. Ask whether a programme increases the capabilities of those least able to purchase alternatives and whether its benefits survive a change of government or contractor.

### Critical appraisal and answer frame

One side prioritises efficient markets, investment and competitive cities; another prioritises redistribution, public provisioning and social rights. Each identifies a genuine failure of the other: a redistributive promise without fiscal resources is fragile, while growth without social protection can intensify exclusion. The synthesis is productive transformation constrained by rights, environmental safeguards and democratic scrutiny. In examinations, avoid treating the number of schemes or the volume of expenditure as proof of success. Connect **inputs → services → outcomes → equity → sustainability**, and mention the counterfactual: what would have happened without the intervention?

## 2. Development processes: planning, institutions, power and implementation

### Development is a process, not a one-time project

A development process begins with diagnosis, not construction. Government identifies a problem, maps affected groups, selects an instrument, finances it, procures or mobilises implementation, monitors outputs, verifies outcomes and provides correction or grievance redress. The sequence is iterative: if a tribal hamlet cannot access a health centre because transport is absent, constructing another building without staffing or referral systems may be the wrong instrument. Planning should begin with a causal theory: **which barrier is preventing the desired outcome and which institution can remove it?**

India moved from Planning Commission-led five-year plans toward NITI Aayog's cooperative-federalism and policy-support role after 2015. Yet constitutional and statutory decentralisation remains essential. Part IX and Part IX-A of the Constitution, introduced by the 73rd and 74th Amendments, recognise rural and urban local institutions. The Eleventh and Twelfth Schedules list potential subjects; they do **not automatically transfer every listed function, employee or rupee**. Actual devolution depends on state legislation, staffing, budget and administrative orders. Article 243ZD envisages district planning committees to consolidate plans of panchayats and municipalities; effective coordination is especially important where expanding urban settlements depend on surrounding villages for water, labour and land.

### Politics of who decides

Technocratic planning promises expertise, economies of scale and standardised quality. Participatory planning seeks local knowledge, legitimacy and protection against elite-defined priorities. Neither is automatically superior. A village meeting can identify a blocked drainage channel invisible to a state dashboard; the same meeting can be dominated by powerful households who silence women or tenants. An engineering design must satisfy safety standards, but residents should be able to influence route, timing, compensation and maintenance. Institutions must therefore combine **expertise, representation and contestability**.

Implementation capacity is a distinct variable. A policy may have a sound objective but fail because departmental data cannot be reconciled, a contractor is paid for incomplete work, a local body lacks engineers, or beneficiaries cannot prove eligibility. Fragmented authority creates “everyone responsible, nobody accountable” outcomes. Good programme design names a lead department, responsible field officer, timeline, funding head, service standard and appeal forum. It also budgets for operation and maintenance, not merely capital expenditure.

### Fiscal and administrative realism

States raise own taxes and receive transfers; local bodies depend on their own revenue and state/central grants. A district-level participatory plan that ignores committed salaries, debt service and maintenance obligations is a wish list. Equally, a centralised plan that ignores local taxes, informal payments and travel costs may overestimate the benefit to the poor. Compare **budgeted allocation, funds released, funds spent, physical completion and verified outcome**. These five figures are different and should never be interchanged.

For Telangana, a Gram Panchayat proposing a community water treatment system must identify whether the rural water supply agency, panchayat, contractor or village committee maintains filters, tests water and pays for electricity. In an urban ward, drainage improvement requires coordination among municipality, utility and road authority; each agency's independent success metric can conceal a system failure. Development administration is the art of aligning these interdependencies.

### Practical evaluation

Use a simple project logic: problem definition; baseline; options and opportunity costs; equity assessment; budget and responsible agency; participation; independent quality checks; grievance resolution; ex-post learning. When discussing success, identify a credible counterfactual or at least compare pre/post trends with known confounders. A rise in school attendance after a transport subsidy may also reflect changes in school calendars or household income. Good administrators ask not only “was the project completed?” but “did the intended group gain a usable capability, at an acceptable social and fiscal cost?”

## 3. The state and provision of services: rights, public goods and delivery architecture

### Provision, financing and production are different decisions

The state can **finance** a service from taxes, **regulate** its standards, **provide** it through a government institution, or **purchase** it from a non-state provider. These functions need not reside in the same organisation. Government may finance a school meal, a women's federation may prepare it, and a school committee may monitor quality. However, outsourcing production does not outsource the state's constitutional responsibility for lawful, equitable and safe administration. An exam answer should separate who pays, who delivers, who sets standards, who verifies performance and who remedies harm.

Public goods in the strict economic sense are non-rival and non-excludable: street lighting or disease surveillance can benefit many people without being easily sold household by household. Education and clinical treatment are often *merit goods* with large positive spillovers and equity justifications; they are not pure public goods in the technical sense. Natural monopolies, such as piped distribution networks, can make parallel competition wasteful. Externalities, information asymmetry and unequal ability to pay justify public intervention. The state also provides collective insurance against shocks that markets may price prohibitively for the poorest.

### Rights-based versus discretionary welfare

Article 21's interpretation protects life and dignity, while Article 21A specifically provides free and compulsory education for children aged six to fourteen. Directive Principles, including Articles 38, 39, 41, 47 and 48A, shape the welfare-state vision but are generally non-justiciable by themselves. Statutory entitlements, such as those under the National Food Security Act, 2013, can create more definite administrative duties. A *scheme* may have changing eligibility and budgets; a *right* may be enforceable through statutory or constitutional procedures. Confusing the two weakens legal analysis. The state must also respect equality under Article 14 and the prohibition of discrimination under Article 15 when designing and administering benefits.

Universal provision can reduce exclusion and stigma, but it is fiscally demanding. Targeted transfers can concentrate scarce funds on vulnerable groups, but identification errors, documentation barriers and discretionary gatekeeping can deny benefits. A digital beneficiary list may remove duplicate records while mistakenly excluding migrant households or persons with mismatched identity documents. Effective design therefore provides accessible offline alternatives, appeal, corrections and published service standards. Efficiency is not achieved by making excluded claimants invisible.

### Service-delivery chain and failure points

Take primary healthcare: finance, facility, doctor availability, drugs, diagnostics, referral transport and follow-up must all function. Counting new facilities does not show whether treatment was completed. In school education, buildings, teacher attendance, learning outcomes, disability access and safe travel form a similar chain. In municipal sanitation, collection, segregation, transport, treatment and safe disposal are interdependent; improving collection without disposal can shift pollution to another community.

Telangana examples include rural drinking-water infrastructure, urban solid-waste services and publicly supported healthcare. They should be assessed with **quality-adjusted service access**, not promotional claims. A household may have a nominal water connection but rely on private tankers in summer. The difference between an announced service and a reliable one determines trust in government and often falls disproportionately on women, who bear unpaid care and water-collection work.

### The accountability bargain

Public delivery faces absenteeism, political interference and weak procurement; private delivery faces profit incentives, cream-skimming and information asymmetry. Hybrid models add contract-management costs. No ownership form guarantees performance. The state should publish eligibility and service standards, use transparent procurement, inspect quality, fund grievance redress and allow legislative and judicial review. The most relevant question is **what governance safeguards make this arrangement serve the public purpose?**

## 4. Public services, fiscal choices and the problem of equity

### Universalism, targeting and progressive universalism

Three models often compete. Universalism gives everyone a baseline entitlement, building political support and reducing administrative exclusion. Targeting reserves resources for identified poor or vulnerable groups and may maximise immediate redistributive effect per rupee, but errors in income assessment can be severe where employment is informal. Progressive universalism guarantees a basic level to all while directing additional spending toward the most disadvantaged, for example universal school enrolment with intensive remedial support in remote tribal schools. The correct choice depends on fiscal space, stigma, administrative capacity and the cost of mistakenly excluding someone.

A benefit's **incidence** differs from its headline cost. A subsidised urban flyover may primarily benefit vehicle owners even though it is publicly funded. A primary-care facility can save poor households catastrophic spending even if its operating budget is smaller. Gender analysis asks whether a transport scheme reduces women's travel time and increases independent mobility, or simply shifts cost without addressing safety. Disability analysis asks whether the physical environment and information systems are usable, not whether a general service nominally exists.

### Subsidies and the political economy of delivery

Subsidies can address externalities, smooth income shocks and make essential services affordable. They can also be captured by better-off groups, encourage inefficient consumption or create arrears to providers. A transparent subsidy states its beneficiary, unit cost, duration, funding source and exit/review criteria. Free electricity to a class of consumers is not costless: the expense appears in government transfers, utility finances or deferred investment. A well-designed policy compares cash transfers, price subsidies and direct public provision against the objective, not ideology.

Public procurement is another distributional mechanism. Bid specifications may exclude small local enterprises even when a contract nominally promotes livelihoods. Conversely, splitting contracts excessively can sacrifice economies of scale or encourage collusion. The public authority should define service outcomes, competition safeguards, conflict-of-interest rules and independent testing. A Telangana municipal waste contract can require segregation rates and worker safety, rather than merely counting vehicles deployed. Payments should follow verified milestones and performance, with transparent deductions for failure.

### Federal and local coordination

Central schemes, state departments, district administrations and local bodies frequently share responsibility. If the state funds an asset but a municipality must maintain it without adequate own-source revenue, the apparent capital success can become an operational failure. State Finance Commissions under Article 243-I and 243-Y recommend local-government resource arrangements; their recommendations and implementation need scrutiny. A functioning Gram Sabha or ward consultation should influence priorities, but must not be a ceremonial signature exercise after the budget is finalised.

### Practical consequences

A good exam illustration is a low-income peri-urban settlement with intermittent water. One option is a capital subsidy for household connections; another is network repair and leakage reduction; a third is targeted tanker support during transition. The first can produce attractive coverage numbers while failing to solve source scarcity. A sound decision tests water quality, continuity, tariff affordability, maintenance, informal-tenure constraints and grievance data. Equity is not a footnote to delivery: it changes which engineering and fiscal option is actually rational.

## 5. State and market: coordination, competition and market failures

### Neither state nor market is a single mechanism

A market coordinates dispersed decisions through prices, contracts and competition. It can reveal demand and reward innovation, but only when consumers have meaningful choices and information, firms face entry and exit, and costs are not shifted onto others. The state sets property and contract rules, supplies public infrastructure, redistributes resources and regulates externalities. A state-owned provider can still buy inputs from markets; a private hospital can still operate under public regulation. “Privatisation” may mean asset sale, contracting, management outsourcing or user-fee recovery—very different instruments with different accountability implications.

Classic market failures include **externalities** (a factory's emissions imposed on neighbours), **public goods** (flood early warning), **natural monopoly** (water pipelines), **information asymmetry** (patients cannot fully assess treatment), **missing insurance markets** (disaster risk), and **coordination failures** (industrial clusters require roads, skills and suppliers together). State failures include weak information, political capture, bureaucratic delay, soft budget constraints and corruption. The choice is between imperfect alternatives, not a perfect market and a perfect government.

### Policy instruments and their limits

Competition law addresses anticompetitive conduct; sector regulators may set tariffs, licensing and quality obligations; environmental consent and liability rules address pollution; public procurement can stimulate innovation or distort it. A subsidy to renewable energy can internalise environmental benefits, but must be periodically evaluated for cost and distribution. Price controls may protect affordability but can produce shortages if suppliers cannot recover efficient costs. Direct public provision can guarantee universal service but requires professional management and transparent budgets.

Industrial policy presents another debate. Proponents argue that public investment and targeted support can overcome coordination failures, build domestic capabilities and create jobs. Critics warn of rent-seeking, protection of inefficient firms and selection of politically connected beneficiaries. A credible industrial policy should specify time-bound performance conditions, competitive selection, export or productivity benchmarks, and independent exit decisions. The question is not whether to intervene, but whether the intervention builds capabilities that outlast the subsidy.

### Telangana illustration: growth corridors and social obligations

Telangana's urban technology economy depends on roads, electricity, skilled labour, land markets, housing and water. Private investment generates employment but can also bid up rents and intensify commuting or flood exposure. Public authorities should plan drainage and public transport before approving high-density development, enforce environmental standards and protect displaced livelihoods. Firms benefit from predictable regulation; citizens benefit when regulatory discretion is limited by public criteria and appeal.

A hypothetical public bus service illustrates the trade-off. If fares alone must cover every cost, socially important low-demand routes may disappear. If the state simply covers all losses, managers may have little incentive to improve punctuality. A service-obligation contract can reimburse verified kilometres or outcomes while retaining safety standards, transparent subsidies and performance penalties. The right contract depends on measurable quality and the public authority's capacity to enforce it.

### Critical synthesis

The state should be strong where rights, coordination and externalities require collective action; markets should be competitive where consumer choice and innovation can work; civil society should provide scrutiny and voice, not a substitute for legal responsibility. Regulatory capture and cronyism are risks at the boundary. Good governance makes the boundary visible through published contracts, beneficial-ownership checks, citizen feedback and independent audit. Development requires *market-shaping capacity* as well as market correction.

## 6. Public choice, regulation and the governance of mixed economies

### Why good intentions are insufficient

Public choice theory applies incentives to political and administrative decisions. Voters face information costs; politicians face election cycles; officials may prioritise budgets, status or risk avoidance; firms lobby for subsidies and favourable rules. These incentives do not prove that public institutions are inevitably corrupt. They explain why good programme design must make performance observable, prevent concentration of discretion and create channels for challenge. An apparently generous policy can transfer resources to organised groups at the expense of dispersed taxpayers or future generations.

Regulatory capture occurs when a regulator becomes overly responsive to the firms it supervises, whether through information dependence, revolving-door employment or political pressure. The converse problem is arbitrary regulation that deters legitimate investment. Independence is therefore paired with transparency: reasons for tariff orders, public consultation, conflict declarations, appeal routes and review of performance. A regulator should not merely collect licences; it should protect competition, safety, access and long-run investment incentives.

### Procurement and contract governance

When a public authority contracts a private operator, the principal–agent problem appears: the principal wants reliable service but cannot continuously observe effort or hidden quality; the agent may optimise what the contract measures. Paying a waste contractor by tonnage collected can reward unnecessary hauling rather than waste reduction. Paying solely by households covered can encourage superficial connections. Contracts should use a balanced scorecard, spot inspections, independent laboratory tests and citizen complaints, with safeguards against manipulation. Performance measures must not create perverse incentives to exclude difficult or expensive users.

Competition for a contract is not the same as competition during the contract. A water utility concession may be competitively tendered once but operate as a monopoly for decades. Therefore tariff regulation, service obligations, information rights, termination provisions and public ownership of essential assets matter after the bid. Renegotiation is sometimes justified by unforeseeable shocks; routine renegotiation to rescue a badly priced bid undermines fair competition. A robust concession allocates risks to the party best able to manage them, while keeping political and force-majeure risks explicit.

### Evidence-based reform

A district can use administrative data to detect delays, but must validate it with physical inspections and independent citizen surveys. Randomised evaluation may help compare communication or delivery options where ethical and feasible; it cannot by itself settle the values of equality, dignity or democratic control. Qualitative evidence can reveal why a scheme fails among migrant families even when average results improve. A credible reform programme publishes baseline, target, cost, implementation responsibility and periodic learning. It also protects whistle-blowers and prevents retaliation against complainants.

### Application to Telangana

In a rapidly expanding municipality, a private developer may seek a road connection while nearby low-income settlements need drainage. A regulator or planning authority must weigh network effects, flood risks, land rights and who bears future maintenance. The decision should not be reduced to the developer's investment amount. Transparent land-use maps, hearings, storm-water modelling, procurement disclosure and independent audit can reduce both state and market failure. The ultimate administrative test is whether people can contest decisions without needing personal political access.

## 7. Civil society: public sphere, advocacy and democratic accountability

### Definition and constitutional setting

Civil society refers to voluntary collective activity between the household, the state and the market: associations, unions, professional bodies, neighbourhood groups, issue-based movements, voluntary service organisations and networks. It is not identical with an NGO registered as a legal entity. A neighbourhood group may be informal; a registered society may operate mainly as a contractor; a labour union may advocate a contested interest. Article 19(1)(c) protects the right to form associations or unions, subject to constitutionally permitted restrictions. Civil society also draws on freedom of speech, assembly, equality and access to information.

Three functions are analytically distinct. **Service provision** fills delivery gaps through education, health or relief; **advocacy** changes law, budgets or social norms; **watchdog activity** exposes corruption, environmental harm and discrimination. The same organisation may perform more than one function, but conflicts can arise. An NGO dependent on a district administration for grants may hesitate to criticise its performance. A donor-funded advocacy campaign may have technical expertise yet limited democratic mandate. Transparency about funding, governance and beneficiaries helps citizens assess credibility.

### Social capital and its darker side

Associations can build trust, mutual help and collective bargaining. Women's savings groups can reduce dependence on moneylenders; disability groups can articulate accessibility needs; farmers' organisations can coordinate irrigation. But social capital may be **bonding** within an exclusive caste or neighbourhood rather than **bridging** across communities. A powerful local association may capture municipal amenities and resist facilities needed by poorer residents. Civil society is therefore neither inherently progressive nor a substitute for elected institutions. Representation must be examined: who belongs, who speaks, who sets the agenda and who can exit safely?

### State–civil society relations

Partnership is productive when government retains statutory duties, organisations contribute local knowledge and contracts specify outcomes and safeguards. Confrontation may be legitimate when citizens challenge arbitrary decisions. Governments may reasonably require financial disclosure and lawful operation, but disproportionate restrictions can weaken public scrutiny. The Foreign Contribution (Regulation) Act, 2010 regulates receipt and use of foreign contributions by specified persons and organisations; it is not a blanket ban on all voluntary activity. Domestic fundraising, registration, tax status and eligibility for foreign contribution are separate legal questions.

### Telangana applications

A tribal community organisation might document a drinking-water source threatened by a project, while an environmental group provides technical sampling and the Gram Sabha voices local priorities. A credible process discloses data, records dissent, protects participation by women and marginalised hamlets, and establishes an appeal route. In Hyderabad, resident associations can monitor lake restoration, but their preferences must be balanced against street vendors and informal workers. The examiner expects this dual view: **civil society strengthens democracy when it widens voice and accountability; it weakens it when it monopolises voice or operates without scrutiny**.

## 8. Community-based organisations: representation, participation and social audit

### CBOs are rooted in a community, but not always representative

A community-based organisation (CBO) is typically constituted by members of a defined locality or shared social group to address collective needs. Examples include village water and sanitation committees, user groups, farmers' producer collectives, forest-resource groups and neighbourhood federations. The distinction from a professional NGO is not absolute, but CBOs generally claim accountability to members, whereas NGOs may be accountable to boards, donors and beneficiaries through different channels. The central design question is whether the people bearing the costs of a decision can meaningfully influence it.

Participation has levels. **Information** tells people what is planned; **consultation** asks for views without transferring authority; **joint decision-making** gives citizens a formal role; **community control** delegates implementation or resource management. Calling a public meeting “participatory” is misleading if budgets, eligibility and contractors have already been chosen. Effective participation requires advance notice in accessible language, women-friendly timing, separate spaces where necessary, records of objections, public responses and follow-up on commitments.

### Constitutional and legal channels

The Gram Sabha under Article 243A is an institutional basis for village participation, with powers determined by state law. The 73rd and 74th Amendments created a framework for elected local government, but actual financial and functional devolution remains uneven. In Scheduled Areas, the Panchayats (Extension to Scheduled Areas) Act, 1996 (PESA) establishes special principles for Gram Sabha powers, subject to the relevant legal applicability and implementation context; do not assume every Telangana village is a PESA Scheduled Area. The Right to Information Act, 2005 enables requests for records, while programme-specific social-audit rules can provide structured public verification. An audit is not merely a survey: it compares official records with lived experience and requires an accountable response.

### Example: water committee design

Suppose a village committee monitors a new drinking-water system. It should publicly track source yield, water-quality tests, electricity bills, downtime, repairs and tariff collections. Representation should include women, Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes where present, hamlets distant from the main settlement and persons with disabilities. A committee dominated by the largest habitation might choose a pipeline route that bypasses a smaller hamlet. Publishing maps and complaints, and allowing independent sampling, turns a nominal committee into a useful accountability mechanism.

### Participation failures and remedies

Elite capture is the central risk: local influence can reproduce caste, gender and property hierarchies. Tokenism occurs when signatures are collected after decisions. Meeting fatigue develops when repeated consultations do not change outcomes. Technical dependence arises when citizens cannot understand budgets or engineering designs. Remedies include quorum and inclusion rules, simplified public budgets, trained facilitators, rotation of representatives, third-party verification and time-bound written replies. Community participation should complement professional engineering and elected accountability, not absolve officials of responsibility for safety.

### Practical consequence

A successful CBO can detect failures faster than a distant department and build local ownership of maintenance. But government must still guarantee equitable service, investigate complaints and fund obligations beyond the community's capacity. In a mains answer, distinguish **voice**, **authority**, **resources** and **accountability**. An organisation with a meeting but no budget or right to inspect contracts is not empowered in the same sense as one that can approve plans and demand corrective action.

## 9. NGOs, charitable institutions and their legal-accountability framework

### Institutional forms

An NGO is a functional description, not one universal Indian legal form. Voluntary organisations may register as societies under applicable societies law, as public charitable trusts under relevant trust law, or as Section 8 companies under the Companies Act, 2013. Registration confers a particular governance and reporting framework; it does not by itself certify service quality, tax exemption, permission to receive foreign contributions or eligibility to implement CSR projects. An association can be lawful and useful while lacking a specific funding permission. The correct exam distinction is between **legal personality, charitable purpose, tax treatment, grant eligibility and foreign-funding compliance**.

A Section 8 company must pursue permitted not-for-profit objects and apply its profits to its objects, subject to the Act's requirements. Societies commonly use membership and governing bodies; trusts are administered by trustees under the governing deed and applicable law. Income-tax registrations under sections such as 12AB and donor deduction under section 80G serve distinct tax purposes and have their own conditions. The FCRA, 2010 and its rules regulate foreign contribution; the Ministry of Home Affairs' 2025 notice emphasised that receipt or use without valid registration or other lawful permission can violate the Act [S13]. A domestic charity should not be described as automatically FCRA-registered.

### Accountability problems

NGOs may innovate where bureaucracies are slow, reach hard-to-serve populations and build trust. Yet dependence on donors can shift priorities toward easily measured projects rather than community-defined needs. Small organisations may lack internal controls, and larger ones may develop managerial distance from beneficiaries. The answer is proportionate accountability: audited accounts, conflict-of-interest declarations, safeguarding of children and vulnerable adults, transparent grievance processes, accessible programme results and independent evaluations. Compliance should be sufficient to prevent misuse without imposing costs that only large organisations can bear.

### Service contractor versus rights advocate

An NGO implementing a government-funded training programme is accountable for contract deliverables, while an advocacy organisation may legitimately criticise that programme. The state should avoid selecting only organisations that endorse its policies. At the same time, an NGO claiming to represent an entire district should disclose how it gathered consent or evidence. It may speak *for* a cause without holding an electoral mandate *from* every affected person. Responsible public administration invites diverse voices, including dissenting associations, and documents reasons for accepting or rejecting proposals.

### Telangana scenario

Suppose a Hyderabad-based charity proposes digital-skills training for women in a remote district. A sound partnership tests language accessibility, travel safety, device availability, childcare needs and whether local employers actually offer jobs. Training completion certificates are outputs; income, safe employment and sustained access are outcomes. If the charity receives CSR funding, eligibility and reporting under CSR rules are additional to its organisational registration. If it receives foreign contribution, FCRA compliance must be separately checked. Multiple funding streams require transparent accounting and avoidance of double-counting beneficiaries.

### Balanced conclusion

NGOs are useful because they can experiment, mobilise trust and scrutinise power. They are not inherently more efficient or more representative than government. Good governance preserves the state's duty to guarantee rights, the organisation's freedom to operate lawfully and the public's ability to see where money went and what changed. In an exam, mention the **risk of both excessive state control and unaccountable private philanthropy**.

## 10. Self-help groups: savings, federations, enterprise and women's agency

### How SHGs work

A self-help group (SHG) is a small, usually affinity-based group whose members save regularly, maintain records, extend internal loans and develop collective capacity to access formal finance and livelihood opportunities. The financial mechanism matters: repeated meetings and transparent accounts can build credit history and peer monitoring, reducing the information and transaction costs that deter banks from serving low-income borrowers. The group is not a bank; loans may come from members' pooled savings, bank linkage or specialised institutions. Group liability and social pressure can support repayment but may also impose stress on vulnerable members.

India's SHG–Bank Linkage Programme has been promoted by NABARD, while DAY-NRLM supports rural women's institutions and livelihoods through federated community structures. SHGs may federate into village organisations, mandal or block-level bodies and higher-tier associations to negotiate services, training and markets. Telangana's **SERP** works with rural women's groups; **MEPMA** addresses urban poverty and women's groups. The two should not be confused. A state district page reports a rural federation architecture of SHGs, village organisations and mandal-level associations, but such administrative figures require a reference date and should not be treated as independently audited impacts [S07].

### Beyond microcredit

Credit alone does not guarantee empowerment. If a loan finances household consumption without stable income, repayment can be burdensome. Enterprise development needs market research, quality certification, working capital, bookkeeping, cold chain or logistics, digital payments, safe transport and bargaining power. SHG procurement opportunities must be commercially viable: buying a bus is not the same as earning a sustainable return after lease terms, maintenance, insurance and loan servicing. Social empowerment is a separate outcome: whether women control income, move safely, participate in Gram Sabhas and can challenge domestic violence or discriminatory practices.

### Telangana's 2025 policy shift

The Telangana government's **Indira Mahila Shakti Policy, announced 8 March 2025**, explicitly sought to move SHGs beyond thrift and bank linkage toward microenterprises and wealth creation. Official state pages describe more than 63 lakh women in rural and urban SHG networks; this is a policy-document administrative figure, not an independently verified count of active enterprises [S05–S06]. In June 2026, the Telangana government reported flagging off **553 SHG-owned buses** for operation by the state road transport corporation and announced plans for more. These are dated government claims about a programme and its intended scale; they do not establish profitability for each SHG [S08].

### Governance risks and remedies

Group records may be controlled by a small leadership circle; caste or religion may affect membership; poorer women may be excluded because they cannot save regularly; bank officers may prioritise repayment over livelihood viability. Transparent elections, member-accessible passbooks, grievance procedures, social inclusion, training and independent financial literacy can reduce these risks. Evaluation should report not only total loans sanctioned but **median net enterprise earnings, loan distress, decision-making autonomy and inclusion of the poorest women**.

### Application to a mains answer

An SHG's comparative advantage is social organisation and local information; its limitation is capital, market access and uneven power relations. A strong policy links the group to infrastructure, reliable procurement, childcare and social rights without converting it into an unpaid arm of the state. The aim is not merely more groups on a dashboard, but members who can exercise economic and civic agency.

## 11. Charities, stakeholders and collective-action governance

### Charities and philanthropy are not identical to public policy

Charitable giving can mobilise flexible resources for relief, experimentation, scholarships and community institutions. Its strength is speed and willingness to finance problems that formal budgets neglect. Its weakness is donor discretion: philanthropists are not elected to determine a district's priorities, and projects may depend on personalities or short funding cycles. Corporate philanthropy, individual giving, religious charity and community mutual aid differ in funding sources, accountability and permissible purposes. A charity may run a hospital or school, but it does not acquire the state's constitutional responsibilities simply by delivering a service.

An ethical funding relationship requires transparent objectives, safeguarding, non-discrimination and credible evidence of benefit. If a donor finances a new diagnostic machine but no recurring maintenance or technicians, the gift may become a stranded asset. If donations concentrate on visible urban facilities while remote communities lack basic services, philanthropic preferences can reinforce spatial inequality. Government should coordinate complementary investments without surrendering public priority-setting to donors.

### Stakeholder analysis as an administrative method

A stakeholder is a person or institution that affects or is affected by a policy. The list extends beyond funders and contractors to residents, frontline workers, elected local bodies, women, children, persons with disabilities, informal workers, future users and ecosystems. Stakeholder mapping typically assesses **interest, influence, vulnerability and legitimacy**. A contractor has high implementation influence but does not represent all users. A displaced tenant may have little formal power but a high stake in land acquisition. Treating influence as the only criterion would reproduce existing inequalities.

A practical mapping exercise for an urban lake restoration identifies the municipality, pollution-control authority, drainage engineers, residents, fishing communities, street vendors, developers and downstream villages. Each has different evidence: engineers model inflows; vendors describe livelihood loss; women report safety; residents report flooding; ecologists assess wetland function. Conflict is not evidence that participation has failed. A good process makes trade-offs explicit, gives reasons for choices and compensates unavoidable harm according to law.

### Collective-action problem

Many desirable outcomes, such as clean streets or groundwater recharge, benefit everyone, while the cost of individual contribution is immediate. Free-riding can prevent voluntary provision. Institutions solve this through enforceable rules, credible monitoring, graduated sanctions, trusted leadership and shared norms. Elinor Ostrom's work on common-pool resources challenges the assumption that only complete privatisation or central control can prevent overuse; locally designed institutions may work where boundaries, monitoring and dispute resolution are credible. This insight is relevant to tanks, grazing lands and local water users, but cannot be transplanted mechanically across different caste and land-tenure structures.

### Telangana example

A village tank restoration initiative can combine government engineering, local water-user knowledge, charitable support for awareness and independent quality testing. Yet upstream and downstream users may have competing needs, and landless households may not benefit directly from irrigation. Stakeholder analysis must therefore include drinking-water security, ecological flows and non-landowners, not only registered cultivators. Durable collective action requires clear maintenance responsibilities and a grievance process for those excluded from the dominant association.

### Exam inference

The word “stakeholder” should not become a substitute for democratic legitimacy. Elected government sets lawful priorities, professionals verify safety, affected people participate meaningfully, and donors or firms disclose interests. A credible answer differentiates consultation from consent, influence from rights, and philanthropic output from public value. This prevents the fashionable language of partnership from concealing unequal bargaining power.

## 12. Public–private partnerships: models, risk allocation and public value

### Definition and why governments use PPPs

A public–private partnership (PPP) is a long-term arrangement in which a private entity undertakes significant responsibility for delivering a public infrastructure asset or service, with contractually allocated risks, payments and performance obligations. It is **not** any purchase from a private vendor. Governments use PPPs to access technical capabilities, coordinate design–construction–maintenance incentives, or spread payments over time. But a PPP is not “free infrastructure”: users, taxpayers or both ultimately finance it, and the public authority retains substantial contingent liabilities.

Models include BOT (build–operate–transfer), DBFOT (design–build–finance–operate–transfer), BOOT (build–own–operate–transfer, subject to concession terms), management contracts and availability-payment concessions. Under a toll concession, the operator may bear demand risk; under an availability-payment arrangement, the authority generally retains demand risk and pays for service readiness subject to deductions. The same label can hide different contracts, so exam answers should explain **who owns the asset, who pays, who bears traffic or revenue risk, and what happens on failure**.

### The project lifecycle

First, government demonstrates the public need and compares alternatives, including traditional public procurement. Second, feasibility studies assess demand, land, environmental impacts, financial viability and distributional effects. Third, procurement uses transparent bidding and credible evaluation, avoiding specifications written for a preferred bidder. Fourth, the concession sets construction milestones, quality standards, tariffs or payment formula, reporting, change-in-law treatment, force majeure, termination compensation and handback condition. Fifth, independent contract management checks actual performance. Sixth, the authority plans transition when the concession ends.

The Department of Economic Affairs maintains India's PPP policy resources, appraisal mechanisms and a project pipeline. Its **Viability Gap Funding (VGF)** arrangements can support economically justified projects that are not financially viable solely from user charges; support is subject to applicable scheme rules and appraisal, not an automatic entitlement [S10–S11]. A current government pipeline listing identifies Telangana projects, but a pipeline entry is **not evidence of financial closure or completed service delivery** [S12].

### Risk allocation is the core test

Construction risk is often better managed by a competent builder; political tariff-setting and land-acquisition risk may lie substantially with government; demand risk depends on whether the private party can influence demand. Transferring a risk on paper does not eliminate it: an operator may later seek renegotiation or fail. The authority should quantify expected fiscal exposure, including guarantees and termination payments, and compare it with a publicly financed alternative. “Off-budget” treatment can conceal liabilities and distort choices.

Value for money requires lifecycle costing, not merely the lowest upfront bid. For a municipal treatment plant, a PPP may incentivise reliable maintenance if payments depend on verified water quality and uptime. It can fail if sampling is manipulated, tariffs exclude poor users or the municipality lacks contract engineers. Independent laboratory testing, transparent grievance data and a legally defensible tariff policy are essential. A well-designed public alternative can outperform a badly governed PPP, and vice versa.

### Telangana-specific illustration

Imagine a wastewater-reuse PPP serving an industrial cluster near Hyderabad. Industry can provide predictable demand, making the project financeable; treated water can reduce pressure on freshwater sources. Yet nearby residents must be protected from odour, contamination and diversion of municipal supplies. Contract design should specify influent assumptions, discharge standards, independent testing, electricity-cost sharing, emergency response and affordability safeguards. A local body should publish performance reports rather than rely solely on operator certificates.

### Critical appraisal

PPP proponents emphasise innovation, timely construction and lifecycle discipline. Critics warn about opaque contracts, renegotiation, high financing costs and reduced democratic control. Both concerns are legitimate. The right recommendation is conditional: choose a PPP only after transparent public-sector comparison, competitive procurement, capable regulation, risk analysis and strong grievance systems. The purpose is better **public service**, not simply more private capital.

## 13. Evaluating development partnerships: evidence, inclusion and environmental justice

### Outputs, outcomes and impact

A partnership may report 20,000 people trained, 100 kilometres of road constructed or 50 clinics supported. These are outputs. Outcomes are improved earnings, reduced travel time, reliable treatment or better learning. Impact asks what change can reasonably be attributed to the intervention compared with a credible alternative. A simple before–after comparison may exaggerate effects when economic growth, migration, rainfall or other policies changed simultaneously. Evaluation should state its limitations instead of inventing causal certainty.

An evaluation framework begins with a theory of change. For an SHG enterprise programme, inputs include training, credit and market access; immediate outputs include operational enterprises; intermediate outcomes include sales and margins; longer-term outcomes include women's control over income and resilience to shocks. If sales rise but debt stress and unpaid labour also rise, an “enterprise success” headline may be misleading. Measure distribution: the median woman, the poorest members, those who left the programme and those unable to join.

### Environmental and social safeguards

Development projects can impose uncompensated costs on communities with the least bargaining power. Environmental Impact Assessment processes, pollution-control consent, land and forest rights, public hearings and rehabilitation requirements must be understood in their applicable legal context. A project described as green may still displace informal livelihoods or consume scarce groundwater. Intergenerational equity asks whether today's fiscal and ecological decisions constrain tomorrow's residents. Climate-resilient infrastructure needs flood, heat and drought scenarios rather than assuming historical conditions will remain stable.

Telangana's tank systems, peri-urban lakes and dryland agriculture show why ecological accounting matters. Restoring a tank can improve recharge and reduce flood peaks, but benefits may be uneven between landowners and landless households. A large industrial park may create jobs while altering runoff and groundwater. The administrative question is how to disclose and mitigate trade-offs, compensate lawful losses and monitor cumulative impacts across projects.

### Social audit and transparency

Public disclosure must be usable, not merely voluminous. Contracts, service standards, budgets, environmental monitoring and grievance statistics should be searchable and understandable in local languages. Citizen report cards can identify problems but require representative sampling; complaint volumes may rise when trust in grievance systems improves, so fewer complaints do not always mean better service. Social audits should compare official expenditure and beneficiary records with field evidence and require time-bound action on findings.

Digital platforms can speed payments and publish dashboards, yet create exclusion through connectivity gaps, biometric failure or inaccessible interfaces. Personal data collected for welfare or SHG financing must be limited to legitimate purposes and safeguarded. A QR-based beneficiary database can help administration while increasing surveillance risk if used without consent and lawful controls. Technology is a tool; its developmental value depends on rights and outcomes.

### An integrated scorecard

Assess each intervention against five dimensions: **effectiveness** (did it solve the problem?), **equity** (who benefited or was excluded?), **efficiency** (what did it cost over its lifecycle?), **accountability** (could citizens challenge failure?), and **sustainability** (can benefits persist fiscally and ecologically?). A high score on one cannot excuse serious violations on another. This structure is particularly useful for 10-mark mains answers because it turns a descriptive list of schemes into a reasoned appraisal.

## 14. Corporate Social Responsibility: legal duties, limits and Telangana applications

### From voluntary philanthropy to a statutory governance obligation

Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR) under the Companies Act, 2013 is not merely a firm's charitable preference. **Section 135**, read with **Schedule VII** and the Companies (CSR Policy) Rules, 2014 (as amended), imposes specified governance and spending obligations on eligible companies. According to the Ministry of Corporate Affairs' official CSR FAQ, a company falls within the provision when, in the immediately preceding financial year, it meets **any one** threshold: net worth at least **₹500 crore**, turnover at least **₹1,000 crore**, or net profit at least **₹5 crore**. The thresholds are alternatives, not cumulative requirements [S09].

Covered companies must generally spend at least **2% of average net profits of the preceding three financial years**, calculated under the statutory method; newer companies use the available immediately preceding years as applicable. The figure is not 2% of turnover, nor 2% of total CSR budget, nor a flat levy on all firms. The Board has responsibility for the policy, spending and disclosure. A CSR committee is generally required, with statutory exceptions including when the CSR spending obligation does not exceed ₹50 lakh, in which case the Board discharges its functions. Eligible activities must relate to Schedule VII, interpreted broadly but not without limit.

### Governance, implementation and unspent funds

Companies may implement qualifying CSR projects directly or through eligible implementing agencies under the rules, subject to applicable registration and due diligence. The MCA's National CSR Exchange Portal connects registered companies and implementing agencies; portal listing does **not** independently certify an agency's quality. The Board must satisfy itself about use of funds and make required disclosures. Unspent amounts are subject to distinct statutory treatment depending on whether they relate to an ongoing project; they cannot simply be carried indefinitely as an informal promise. Rule 8 also provides for independent impact assessment by specified larger CSR spenders and projects. A correct answer should distinguish **financial compliance** from **social effectiveness**.

### What is not CSR

Ordinary statutory compliance, routine business expenditure, political-party contributions and activities benefiting only employees are not automatically qualifying CSR. Sponsoring a publicity event is not equivalent to a sustained eligible project. Local-area preference exists in the statute, but the MCA FAQ treats it as directory rather than an absolute rule: companies should balance local needs with broader national priorities. CSR spending is not generally deductible as ordinary business expenditure merely because it satisfies Section 135 [S09].

### Telangana applications and critical viewpoints

Hyderabad's pharmaceutical, technology and manufacturing firms can support public-interest projects such as school science facilities, disability access, community health, skills and environmental restoration, subject to Schedule VII and statutory rules. A credible CSR project identifies a public need through community consultation, coordinates with the responsible department, budgets maintenance, publishes independent evaluation and avoids replacing core public expenditure. A company funding a school lab should verify teacher training and consumables; one funding lake restoration should disclose water-quality trends and not use CSR to offset its own unremedied pollution obligations.

Proponents argue that CSR mobilises private resources and managerial expertise for social objectives. Critics argue that companies may select publicity-friendly causes, influence local priorities or use spending to mask harmful core operations. Both are plausible. CSR should complement—not privatise—the democratic allocation of public goods. The strongest governance model combines legal compliance, transparent project selection, independent impact evidence, community voice and corporate accountability for ordinary environmental and labour duties.

### Exam facts and analytical conclusion

Remember the **₹500 crore / ₹1,000 crore / ₹5 crore** applicability tests; **2% of average net profits**; **Section 135, Schedule VII, CSR Rules 2014**; Board responsibility; eligible implementing agencies; treatment of unspent funds; and independent impact assessment where prescribed. Then ask whether the programme improves capabilities, protects excluded groups and survives after the funding cycle. Counting CSR expenditure alone does not prove development.

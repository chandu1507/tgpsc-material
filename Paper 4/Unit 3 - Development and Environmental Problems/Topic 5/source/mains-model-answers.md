## Six complete mains model answers (original practice)

### Model answer 1. Explain the scientific basis of global warming and why one hot year does not define the Paris temperature threshold.

**Background:** Global warming means a persistent rise in global mean surface temperature, while climate change includes shifts in rainfall, extremes and other climatic characteristics. The distinction is essential for interpreting climate statistics and policy commitments.

**Physical mechanism:** Solar energy enters the Earth system, and outgoing infrared radiation is partly absorbed and re-emitted by greenhouse gases. Human emissions of carbon dioxide, methane and nitrous oxide strengthen this natural effect. Ocean heat uptake delays some surface response, while ice–albedo and water-vapour feedbacks amplify warming.

**Evidence:** The IPCC's 2023 synthesis finds human causation unequivocal and estimates 1.1°C warming for 2011–2020 relative to 1850–1900. WMO's March 2026 assessment placed 2025 near 1.43°C above the same baseline, with 2015–2025 the eleven warmest years recorded. Independent ocean, ice and atmospheric observations corroborate the trend.

**Interpretation:** Annual temperature varies with El Niño, volcanic effects and other factors. A single calendar year above 1.5°C is therefore not equivalent to sustained long-term warming beyond the Paris objective. Different observational datasets can also produce slightly different annual rankings.

**Conclusion:** Sound policy also requires local participation, measurable implementation, and transparent public accountability.  Climate policy should rely on long-term observations, attribution science and transparent baselines, not anecdotes about individual seasons. Rapid emissions cuts and risk-informed adaptation are complementary responses.

### Model answer 2. Assess the climate-change risks for Telangana agriculture and recommend a practical adaptation strategy.

**Context:** Telangana combines rainfed farming, irrigated crops, tank systems and borewell-dependent agriculture. Climate change can intensify heat stress, rainfall variability and the risk of damaging extremes, although local land and water management strongly influence outcomes.

**Risk pathways:** Delayed monsoon onset can disturb sowing, high temperature during flowering can reduce yields, and concentrated rainfall may erode soil or damage harvests. Repeated dry spells increase irrigation demand, while groundwater depletion magnifies vulnerability. Crop productivity and farm income are not identical because prices, debt and market access matter.

**Adaptation package:** District agriculture departments should integrate agrometeorological advisories, resilient seed varieties, crop diversification, soil-moisture conservation and restoration of appropriate tank and watershed systems. Groundwater budgets must accompany irrigation expansion. Farmers shifting away from water-intensive crops need procurement, storage, extension and price-risk protection.

**Inclusion and implementation:** Tenant farmers, smallholders and women cultivators must be eligible for advisories, insurance and credit. Agricultural universities, local institutions and EPTRI can support climate-risk assessment. Projects should compare outcomes against baseline conditions rather than count only subsidy beneficiaries.

**Conclusion:** Sound policy also requires local participation, measurable implementation, and transparent public accountability.  A successful strategy protects net farm income and water security together. Evaluation should track yield stability, groundwater levels, input costs, household debt and access for vulnerable cultivators over several seasons.

### Model answer 3. Compare mitigation and adaptation with examples from Hyderabad and Telangana.

**Definitions:** Mitigation reduces greenhouse-gas emissions or enhances credible removals; adaptation adjusts systems to actual or expected climatic effects. Both are essential because past emissions already create risks while future emissions determine the severity of longer-term warming.

**Mitigation measures:** Hyderabad can expand dependable public transport, efficient buildings, distributed solar energy, waste-methane control and cleaner industrial processes. These reduce emissions when their lifecycle and electricity-system effects are properly counted. Renewable installed capacity alone should not be confused with actual clean electricity generation.

**Adaptation measures:** Heat alerts, shaded public spaces, cool roofs, reliable drinking water, restored urban lakes and maintained drainage can reduce exposure. Rural Telangana needs drought planning, water budgeting, crop advisories and resilient public health services. These measures often deliver immediate welfare benefits without materially changing global emissions.

**Synergies and risks:** Urban trees can moderate heat and store some carbon, but survival and water requirements matter. Air-conditioning can save lives while increasing peak power demand; energy-efficient passive cooling reduces that tension. Unrestricted groundwater pumping during drought may create maladaptation by depleting aquifers.

**Governance:** Municipalities, state departments, EPTRI and community organisations should use measurable indicators: avoided heat illness, flood-loss reduction, reliable power, emissions per service and inclusion of informal workers.

**Conclusion:** Climate resilience requires a balanced portfolio. Neither mitigation without protection of vulnerable people nor adaptation without emissions reduction can deliver sustainable development.

### Model answer 4. Examine India’s 2031–2035 NDC and distinguish commitments from observed achievements.

**Context:** Nationally determined contributions are countries' climate commitments under the Paris Agreement. India submitted its 2031–2035 NDC to the UNFCCC on 24 April 2026, linking climate action to development, equity and international cooperation.

**Quantified commitments:** The document targets a 47% reduction in GDP emissions intensity by 2035 relative to 2005; about 60% cumulative installed electric-power capacity from non-fossil sources by 2035; and a forest-and-tree-cover carbon sink of 3.5–4.0 billion tonnes of CO2 equivalent by 2035 compared with 2005. It also stresses adaptation, Mission LiFE, finance and technology.

**Measurement distinctions:** Emissions intensity is emissions per unit GDP, not absolute emissions. Installed capacity is not electricity actually generated. Carbon-sink targets require credible accounting and permanence. The NDC explicitly avoids imposing individual-sector reduction obligations, including on agriculture.

**Observed position:** The same official document reports that non-fossil sources accounted for 52.57% of installed electric-power capacity on 28 February 2026. This is a dated observed statistic, not proof that the 2035 target has been fulfilled. India’s net-zero goal for 2070 is a separate long-term objective.

**Conclusion:** Sound policy also requires local participation, measurable implementation, and transparent public accountability.  Implementation requires reliable grids, finance, technology, ecological safeguards and a just transition. Examination answers must label baselines, years, targets and outcomes precisely.

### Model answer 5. Discuss sustainable development and the policy tensions between growth, equity and ecology.

**Meaning:** Sustainable development meets present needs without undermining the ability of future generations to meet theirs. It integrates economic opportunity, social justice and ecological integrity rather than treating environmental protection as an optional addition to GDP growth.

**Principles:** Intergenerational equity protects future people; intragenerational equity addresses unequal burdens today. The precautionary principle supports action against serious possible harm despite uncertainty. Polluter-pays encourages responsible parties to bear pollution-control and damage costs. Participation and transparency improve legitimacy.

**Trade-offs:** A dam may support water supply but disrupt downstream ecosystems. Renewable-energy parks reduce fossil dependence but can compete with grazing or agricultural land. An industrial estate may generate jobs while stressing local groundwater. Policy should compare alternatives, compensate affected groups, maintain critical ecological services and measure lifecycle effects.

**Institutions and indicators:** Environmental impact assessment, public consultation, urban planning, water budgeting and independent monitoring complement financial appraisal. The UN 2030 Agenda's 17 goals and 169 targets link climate action with health, energy, cities, water, biodiversity and reduced inequality. Telangana can use disaggregated indicators for flood risk, air quality, farm incomes and water security.

**Conclusion:** Sustainable development is a governance discipline, not a slogan. Projects should be judged by who benefits, who bears costs, whether ecological limits are respected and whether gains endure across generations.

### Model answer 6. Evaluate international climate governance and the importance of climate justice for developing countries.

**Institutional evolution:** The UNFCCC of 1992 established a cooperative framework and the principle of common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities. The Kyoto Protocol assigned quantified commitments to certain developed countries. The Paris Agreement of 2015 uses nationally determined contributions and a transparency framework involving all parties.

**Paris architecture:** Article 2 aims to keep warming well below 2°C and pursue efforts toward 1.5°C, strengthen adaptation and align finance with climate-resilient development. Periodic NDC updates and global stocktakes assess collective ambition. Targets, COP decisions and actual emissions outcomes must be distinguished.

**Justice concerns:** Developed countries have contributed disproportionately to historical cumulative emissions and generally possess greater financial and technological capacity. Developing countries emphasise poverty eradication, affordable energy, adaptation needs and fair access to the remaining carbon budget. Equity cannot remove the physical need to cut emissions, but it influences fair burden-sharing.

**Implementation barriers:** Finance pledges may not translate into accessible grants; debt-heavy loans can burden vulnerable states. Technology transfer requires skills, maintenance and intellectual-property arrangements. Loss and damage also includes non-economic harms that conventional insurance may not capture.

**Conclusion:** Effective governance combines stronger mitigation with predictable adaptation finance, transparent reporting and just transitions. India’s approach links domestic low-carbon development with CBDR-RC, while states such as Telangana translate global objectives into local water, health and agricultural resilience.

*Model-answer length: 215 words.*
